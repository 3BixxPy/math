The Chain Rule

Module 1 Chapter 10 defined composition, (f∘g)(x) = f(g(x)) — one function wrapped around another. This chapter is that exact idea's derivative rule.

Why this matters

Every rule so far handles a specific shape: a bare power (Chapter 4), a sum (Chapter 5), a product (Chapter 6), a quotient (Chapter 7). None of them, alone, differentiate something like (x²+1)⁵ or sin(3x) — these aren't sums, products, or quotients of simple pieces; they're one function composed with another, exactly Module 1 Chapter 10's f(g(x)) shape. Composition is arguably the most common way real functions actually get built, so this rule ends up used more than any other in this module.

Rule — The chain rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(g(x))\right] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \]
(6.9.1)

This is literally composition's derivative rule — differentiate f (the outside function), evaluated at g(x) (leave the inside alone while doing this part), then multiply by g'(x) (the derivative of the inside). The mental model: outside-inside. Identify what the "last operation applied" is — that's the outside function — differentiate it first while treating everything inside it as a single frozen chunk, then multiply by that chunk's own derivative.

Worked example — a power of a polynomial

Differentiate (x²+1)⁵.

Step 1 — identify outside and inside

Outside: "something to the 5th power" — \(f(u)=u^5\). Inside: \(g(x)=x^2+1\).

Step 2 — differentiate the outside (power rule, Ch. 4), leaving the inside untouched

\(f'(u)=5u^4\), so \(f'(g(x)) = 5(x^2+1)^4\).

Step 3 — multiply by the inside's own derivative

\(g'(x)=2x\)

Result

\(5(x^2+1)^4 \cdot 2x = 10x(x^2+1)^4\)

Worked example — a trig function of a linear inside

Differentiate sin(3x).

Step 1 — outside and inside

Outside: \(f(u)=\sin u\), inside: \(g(x)=3x\).

Step 2 — differentiate the outside (Ch. 8), inside frozen

\(f'(u)=\cos u\), so \(f'(g(x)) = \cos(3x)\).

Step 3 — multiply by the inside's derivative

\(g'(x)=3\)

Result

\(\cos(3x)\cdot3 = 3\cos(3x)\)

Common mistake

Differentiating the outside function correctly, then simply stopping — forgetting to multiply by the inner function's own derivative at all. For (x²+1)⁵, this looks like writing 5(x²+1)⁴ and calling it done, silently dropping the required ·2x factor. This is the single most common chain-rule error, and it's easy to miss precisely because 5(x²+1)⁴ looks like a complete, reasonable-looking answer on its own — nothing about it screams "incomplete." The fix is procedural, not conceptual: after differentiating the outside, always ask "what's the derivative of what's inside the parentheses?" and multiply by it, every single time, even when the inside looks simple.

Try it yourself

Differentiate (2x)³ using the chain rule.

Practice

  1. Differentiate (x+5)⁴.
    \(4(x+5)^3\cdot1=4(x+5)^3\)
  2. Differentiate (3x-2)⁶.
    \(6(3x-2)^5\cdot3=18(3x-2)^5\)
  3. Differentiate cos(5x).
    \(-\sin(5x)\cdot5=-5\sin(5x)\)
  4. Differentiate √(x²+9).
    Rewrite as \((x^2+9)^{1/2}\): \(\frac12(x^2+9)^{-1/2}\cdot2x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}\)
  5. Differentiate tan(2x).
    \(\sec^2(2x)\cdot2=2\sec^2(2x)\)
  6. Differentiate (x³-1)² two ways: with the chain rule, and by expanding first — confirm the two match.
    Chain rule: \(2(x^3-1)\cdot3x^2=6x^2(x^3-1)=6x^5-6x^2\). Expanded: \((x^3-1)^2=x^6-2x^3+1\), derivative \(6x^5-6x^2\). Match.
  7. bonus — Differentiate (1-x)³, and note the sign carefully.
    \(3(1-x)^2\cdot(-1)=-3(1-x)^2\) — inner derivative is \(-1\), easy to forget the sign on.
  8. bonus — Differentiate sin(x²), and explain why this is genuinely different from sin²(x) (which is (sin x)²) — different inside/outside structure entirely.
    \(\cos(x^2)\cdot2x = 2x\cos(x^2)\). Different from \(\sin^2x\): here the outside is "square something" and the inside is \(\sin x\) — opposite composition order from \(\sin(x^2)\), where the outside is sine and the inside is \(x^2\). Same two ingredients, reversed roles, different derivatives entirely.
  9. bonus — Differentiate 1/(x²+1)³. (Rewrite as a negative power first.)
    \((x^2+1)^{-3}\): \(-3(x^2+1)^{-4}\cdot2x = \dfrac{-6x}{(x^2+1)^4}\)
  10. bonus — If h(x) = f(g(x)), g(2)=5, g'(2)=3, and f'(5)=7, find h'(2).
    \(h'(2)=f'(g(2))\cdot g'(2) = f'(5)\cdot3 = 7\cdot3=21\)
  11. challenge — Differentiate sin(cos(x)) — a composition of two trig functions, so the chain rule has to be applied with sin as the outside and the entire cos(x) as the inside. Then identify why this problem could not be solved by the product rule, even though two trig functions are both present.
    \(\cos(\cos x)\cdot(-\sin x) = -\sin(x)\cos(\cos x)\). The product rule doesn't apply because \(\sin(\cos x)\) isn't two functions multiplied together — it's one function nested inside another (composition, not multiplication), which is exactly the structural signal that the chain rule, not the product rule, is the right tool.
