Chapter 2 · concept:derivative-definition

The Derivative — A Limit of Secant Slopes

Chapter 1 ended with exactly this expression, unsimplified: the average rate of change of f(x)=x² on [a, a+h] works out to 2a+h. This chapter asks the one question that expression was built for: what happens as h → 0? That's a limit — Module 5, twelve chapters of it, applied to this one specific setup.

Why this matters — the whole point of Module 5

Chapter 1's secant line connects two points on a curve, a width h apart. Slide the second point toward the first — shrink h toward 0 — and the secant line rotates. It doesn't rotate forever; it settles into one specific line, the one that just grazes the curve at a single point instead of cutting through it at two. That settling-into line is the tangent line, and its slope is the derivative. Nothing about this is a new kind of mathematical object — it is precisely a limit, computed on the average-rate-of-change expression from Chapter 1, as the interval width goes to zero. Every ounce of care Module 5 spent on "does a limit exist," "how do you actually evaluate one," "what does 0/0 mean and how do you get past it" was spent so that this definition would not be a cold, unfamiliar symbol today.

Definition — The derivative
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]
(6.2.1)

Compare this directly to Chapter 1's formula (6.1.1) with a=x and b=x+h: the average rate of change over [x, x+h] is exactly [f(x+h)−f(x)]/h. Equation (6.2.1) is that same secant slope, with a Module 5 limit wrapped around it, letting h shrink to 0. f'(x) ("f prime of x") is read as "the derivative of f at x," and it means: the instantaneous rate of change of f at the single point x — the slope of the tangent line there, not an approximation of it. Other common notations for the same object: dy/dx (Leibniz notation, read "dee-y dee-x," which literally comes from an infinitesimal Δy/Δx), y', and d/dx[f(x)]. When f'(x) exists at a point, f is called differentiable there — the subject of Chapter 3.

Worked example — f(x) = x² from the definition, in full

Use (6.2.1) to find f'(x) for f(x) = x².

Step 1 — set up the difference quotient

\(f'(x) = \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}\)

Step 2 — expand (x+h)² fully (Module 0's FOIL)

\((x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\), so the numerator becomes \(x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2\).

Step 3 — the x² terms cancel

\(x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2 = 2xh+h^2\), so \(f'(x) = \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}\).

Step 4 — factor h out of the numerator and cancel with the denominator

\(\dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h} = \dfrac{h(2x+h)}{h} = 2x+h\) for \(h\neq0\) — this is exactly Chapter 1's challenge-problem result, \(2a+h\), with \(a\) renamed \(x\). Direct substitution now works, because the \(0/0\) form is gone (this is Module 5 Chapter 5's factor-and-cancel move, applied here instead of to a plain rational expression).

Step 5 — now take the limit

\(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h) = 2x\) — direct substitution, since \(2x+h\) is just a polynomial in \(h\) (Module 5 Chapter 4).

Result

\(f'(x) = 2x\) for \(f(x)=x^2\). Check it against the diagram above: at \(x_0=1\), the formula gives \(f'(1)=2\) — exactly what the secant's slope converges to as \(h\to0\).

Common mistake

Plugging in h=0 before fully simplifying the fraction. Substitute h=0 straight into [f(x+h)-f(x)]/h as written and you get 0/0 every single time — that's not wrong arithmetic, it's the definition working exactly as expected, because x+h=x the instant h=0. The whole reason (6.2.1) is a limit and not a direct evaluation is that direct substitution always fails here at first. The required move — same lesson as Module 5 Chapter 5 — is to expand and simplify algebraically until the h in the denominator cancels, exactly like Step 4 above, and only take the limit after that cancellation. Stopping at the unsimplified 0/0 form, or worse, declaring the derivative "undefined," throws away a limit that actually exists and is easy to find.

Try it yourself

Using the definition (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = 3x.

Practice

  1. Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = 5x.
    \(\lim_{h\to0}[5(x+h)-5x]/h = \lim_{h\to0} 5h/h = 5\)
  2. Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = x² + 1.
    Numerator: \((x+h)^2+1-(x^2+1)=2xh+h^2\); divide by h, limit gives \(2x\) — the \(+1\) contributed nothing (it cancels immediately).
  3. Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = 7 (a constant function).
    \(\lim_{h\to0}(7-7)/h = \lim_{h\to0} 0/h = 0\) — a constant function's tangent line is always flat.
  4. Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = x² - 3x.
    Numerator: \([(x+h)^2-3(x+h)]-[x^2-3x] = 2xh+h^2-3h = h(2x+h-3)\); divide and take the limit: \(2x-3\).
  5. Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = -4x + 2.
    Numerator: \([-4(x+h)+2]-[-4x+2]=-4h\); divide by h, limit gives \(-4\) — matches the line's own slope, as expected.
  6. For f(x)=x² (this chapter's running example), evaluate f'(3) using the formula found in the worked example, and explain what that number means about the graph at x=3.
    \(f'(x)=2x \Rightarrow f'(3)=6\). It means the tangent line to \(y=x^2\) at the point \((3,9)\) has slope 6 — the instantaneous rate \(f\) is changing at exactly that point.
  7. bonus — Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = x³. (You'll need \((x+h)^3 = x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\).)
    Numerator: \(3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3=h(3x^2+3xh+h^2)\); divide by h, take \(h\to0\): \(3x^2\).
  8. bonus — Explain in one sentence why (6.2.1) always produces a \(0/0\) form if you substitute \(h=0\) before simplifying, for any function f, not just \(x^2\).
    Because at \(h=0\) the numerator becomes \(f(x)-f(x)=0\) and the denominator becomes \(0\) as well, regardless of what \(f\) is — the cancellation step is unavoidable, not a quirk of any one function.
  9. bonus — True or false: \(f'(x)\) is itself a function of x (a new function built from f), not just a single number.
    True — e.g. \(f'(x)=2x\) for \(f(x)=x^2\) gives a different output at every input \(x\); it only becomes a single number once you plug in a specific \(x\), like \(f'(3)=6\).
  10. bonus — Using (6.2.1) with the two-point form f'(a) = lim_{x→a} [f(x)-f(a)]/(x-a) (an equivalent version — let x play the role of x₀+h, approaching a instead of h approaching 0), find f'(2) for f(x)=x² directly, without first finding the general formula.
    \(\lim_{x\to2}\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \lim_{x\to2}\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=\lim_{x\to2}(x+2)=4\) — same technique as Module 5 Chapter 5, and it matches \(f'(x)=2x\) at \(x=2\).
  11. challenge — Using (6.2.1), find f'(x) for f(x) = 1/x. (You'll need to combine the two fractions in the numerator over a common denominator before the h in the overall denominator will cancel.)
    \(\dfrac{1/(x+h)-1/x}{h} = \dfrac{x-(x+h)}{hx(x+h)} = \dfrac{-h}{hx(x+h)}=\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}\) (h cancels). Taking \(h\to0\): \(f'(x)=-1/x^2\).
