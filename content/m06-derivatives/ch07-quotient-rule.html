Chapter 7 · concept:quotient-rule

The Quotient Rule

The product rule's sibling, for division instead of multiplication. It's built from the same idea, but the formula has a specific term order that's notoriously easy to scramble — this chapter is as much about a reliable memory aid as it is about the rule itself.

Why this matters

Rational functions — one polynomial divided by another, like Module 1 Chapter 14's asymptote-bearing functions — show up everywhere, and so do quotients involving trig or exponential pieces once Chapters 8 and 11 arrive. Differentiating a quotient by rewriting it and hoping the product rule alone handles it doesn't generally work; this rule is the direct tool.

Rule — The quotient rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{\left[g(x)\right]^2} \]
(6.7.1)

Requires g(x)≠0. A classic memory aid, if f is called "high" (the numerator, on top) and g is called "low" (the denominator, on the bottom): "low-d-high minus high-d-low, all over low-squared" — (low)(d-high) − (high)(d-low), over (low)². Say it as a chant if it helps; the order genuinely matters, unlike the product rule's two symmetric terms.

Worked example

Differentiate (x²+1)/(x-3).

Step 1 — identify high and low, and their derivatives

High: \(f=x^2+1\), \(f'=2x\). Low: \(g=x-3\), \(g'=1\).

Step 2 — apply (6.7.1): low-d-high minus high-d-low, over low-squared

\(\dfrac{(x-3)(2x) - (x^2+1)(1)}{(x-3)^2}\)

Step 3 — expand the numerator only, and leave the denominator factored

\((x-3)(2x) = 2x^2-6x\); numerator becomes \(2x^2-6x-x^2-1 = x^2-6x-1\).

Result

\(\dfrac{x^2-6x-1}{(x-3)^2}\)

Common mistake

Getting the numerator's term order or sign backwards — writing f·g' − g·f' (high-d-low minus low-d-high) instead of the correct f'·g − f·g' (low-d-high minus high-d-low). Unlike the product rule, where the two terms add and their order doesn't matter, the quotient rule's terms are subtracted, so swapping them flips the entire answer's sign. This is a famously easy rule to misremember under pressure — lean on the "low-d-high minus high-d-low, over low-squared" chant every time rather than trusting memory alone.

Try it yourself

Differentiate x/x² using the quotient rule (it should simplify to match d/dx[1/x] = -1/x²).

Practice

  1. Differentiate x/(x+1).
    \(\dfrac{1(x+1)-x(1)}{(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2}\)
  2. Differentiate (x-1)/(x+2).
    \(\dfrac{1(x+2)-(x-1)(1)}{(x+2)^2}=\dfrac{3}{(x+2)^2}\)
  3. Differentiate (3x²)/(x-1).
    \(\dfrac{6x(x-1)-3x^2(1)}{(x-1)^2}=\dfrac{3x^2-6x}{(x-1)^2}\)
  4. Differentiate 5/x³ using the quotient rule (with f=5, constant), then check against the power rule's shortcut (rewrite as 5x⁻³ first).
    Quotient rule: \(\dfrac{0\cdot x^3 - 5(3x^2)}{x^6}=\dfrac{-15x^2}{x^6}=\dfrac{-15}{x^4}\). Power rule: \(5x^{-3}\to-15x^{-4}=-15/x^4\). Match.
  5. Differentiate (x²-4)/(x+2), first using the quotient rule directly.
    \(\dfrac{2x(x+2)-(x^2-4)(1)}{(x+2)^2}=\dfrac{2x^2+4x-x^2+4}{(x+2)^2}=\dfrac{x^2+4x+4}{(x+2)^2}=\dfrac{(x+2)^2}{(x+2)^2}=1\)
  6. Differentiate (2x+1)/(3x-2).
    \(\dfrac{2(3x-2)-(2x+1)(3)}{(3x-2)^2}=\dfrac{6x-4-6x-3}{(3x-2)^2}=\dfrac{-7}{(3x-2)^2}\)
  7. bonus — Redo problem 5, (x²-4)/(x+2), by first factoring and canceling algebraically (Module 0), then differentiating the simplified form. Why does the answer stay 1 either way?
    \(\dfrac{x^2-4}{x+2}=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x+2}=x-2\) for \(x\neq-2\), whose derivative is simply 1. Both methods agree because the function itself simplifies to a line (with a hole at x=-2) — same reasoning as Module 5's factor-and-cancel limits.
  8. bonus — Differentiate 1/(x²+1).
    \(\dfrac{0(x^2+1)-1(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2}=\dfrac{-2x}{(x^2+1)^2}\)
  9. bonus — If f(1)=4, f'(1)=-2, g(1)=2, g'(1)=1, find (f/g)'(1).
    \(\dfrac{f'(1)g(1)-f(1)g'(1)}{[g(1)]^2}=\dfrac{-2(2)-4(1)}{2^2}=\dfrac{-8}{4}=-2\)
  10. bonus — Differentiate x²/(2x-1), and simplify the numerator as far as it will go.
    \(\dfrac{2x(2x-1)-x^2(2)}{(2x-1)^2}=\dfrac{4x^2-2x-2x^2}{(2x-1)^2}=\dfrac{2x^2-2x}{(2x-1)^2}=\dfrac{2x(x-1)}{(2x-1)^2}\)
  11. challenge — Derive the quotient rule from the product rule: write f/g as f·g⁻¹, apply the product rule together with the power-rule-style fact d/dx[g⁻¹]=−g⁻²·g' (a preview of Chapter 9's chain rule), and show the result simplifies to (6.7.1).
    \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[f\cdot g^{-1}\right] = f'g^{-1} + f(-g^{-2}g') = \dfrac{f'}{g} - \dfrac{fg'}{g^2} = \dfrac{f'g}{g^2}-\dfrac{fg'}{g^2} = \dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\) — exactly (6.7.1). The quotient rule isn't independent of the product rule; it's the product rule applied to \(f\cdot g^{-1}\), with the "derivative of a reciprocal" fact folded in.
