The Product Rule

Chapter 5's sum rule says derivatives distribute over addition cleanly: differentiate each piece, add. Multiplication is not that simple — and knowing exactly how it isn't that simple is this chapter's whole point.

Why this matters

It would be convenient if d/dx[f·g] were just f'·g' — multiply the two derivatives, done, same pattern as the sum rule. It is not. Products of functions show up constantly (a rectangle's area as width times height, revenue as price times quantity sold, and — heavily, starting in Chapter 8 — any expression combining a polynomial with a trig function), so getting this rule exactly right, not just approximately intuitive, matters immediately.

Rule — The product rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \]
(6.6.1)

In words: derivative of the first, times the second, plus the first, times derivative of the second. Both terms are needed — the product rule always produces a sum of two pieces, never a single term.

Worked example — checking the rule against direct expansion

Differentiate (x²+1)(x³-2x) two ways: with the product rule, and by expanding first.

Method 1 — the product rule directly

Let \(f=x^2+1\) (\(f'=2x\)) and \(g=x^3-2x\) (\(g'=3x^2-2\)). By (6.6.1): \(f'g+fg' = 2x(x^3-2x) + (x^2+1)(3x^2-2)\).

Method 1, continued — expand and simplify

\(2x(x^3-2x) = 2x^4-4x^2\). \((x^2+1)(3x^2-2) = 3x^4-2x^2+3x^2-2 = 3x^4+x^2-2\). Sum: \(2x^4-4x^2+3x^4+x^2-2 = 5x^4-3x^2-2\).

Method 2 — expand the original product first (Module 0's FOIL), then differentiate term by term

\((x^2+1)(x^3-2x) = x^5-2x^3+x^3-2x = x^5-x^3-2x\). Differentiate directly (Chapters 4–5): \(5x^4-3x^2-2\).

Result

Both methods give \(5x^4-3x^2-2\) — the product rule isn't a separate fact to trust blindly, it agrees exactly with expand-then-differentiate whenever expanding is actually feasible. Its real value shows up when expanding first isn't practical — e.g. once a trig or exponential factor is involved (Chapters 8 and 11), where there's nothing to FOIL.

Common mistake

Assuming d/dx[fg] = f'g' — multiplying the two derivatives directly, the same pattern the sum rule uses for addition. This is false, and it's the single most common error in all of differential calculus. A quick numeric counterexample: let f(x)=x and g(x)=x, so f(x)g(x)=x², whose derivative is 2x (power rule). But f'(x)g'(x) = (1)(1) = 1 — a constant, not 2x. They don't match, and they never will in general; the correct rule (6.6.1) always has two terms, not one.

Try it yourself

Differentiate x · x² using the product rule (it should match the power rule's x³ → 3x² result).

Practice

  1. Differentiate x²(x+3) using the product rule.
    \(f=x^2,f'=2x;\ g=x+3,g'=1\). \(2x(x+3)+x^2(1) = 2x^2+6x+x^2=3x^2+6x\)
  2. Differentiate (2x-1)(x²+4).
    \(2(x^2+4)+(2x-1)(2x) = 2x^2+8+4x^2-2x = 6x^2-2x+8\)
  3. Differentiate (x³)(x⁴) using the product rule, and check against the power rule applied to x⁷ directly.
    \(3x^2(x^4)+x^3(4x^3)=3x^6+4x^6=7x^6\); matches \(d/dx[x^7]=7x^6\) directly.
  4. Differentiate (√x)(x-1). (Rewrite √x as x^(1/2) first.)
    \(f=x^{1/2},f'=\frac12x^{-1/2};\ g=x-1,g'=1\). \(\frac12x^{-1/2}(x-1)+x^{1/2}(1) = \dfrac{x-1}{2\sqrt{x}}+\sqrt{x}\)
  5. Differentiate (3x+2)(5x-4), once with the product rule and once by expanding first, to confirm they match.
    Product rule: \(3(5x-4)+(3x+2)(5) = 15x-12+15x+10=30x-2\). Expanded: \(15x^2-2x-8\), derivative \(30x-2\). Match.
  6. Differentiate x³(2x²-5).
    \(3x^2(2x^2-5)+x^3(4x) = 6x^4-15x^2+4x^4=10x^4-15x^2\)
  7. bonus — Differentiate (x²+1)(x²-1) two ways: product rule, and by recognizing the difference-of-squares shortcut (Module 0) first.
    Product rule: \(2x(x^2-1)+(x^2+1)(2x) = 2x^3-2x+2x^3+2x=4x^3\). Shortcut: \((x^2+1)(x^2-1)=x^4-1\), derivative \(4x^3\). Match.
  8. bonus — If f(2)=3, f'(2)=5, g(2)=-1, and g'(2)=4, find (fg)'(2) without knowing f or g explicitly.
    \((fg)'(2)=f'(2)g(2)+f(2)g'(2) = 5(-1)+3(4)=-5+12=7\)
  9. bonus — Show, using specific simple functions, that d/dx[fg] ≠ f'·g in general (i.e., you can't drop the second term either).
    f=x,g=x: fg=x², derivative 2x. f'·g alone = 1·x=x ≠ 2x. The second term, f·g'=x·1=x, is exactly the missing piece: x+x=2x.
  10. bonus — Differentiate x²·x³·x by first combining into a single power, then check by grouping as (x²·x³)·x and applying the product rule twice.
    Combined: \(x^6\), derivative \(6x^5\). Grouped: \(d/dx[x^2\cdot x^3]=5x^4\) (as in problem 3), then \((x^2x^3)\cdot x = x^5\cdot x\): \(5x^4(x)+x^5(1)=5x^5+x^5=6x^5\). Match — the product rule extends consistently to more than two factors.
  11. challenge — Derive the product rule's two-term structure informally: starting from the definition \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)}{h}\), the standard trick is to subtract and add \(f(x+h)g(x)\) in the numerator. Show what this produces, and identify which piece becomes each term of (6.6.1) as \(h\to0\).
    Numerator becomes \(f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x+h)g(x) + f(x+h)g(x)-f(x)g(x) = f(x+h)[g(x+h)-g(x)] + g(x)[f(x+h)-f(x)]\). Dividing by h and taking \(h\to0\): the first piece \(\to f(x)\cdot g'(x)\) (since \(f(x+h)\to f(x)\) by continuity, Chapter 3, and the bracket over h is g's own difference quotient); the second \(\to g(x)\cdot f'(x)\). Together: \(f(x)g'(x)+g(x)f'(x)\) — equation (6.6.1).
