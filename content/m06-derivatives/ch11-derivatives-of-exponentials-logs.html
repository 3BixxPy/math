Derivatives of Exponentials & Logarithms

Module 3 Chapter 2 introduced e as "the base that appears whenever something grows continuously" and promised it would matter once calculus arrived. Here's why it was worth its own letter.

Why this matters

Module 3 defined e via a limit — lim (1+1/n)ⁿ — and called it "the base calculus prefers," without fully justifying why. This chapter is that justification: e is, by a wide margin, the only base whose exponential function is its own derivative. No other base has that property. That single fact is the actual reason e gets singled out with its own symbol instead of being just another number like 7 or 10.

Theorem — The two key derivatives
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[e^x\right] = e^x \]
(6.11.1)
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln x\right] = \frac{1}{x} \]
(6.11.2)

(6.11.1) is exactly the promised payoff: differentiating e^x gives back e^x, unchanged. This is why e is the natural base for calculus — Module 3 Chapter 2 defined e as the limit that makes continuous growth work out cleanly, and this is that cleanliness made explicit as a derivative rule. (6.11.2) is ln x's derivative — worth noting how strange it is on its face: an exponential-family function (ln, the inverse of e^x) differentiates down to a plain rational-power function, 1/x = x⁻¹. Chapter 14 will show this isn't a coincidence — it follows directly from the inverse-function derivative formula applied to e^x and ln x as a pair.

Rule — General bases, via the chain rule
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[a^x\right] = a^x\ln a \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\log_a x\right] = \frac{1}{x\ln a} \]
(6.11.3)

Neither of these needs to be memorized as an unrelated new fact. For the first: any base a can be rewritten as a^x = e^{x\ln a} (Module 0's exponent laws), so by the chain rule (Ch. 9) with outside e^u and inside u=x\ln a: d/dx[e^{x\ln a}] = e^{x\ln a}\cdot\ln a = a^x\ln a, using (6.11.1) plus the fact that \ln a is just a constant. For the second: Module 3 Chapter 7's change-of-base formula (equation 3.7.2) already says log_a x = \ln x/\ln a; since 1/\ln a is a constant multiplier, differentiating just needs (6.11.2) and the constant-multiple rule (6.5.2): 1/(x\ln a).

Worked example — chain rule on both families

Differentiate e^(3x) and ln(x²+1).

e^(3x) — outside e^u, inside u=3x

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[e^{3x}\right] = e^{3x}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}[3x] = e^{3x}\cdot3 = 3e^{3x}\)

ln(x²+1) — outside ln(u), inside u=x²+1

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\ln(x^2+1)\right] = \dfrac{1}{x^2+1}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^2+1] = \dfrac{1}{x^2+1}\cdot2x = \dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}\)

Common mistake

Treating d/dx[e^x] like the power rule — writing x·e^(x-1), the way x^n would differentiate. This is a genuine category error, not just a sign slip: the power rule (Chapter 4) applies when the base is the variable and the exponent is fixed (like x²). Here it's the opposite — the base e is a fixed constant and the exponent is the variable. These are two structurally different families of function, and they have completely different derivative rules. e^x is not x raised to any power at all — its derivative is the remarkably simple (6.11.1), not anything built from bringing an exponent down.

Try it yourself

Find d/dx[ln(x)].

Practice

  1. Find d/dx[e^x].
    \(e^x\)
  2. Find d/dx[5e^x].
    \(5e^x\)
  3. Find d/dx[ln(5x)].
    \(\dfrac{1}{5x}\cdot5=\dfrac1x\) — the constant inside ln always cancels this way (Module 3's log-of-a-product would give the same result: ln(5x)=ln5+lnx, whose derivative is just 1/x since ln5 is a constant).
  4. Find d/dx[e^(-2x)].
    \(e^{-2x}\cdot(-2) = -2e^{-2x}\)
  5. Find d/dx[2^x] using (6.11.3).
    \(2^x\ln2\)
  6. Find d/dx[ln(x³)] two ways: directly with the chain rule, and by first using Module 3's power law of logs to rewrite ln(x³) as 3ln(x).
    Direct: \(\frac{1}{x^3}\cdot3x^2=\frac3x\). Rewritten: \(d/dx[3\ln x]=3\cdot\frac1x=\frac3x\). Match.
  7. bonus — Find d/dx[x·e^x] using the product rule.
    \(1\cdot e^x + x\cdot e^x = e^x(1+x)\)
  8. bonus — Find d/dx[e^x/x] using the quotient rule.
    \(\dfrac{e^x\cdot x - e^x\cdot1}{x^2} = \dfrac{e^x(x-1)}{x^2}\)
  9. bonus — Find d/dx[ln(sin x)]. (Chain rule with a trig inside.)
    \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\cos x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x\)
  10. bonus — Find d/dx[10^x], and evaluate it at x=0 to check it equals ln(10) numerically.
    \(10^x\ln10\). At x=0: \(10^0\ln10 = \ln10\approx2.303\).
  11. challenge — Find d/dx[e^(x²)] and d/dx[(e^x)²], and explain why they come out different even though both expressions "look similar" at a glance.
    \(e^{x^2}\): chain rule, outside \(e^u\), inside \(u=x^2\): \(e^{x^2}\cdot2x=2xe^{x^2}\). \((e^x)^2=e^{2x}\) (Module 0's exponent laws): chain rule gives \(e^{2x}\cdot2=2e^{2x}\), which also equals \(2(e^x)^2\). They differ because the composition order is reversed — the first is "e raised to a squared thing," the second is "a squared thing that happens to be e raised to something" — different inside/outside structure, different derivatives, despite superficially similar notation.
