Higher-Order Derivatives

f'(x) is itself a function of x — nothing stops it from being differentiated all over again. This chapter is genuinely that simple: apply everything from Chapters 4–12 a second time, to a new starting function.

Why this matters

A quick, concrete reason this isn't just an abstract exercise in repeating a process: if f(t) is an object's position at time t, then f'(t) is its velocity — and the derivative of that, the second derivative of position, is its acceleration. Differentiating twice isn't a special new operation; it's the same operation, run twice, and it already has real physical meaning the first time it comes up. Module 7 builds an entire unit around exactly this; this chapter just makes sure the notation and mechanics are second nature first.

Definition — Second derivative and beyond
\[ f''(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left[f'(x)\right] \]
(6.13.1)

The second derivative, f''(x) ("f double-prime"), is just the derivative of f'(x) — differentiate once to get f', then differentiate that result again. The pattern continues: f'''(x) (third derivative) is the derivative of f''(x); beyond that, the prime notation gets clumsy, so it switches to f⁽⁴⁾(x), f⁽⁵⁾(x), and so on, with the order written as a parenthesized superscript. In Leibniz notation (Chapter 2), the second derivative is written d²y/dx² — read "dee-squared-y dee-x-squared" — which looks unusual at first but is simply Leibniz notation applied twice in a row.

Worked example

Find f'(x) and f''(x) for f(x) = x⁴ − 3x² + 2x, then evaluate both at x=1.

Step 1 — first derivative (Chapters 4–5, term by term)

\(f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x + 2\)

Step 2 — second derivative: differentiate f'(x) again

\(f''(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}\left[4x^3-6x+2\right] = 12x^2 - 6\)

Step 3 — evaluate f'(1)

\(f'(1) = 4(1)-6(1)+2 = 0\)

Result — evaluate f''(1)

\(f''(1) = 12(1)-6 = 6\). Two separate numbers from two separate functions: \(f'(1)=0\) says the original curve is momentarily flat at \(x=1\); \(f''(1)=6\) is a fact about how \(f'\) itself is changing there — unrelated arithmetic, just built from the same starting function.

Common mistake

Confusing f''(x) (the derivative of the derivative) with [f'(x)]² (the first derivative, squared). These are completely different operations that happen to look similar in casual notation. For f(x)=x³: f'(x)=3x², so f''(x) = d/dx[3x²] = 6x — a fresh differentiation. But [f'(x)]² = (3x^2)^2 = 9x^4 — just algebraically squaring the expression, no differentiation involved at all. 6x and 9x⁴ are not remotely the same function. The double-prime notation means "differentiate again," never "square the result."

Practice

  1. Find f'(x) and f''(x) for f(x) = x⁵.
    \(f'(x)=5x^4\), \(f''(x)=20x^3\)
  2. Find f''(x) for f(x) = x² - 7x + 3.
    \(f'(x)=2x-7\), \(f''(x)=2\)
  3. Find f''(x) for f(x) = sin(x).
    \(f'(x)=\cos x\), \(f''(x)=-\sin x\)
  4. Find f''(x) for f(x) = e^x, and explain why the answer is unsurprising given Chapter 11.
    \(f'(x)=e^x\), \(f''(x)=e^x\) — unsurprising because e^x is its own derivative (equation 6.11.1), so differentiating it again just reproduces e^x once more, and every derivative after that too.
  5. Find f'''(x) (the third derivative) for f(x) = x⁴.
    \(f'(x)=4x^3\), \(f''(x)=12x^2\), \(f'''(x)=24x\)
  6. For f(x) = x³ - 6x, evaluate f'(2) and f''(2).
    \(f'(x)=3x^2-6\), \(f'(2)=6\); \(f''(x)=6x\), \(f''(2)=12\)
  7. bonus — Find f''(x) for f(x) = ln(x).
    \(f'(x)=1/x=x^{-1}\), \(f''(x)=-x^{-2}=-1/x^2\)
  8. bonus — Find the fourth derivative of f(x) = x⁴, and explain in one sentence what happens to every derivative after that.
    \(f'=4x^3, f''=12x^2, f'''=24x, f^{(4)}=24\) (a constant); every derivative beyond the fourth is 0, since the constant rule (6.5.1) kicks in permanently once the x's are gone.
  9. bonus — If f'(3) = 0 and f''(3) = 5, is this enough information alone to know f(3)? Why or why not?
    No — f'(3) and f''(3) describe the function's slope and how that slope is changing at x=3, but say nothing about the function's actual height there; f(3) could be any value consistent with those derivative facts.
  10. bonus — Find f''(x) for f(x) = (x+1)³, using the chain rule for f' and again for f''.
    \(f'(x)=3(x+1)^2\); \(f''(x) = 3\cdot2(x+1)\cdot1 = 6(x+1)\)
  11. challenge — If f(t) represents an object's position at time t and f(t) = -5t² + 20t (a ball thrown upward, in some convenient units), find f'(t) and f''(t), and interpret both physically. At what time is f'(t)=0, and what does that moment represent?
    \(f'(t)=-10t+20\) (velocity), \(f''(t)=-10\) (acceleration — constant, matching the idea of constant gravity). \(f'(t)=0\) when \(t=2\): the instant velocity is exactly zero, i.e. the peak of the ball's flight, the moment it stops rising and is about to start falling.
