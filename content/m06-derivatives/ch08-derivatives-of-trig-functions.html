Chapter 8 · concept:trig-derivatives

Derivatives of Trig Functions

Module 5 Chapter 11 proved two limits — lim sin(h)/h = 1 and lim (1-cos h)/h = 0 — and promised they'd become the literal foundation of differentiating sine and cosine the moment this module began. This is that moment.

Why this matters

Every trig derivative in this chapter traces back to those exact two limits — nothing else is needed. That's worth sitting with for a second: an entire family of six derivative rules, all deriveable from two specific number facts proven seven chapters ago. This is the payoff Module 5 was built for, made completely explicit rather than left as an abstract promise.

Theorem — The two base trig derivatives
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin x\right] = \cos x \]
(6.8.1)
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\cos x\right] = -\sin x \]
(6.8.2)

Note the minus sign on cosine's derivative — it's real, and it's the single most common sign slip in this chapter (see the mistake box below).

Worked derivation — d/dx[sin x], in full

Derive (6.8.1) directly from the limit definition (6.2.1).

Step 1 — set up the difference quotient

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}\)

Step 2 — expand sin(x+h) with the angle-sum identity

One trig fact from outside Module 2 is needed here: the angle-sum identity sin(A+B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B (provable from the unit circle and rotation, but taken as given here). Applying it with \(A=x, B=h\): \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\), so the numerator becomes \(\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x\).

Step 3 — group the two sin x terms together

\(\sin x\cos h - \sin x + \cos x\sin h = \sin x(\cos h - 1) + \cos x\sin h\)

Step 4 — split into two limits (Module 5 Chapter 4's sum law) and factor out what doesn't depend on h

\(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\left[\sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h-1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}\right] = \sin x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h} \;+\; \cos x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}\)

Step 5 — plug in Module 5 Chapter 11's two essential trig limits

\(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\) (equation 5.11.1) directly. And \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h} = -\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-\cos h}{h} = -(0) = 0\) (equation 5.11.2, sign-flipped).

Result

\(\sin x\cdot(0) + \cos x\cdot(1) = \cos x\). That's (6.8.1) — built entirely out of the angle-sum identity above and the two trig limits from Module 5, nothing more.

The same computation for cos x

The derivation of (6.8.2) is structurally identical, using cos(x+h) = cos x cos h − sin x sin h in place of the sine sum formula.

Set up and expand

\(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos(x+h)-\cos x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h - \cos x}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\left[\cos x\cdot\frac{\cos h-1}{h} - \sin x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}\right]\)

Result

Same two limits as before: \(\cos x\cdot(0) - \sin x\cdot(1) = -\sin x\). That's (6.8.2) — the minus sign comes directly out of the angle-sum expansion, not from anywhere arbitrary.

Rule — The other four, via the quotient rule
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] &= \sec^2 x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\cot x] &= -\csc^2 x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\sec x] &= \sec x\tan x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\csc x] &= -\csc x\cot x \end{aligned} \]
(6.8.3)

None of these need to be memorized as separate cold facts — every one of them comes straight out of Chapter 7's quotient rule applied to tan x = sin x/cos x, cot x = cos x/sin x, and the reciprocal definitions of sec and csc, combined with (6.8.1)–(6.8.2). For example: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right] = \dfrac{\cos x\cos x - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x} = \dfrac{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}{\cos^2x} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2x} = \sec^2x\) — the Pythagorean identity (Module 2 Chapter 9) collapsing the numerator to exactly 1 is what makes this come out so clean.

Worked example — product rule + trig derivatives together

Differentiate 3sin(x) − x²cos(x).

Step 1 — the first term is a plain constant multiple (6.5.2)

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[3\sin x] = 3\cos x\)

Step 2 — the second term needs the product rule (Ch. 6): f=x², g=cos x

\(f'=2x\), \(g'=-\sin x\). \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^2\cos x] = 2x\cos x + x^2(-\sin x) = 2x\cos x - x^2\sin x\)

Result — combine via the difference rule (6.5.3), watching the sign on the whole second term

\(3\cos x - \left(2x\cos x - x^2\sin x\right) = 3\cos x - 2x\cos x + x^2\sin x\)

Common mistake

Two sign/identity mix-ups happen constantly here. First: dropping the negative on cosine's derivative — writing d/dx[cos x] = sin x instead of -sin x (equation 6.8.2). Second: swapping which of sec²x and sec(x)tan(x) belongs to which function — tan x's derivative is sec²x (no tan in the answer), while sec x's derivative is sec(x)tan(x) (both functions appear, multiplied). One quick way to keep them straight: every "co-" function (cos, cot, csc) gets a negative derivative; the plain versions (sin, tan, sec) don't.

Find d/dx[cos x].

Practice

  1. Find d/dx[sin x].
    \(\cos x\)
  2. Find d/dx[5cos x].
    \(-5\sin x\)
  3. Find d/dx[tan x].
    \(\sec^2x\)
  4. Find d/dx[sec x].
    \(\sec x\tan x\)
  5. Find d/dx[sin x + cos x].
    \(\cos x - \sin x\)
  6. Differentiate x·sin x using the product rule.
    \(1\cdot\sin x + x\cos x = \sin x + x\cos x\)
  7. bonus — Find d/dx[cot x], deriving it from cos x/sin x via the quotient rule rather than recalling it from memory.
    \(\dfrac{-\sin x\sin x - \cos x\cos x}{\sin^2x} = \dfrac{-(\sin^2x+\cos^2x)}{\sin^2x} = \dfrac{-1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x\)
  8. bonus — Differentiate cos(x)/x using the quotient rule.
    \(\dfrac{-\sin x\cdot x - \cos x\cdot1}{x^2} = \dfrac{-x\sin x-\cos x}{x^2}\)
  9. bonus — Differentiate 4tan x - 2sec x.
    \(4\sec^2x - 2\sec x\tan x\)
  10. bonus — Evaluate d/dx[sin x] at x=0 and at x=π/2. What do these two derivative values say about the steepness of the sine curve at those two points?
    At x=0: \(\cos(0)=1\) — steepest, sine is rising fastest there. At x=π/2: \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\) — momentarily flat, exactly at sine's peak (Module 2's graph makes this visually obvious: the curve crests and flattens right at π/2).
  11. challenge — Differentiate sin²x (that is, (sin x)²) by treating it as sin(x)·sin(x) and applying the product rule — don't use the chain rule yet, that's next chapter.
    Product rule on \(\sin x\cdot\sin x\): \(\cos x\cdot\sin x + \sin x\cdot\cos x = 2\sin x\cos x\) — so \(d/dx[\sin^2x] = 2\sin x\cos x\), a result Chapter 9's chain rule will reproduce in one line instead of two.
