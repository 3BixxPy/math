Chapter 14 · concept:inverse-function-derivative

Derivatives of Inverse Functions

Module 1 Chapter 11 showed that a function and its inverse are mirror images across the line y=x. This chapter asks what that mirroring does to slopes — and the answer sets up Chapter 15's inverse trig derivatives directly.

Why this matters

Reflecting a graph over y=x swaps every point's coordinates: (a,b) becomes (b,a). A tangent line is a local picture of a graph's slope at one point — so reflecting the whole graph reflects its tangent lines too, and a reflected line's slope is the reciprocal of the original (a line of slope m, reflected over y=x, becomes a line of slope 1/m). That geometric fact is the entire content of this chapter's formula.

Theorem — Derivative of an inverse function
\[ \left(f^{-1}\right)'(x) = \frac{1}{f'\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)} \]
(6.14.1)

To find the inverse's slope at a given x, first find the mirrored input f⁻¹(x) — the point on the original function that reflects to this one — then take the reciprocal of f's own slope there. This is precisely Module 1 Chapter 11's reflection-over-y=x idea, made quantitative: mirrored points get reciprocal slopes.

Worked example — verifying the formula numerically

Let f(x) = x³ + 1, with inverse f⁻¹(x) = (x-1)^(1/3). Verify (6.14.1) at x=9, by computing both sides independently.

Step 1 — right side: find f⁻¹(9) first

\(f^{-1}(9) = (9-1)^{1/3} = 8^{1/3} = 2\). (Check: \(f(2)=2^3+1=9\) ✓, confirming 2 really is the right mirrored input.)

Step 2 — right side: compute f'(x) and evaluate at that mirrored input

\(f'(x)=3x^2\) (power/sum rules), so \(f'(f^{-1}(9)) = f'(2) = 3(2)^2 = 12\). By (6.14.1), \((f^{-1})'(9) = 1/12\).

Step 3 — left side: differentiate f⁻¹(x) directly and evaluate at x=9

\(f^{-1}(x)=(x-1)^{1/3}\); by the chain rule (Ch. 9), \((f^{-1})'(x) = \dfrac13(x-1)^{-2/3}\). At \(x=9\): \(\dfrac13(8)^{-2/3} = \dfrac13\cdot\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac1{12}\).

Result

Both routes give \(1/12\) — (6.14.1) checks out. Notice the formula's real value: it found \((f^{-1})'(9) = 1/12\) using only facts about \(f\) itself (its formula and its derivative), without ever needing an explicit formula for \(f^{-1}\) at all. That matters enormously in Chapter 15, where the inverse trig functions don't have a simple closed-form "swap and solve" inverse the way this cubic did.

Common mistake

Confusing "the derivative of the inverse," (f⁻¹)'(x), with "the inverse of the derivative" — these sound similar but are entirely different objects. (f⁻¹)'(x) means: first find the inverse function, then differentiate it (or, equivalently, use (6.14.1) directly). "The inverse of the derivative" would mean something like finding the functional inverse of f' itself — a completely different, usually much messier operation. For f(x)=x^3+1: \(f'(x)=3x^2\) isn't even one-to-one (it fails the horizontal line test — \(f'(1)=f'(-1)=3\)), so it doesn't have a genuine inverse function at all in the usual sense, while \((f^{-1})'(x)\) — the actual object this chapter studies — is perfectly well-defined, as the worked example shows. Reading the words in the right order matters: "derivative of [the inverse]" is not "[the inverse] of the derivative."

Try it yourself

If f(3) = 7 and f'(3) = 4, find (f⁻¹)'(7).

Practice

  1. If f(2) = 5 and f'(2) = 3, find (f⁻¹)'(5).
    \(1/3\)
  2. If f(0) = 1 and f'(0) = -2, find (f⁻¹)'(1).
    \(-1/2\)
  3. For f(x) = 2x + 3 (a line), find f⁻¹(x) directly (Module 1's swap-and-solve method), then find (f⁻¹)'(x) by differentiating it directly.
    \(f^{-1}(x)=(x-3)/2\), whose derivative is \(1/2\) — the constant reciprocal of f's own constant slope, 2.
  4. For the same f(x) = 2x+3, verify problem 3's answer using (6.14.1) instead.
    \(f'(x)=2\) everywhere, so \(f'(f^{-1}(x))=2\) regardless of the input, and \((f^{-1})'(x)=1/2\) — matches.
  5. If f(x) = x⁵, and knowing \(f(1)=1\), find (f⁻¹)'(1) using (6.14.1).
    \(f'(x)=5x^4\), \(f'(1)=5\); \((f^{-1})'(1)=1/5\)
  6. True or false: (f⁻¹)'(x) and 1/f'(x) are always the same expression.
    False — (6.14.1) requires evaluating f' at f⁻¹(x), the mirrored input, not at x itself. They only coincide where \(f^{-1}(x)=x\).
  7. bonus — For f(x) = x³, find (f⁻¹)'(8) using (6.14.1), then double-check by differentiating f⁻¹(x)=x^(1/3) directly.
    \(f^{-1}(8)=2\), \(f'(2)=12\), so \((f^{-1})'(8)=1/12\). Direct: \(f^{-1}(x)=x^{1/3}\), derivative \(\frac13x^{-2/3}\), at x=8: \(\frac13(4)^{-1}=\frac1{12}\) — using \(8^{2/3}=4\). Match.
  8. bonus — Explain why (6.14.1) would fail (produce an undefined result) at any x where f'(f⁻¹(x)) = 0. What does that correspond to on the original graph of f?
    Dividing by zero is undefined — this happens exactly where f itself has a horizontal tangent at the mirrored point. Reflected over y=x, a horizontal tangent becomes a vertical one, and a vertical tangent line has no defined (finite) slope — consistent with Chapter 3's discussion of vertical tangents breaking differentiability.
  9. bonus — If g is the inverse of f, and f(4)=10, f'(4)=6, find g'(10).
    \(g'(10)=1/f'(4)=1/6\) — same formula, different variable name for the inverse.
  10. bonus — For f(x) = e^x (Chapter 11), whose inverse is ln(x), use (6.14.1) at x=e (so f⁻¹(e)=1) to find (f⁻¹)'(e), and compare it to the known formula d/dx[ln x]=1/x evaluated at x=e.
    \(f^{-1}(e)=\ln(e)=1\); \(f'(1)=e^1=e\); \((f^{-1})'(e)=1/e\). Direct formula: \(1/x\) at \(x=e\) is also \(1/e\). Match — this is exactly why d/dx[ln x]=1/x works out the way it does.
  11. challenge — Using (6.14.1) in general symbolic form, with f(x)=e^x and f⁻¹(x)=\ln x, derive the formula d/dx[\ln x] = 1/x from scratch (rather than just checking it at one point like the bonus problem above).
    \((f^{-1})'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(x))}\). Here \(f'(u)=e^u\), so \(f'(f^{-1}(x)) = e^{\ln x} = x\) (the defining cancellation property of inverses, Module 1 Ch. 11, equation 1.11.1). So \((f^{-1})'(x) = 1/x\) — exactly (6.11.2), derived here as a genuine consequence of (6.14.1) rather than asserted on its own.
