Differentiability & Continuity

Module 5 Chapter 8 built continuity out of limits: no jumps, no holes, no blow-ups. This chapter connects that idea to Chapter 2's new one — and shows they're related, but not the same relationship in both directions.

Why this matters

Before grinding through differentiation rules starting next chapter, it's worth knowing exactly when a derivative is guaranteed to exist at all — and, just as importantly, when it isn't. A function can be perfectly continuous (no break in the graph, Module 5 Chapter 8's three-part test fully satisfied) and still have no derivative at a point, if the graph has a sharp corner there. Knowing what that looks like now means it's never a mysterious "why did this rule fail" surprise later.

Theorem — Differentiable implies continuous
\[ f \text{ is differentiable at } x=a \implies f \text{ is continuous at } x=a \]
(6.3.1)

If f'(a) exists (the limit in equation 6.2.1 exists at that point), then f automatically satisfies all three of Module 5 Chapter 8's continuity conditions at a. Informally: a curve can't have a well-defined tangent line at a point where it jumps or has a hole — there'd be nothing single and stable for the secant slopes to converge to.

The converse is false. Continuity does not guarantee differentiability. A function can be perfectly continuous at a point — no jump, no hole, the three-part test fully passed — and still fail to have a derivative there. The next section shows the standard example.

f(x) = |x|. No break in the graph anywhere — continuous everywhere, including at x=0. But that corner at the origin is exactly where the derivative fails to exist.

Worked example — why |x| isn't differentiable at x=0

Show that f(x) = |x| is continuous at x=0 but not differentiable there.

Step 1 — continuity check first (Module 5 Chapter 8, all three conditions)

(1) \(f(0)=|0|=0\), defined. (2) \(\lim_{x\to0}|x|=0\) (both sides approach 0). (3) The limit \(0\) equals \(f(0)=0\). All three hold — \(f\) is continuous at \(x=0\).

Step 2 — now try the derivative at 0, using (6.2.1)

\(f'(0) = \displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{|0+h|-|0|}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{|h|}{h}\). Because of the absolute value, this needs one-sided limits — exactly Module 5 Chapter 2's tool.

Step 3 — right-hand limit (h→0⁺, so h>0)

For \(h>0\), \(|h|=h\), so \(\dfrac{|h|}{h}=\dfrac{h}{h}=1\). So \(\lim_{h\to0^+}\dfrac{|h|}{h}=1\).

Step 4 — left-hand limit (h→0⁻, so h<0)

For \(h<0\), \(|h|=-h\), so \(\dfrac{|h|}{h}=\dfrac{-h}{h}=-1\). So \(\lim_{h\to0^-}\dfrac{|h|}{h}=-1\).

Result

The one-sided limits disagree (\(1\neq-1\)), so by Module 5 Chapter 2's existence rule (equation 5.2.1), \(\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{|h|}{h}\) does not exist — meaning \(f'(0)\) does not exist. \(|x|\) is continuous but not differentiable at \(x=0\). Graphically, this disagreement is exactly the corner: the two one-sided secant slopes are heading toward two genuinely different tangent directions, so there's no single tangent line to settle on.

Other ways differentiability can fail

|x|'s sharp corner is the most common example, but two other shapes also break differentiability at an otherwise-continuous point: a cusp (like \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\) at \(x=0\), where the graph comes to an even sharper point and the one-sided slopes both head toward infinity but with opposite signs), and a vertical tangent (like \(f(x)=x^{1/3}\) at \(x=0\), where the curve is smooth with no corner, but the tangent line itself would have to be perfectly vertical — an undefined, infinite slope). In every case the diagnosis is the same: the difference-quotient limit in (6.2.1) fails to exist, even though the function itself never breaks.

Common mistake

Assuming continuity is enough to guarantee a derivative exists — reasoning "the graph doesn't break here, so it must be differentiable." (6.3.1) only runs in one direction. f(x)=|x| at x=0 is the standing counterexample worth keeping in mind permanently: perfectly continuous, provably not differentiable. Whenever a function's graph might have a corner, a cusp, or a vertical tangent — often near an absolute value, a piecewise breakpoint, or a fractional power — continuity alone doesn't settle whether the derivative exists; it has to be checked directly with (6.2.1), one-sided if needed.

Practice

  1. If f is differentiable at x=2, must it also be continuous at x=2?
    Yes — by (6.3.1), differentiable always implies continuous.
  2. If g is continuous at x=5, must it also be differentiable at x=5?
    No — the converse of (6.3.1) is false; |x| at x=0 is the standing counterexample.
  3. For f(x) = |x - 3|, at which x-value would you expect a differentiability failure, and why?
    \(x=3\) — that's where the absolute value's corner sits, same structure as |x| at 0 but shifted right 3.
  4. Compute limh→0⁺ |h|/h and limh→0⁻ |h|/h separately.
    Right-hand: 1. Left-hand: -1.
  5. If a function is not continuous at x=a, can it be differentiable there?
    No — (6.3.1) says differentiable ⇒ continuous, so by contrapositive, not continuous ⇒ not differentiable.
  6. Name the three shapes a graph can have at a point where it's continuous but not differentiable.
    A corner, a cusp, or a vertical tangent.
  7. bonus — For f(x) = {x² if x<1; 2x-1 if x≥1}, is f continuous at x=1? (Check first — differentiability isn't even worth checking if this fails.)
    Left-hand limit: \(1^2=1\). Right-hand limit: \(2(1)-1=1\). f(1)=1 (second piece). All match — continuous at x=1.
  8. bonus — For the same f, is it differentiable at x=1? (Compute both one-sided difference-quotient limits using each piece's own slope formula.)
    Left piece x² has derivative 2x → at x=1, slope 2. Right piece 2x-1 has derivative 2 → at x=1, slope 2. Both one-sided slopes agree (2=2), so f IS differentiable at x=1 — the pieces meet smoothly, no corner, despite being piecewise.
  9. bonus — Explain briefly why a vertical tangent (like f(x)=x^(1/3) at x=0) breaks differentiability even though the graph has no corner or jump at all.
    The difference-quotient limit still fails to exist as a finite number — it diverges to +∞ or -∞ instead, because the tangent line itself would have to be perfectly vertical (undefined slope). "Smooth-looking" isn't the same test as "the defining limit exists and is finite."
  10. bonus — True or false: every polynomial is differentiable at every real number.
    True — polynomials have no corners, cusps, or vertical tangents anywhere; Chapter 4 will show exactly why via the power rule.
  11. challenge — Construct a function that is continuous at x=0 but fails to be differentiable there for a reason other than a corner or the standard |x| shape (i.e., using the cusp or vertical-tangent idea), and briefly justify why it fits.
    E.g. \(f(x)=x^{2/3}\): continuous at 0 (f(0)=0, and the limit as x→0 is 0), but the difference quotient \([( 0+h)^{2/3}-0]/h = h^{2/3}/h = h^{-1/3}\) blows up to \(+\infty\) as \(h\to0^+\) and to \(-\infty\) as \(h\to0^-\) — a cusp, not a corner, but differentiability fails the same way: the defining limit in (6.2.1) doesn't exist.
