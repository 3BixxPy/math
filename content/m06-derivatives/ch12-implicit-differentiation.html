Chapter 12 · concept:implicit-differentiation

Implicit Differentiation

Every derivative so far started with y already isolated: f(x) = something in terms of x alone. Not every equation cooperates that way — a circle's equation never solves cleanly for a single y. This chapter differentiates anyway.

Why this matters

An equation like x²+y²=25 (a circle) genuinely can't be written as one function y=f(x) — for most x-values there are two y's, not one, so solving for y and differentiating that formula directly isn't an option without first splitting into two separate pieces (top half, bottom half) and picking one. Implicit differentiation sidesteps the split entirely: differentiate the whole equation as it stands, both sides, treating y the whole time as some unspecified function of x — which is exactly what the chain rule (Chapter 9) is built to handle.

Method — differentiating implicitly
  1. Differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to x, term by term.
  2. Every time a term contains y, treat y as an unknown function of x and apply the chain rule to it — this produces a factor of dy/dx that a plain x-term would never get. In particular, a power of y follows this pattern:
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[y^n\right] = n\,y^{n-1}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} \]
(6.12.1)
  1. Once every term is differentiated, every dy/dx is still just an unknown factor sitting in the equation — collect all the terms containing dy/dx on one side, everything else on the other, factor out dy/dx, and solve for it algebraically (Module 0's linear-equation-solving skill, applied to an equation where the "unknown" happens to be dy/dx instead of a plain variable).
Worked example — a circle

Find dy/dx for x² + y² = 25, then find the tangent line's slope at the point (3,4) on the circle.

Step 1 — differentiate both sides with respect to x

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^2] + \dfrac{d}{dx}[y^2] = \dfrac{d}{dx}[25]\)

Step 2 — apply the power rule to x², (6.12.1) to y², and the constant rule to 25

\(2x + 2y\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\)

Step 3 — solve algebraically for dy/dx

\(2y\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -2x \implies \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{-2x}{2y} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\)

Step 4 — evaluate at the specific point (3,4)

\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\bigg|_{(3,4)} = -\dfrac{3}{4}\) — the tangent line to the circle at \((3,4)\) has slope \(-3/4\). Geometrically, this checks out: a circle's tangent line at any point is always perpendicular to the radius drawn to that point, and the radius to \((3,4)\) has slope \(4/3\) — \(-3/4\) is exactly the number that makes those two slopes multiply to \(-1\), the signature of two perpendicular lines.

Common mistake

Forgetting to multiply by dy/dx when differentiating a term containing y — writing d/dx[y²] = 2y and stopping, instead of the correct 2y·(dy/dx) from (6.12.1). This is the exact same chain-rule mistake as Chapter 9's (forgetting the inner-function derivative factor), just easy to miss here because the "inner function" is the mostly-invisible y itself rather than an obvious expression like (x+3). The habit that prevents this: y is never a plain variable in this method — it's always secretly y(x), a function of x, so every single term with a y in it needs a chain-rule pass, without exception, even a lone y by itself (which differentiates to 1·dy/dx, not 1).

Try it yourself

Implicitly differentiate x + y² = 4. Find dy/dx.

Practice

  1. Implicitly differentiate x² + y² = 9. Find dy/dx.
    \(2x+2y(dy/dx)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dx = -x/y\)
  2. Implicitly differentiate x²y = 6. (You'll need the product rule on the left side, since x² and y are multiplied.)
    \(2xy + x^2(dy/dx) = 0 \Rightarrow dy/dx = -2xy/x^2 = -2y/x\)
  3. Implicitly differentiate y³ = x. Find dy/dx.
    \(3y^2(dy/dx)=1 \Rightarrow dy/dx = 1/(3y^2)\)
  4. Implicitly differentiate x² - y² = 1. Find dy/dx.
    \(2x-2y(dy/dx)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dx=x/y\)
  5. For x² + y² = 9 (problem 1), find the slope of the tangent line at the point (0,3).
    \(dy/dx=-x/y=-0/3=0\) — flat tangent at the very top of the circle, as expected.
  6. Implicitly differentiate 3x + 2y = 10, and check the result matches what you'd get by solving for y first and differentiating directly.
    \(3+2(dy/dx)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dx=-3/2\). Direct: \(y=(10-3x)/2=5-\frac32x\), whose derivative is \(-3/2\). Match.
  7. bonus — Implicitly differentiate xy = 12 using the product rule, then check against solving for y=12/x and differentiating directly.
    Implicit: \(1\cdot y+x(dy/dx)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dx=-y/x\). Direct: \(y=12/x=12x^{-1}\), \(dy/dx=-12x^{-2}=-12/x^2\). Since \(y=12/x\), \(-y/x=-12/x^2\) too — match.
  8. bonus — Implicitly differentiate x² + xy + y² = 7. (The middle term needs the product rule.)
    \(2x + [1\cdot y+x(dy/dx)] + 2y(dy/dx) = 0 \Rightarrow 2x+y+(x+2y)(dy/dx)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dx = -\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\)
  9. bonus — Implicitly differentiate sin(y) = x. (dy/dx will involve cos(y).)
    \(\cos(y)(dy/dx)=1 \Rightarrow dy/dx = 1/\cos(y)\)
  10. bonus — For the circle x² + y² = 25 (the worked example), find the one point where the tangent line is perfectly vertical (dy/dx undefined), and explain what that means about y there.
    \(dy/dx=-x/y\) is undefined exactly when \(y=0\), i.e. at \((\pm5,0)\) — the circle's leftmost and rightmost points, where the tangent really is vertical, matching the picture directly.
  11. challenge — Implicitly differentiate x³ + y³ = 6xy (a classic "folium" curve), a genuine three-term implicit problem needing the product rule on the right side as well as (6.12.1) on the y³ term.
    \(3x^2 + 3y^2(dy/dx) = 6y + 6x(dy/dx)\) (right side via product rule: \(d/dx[6xy]=6y+6x(dy/dx)\)). Collect dy/dx terms: \(3y^2(dy/dx)-6x(dy/dx) = 6y-3x^2 \Rightarrow (dy/dx)(3y^2-6x)=6y-3x^2 \Rightarrow dy/dx = \dfrac{6y-3x^2}{3y^2-6x} = \dfrac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}\).
