Combining Rules — Product, Quotient & Chain Together

No new rule in this chapter — just Chapters 6, 7, and 9, stacked in the same problem. Real expressions rarely arrive pre-sorted into "this is a product" or "this is a composition"; reading the structure correctly, before differentiating anything, is the actual skill.

Why this matters

Every rule chapter so far isolated one structure at a time on purpose, so each rule could be trusted on its own before being combined with anything else. Real expressions don't come pre-labeled. This chapter is entirely about a habit: before writing a single derivative symbol, ask "what is the outermost structure here — is this fundamentally a product of two things, a quotient of two things, or one function wrapped around another?" — answer that first, apply the matching top-level rule, and only then worry about whatever chain-rule work each piece needs internally.

Method — read the outermost structure first
  1. Look at the whole expression and ask: is the very last operation a multiplication (→ product rule), a division (→ quotient rule), or is one function's entire output feeding into another (→ chain rule)?
  2. Apply that one top-level rule, writing down its skeleton (e.g. product rule's f'g + fg') before computing any individual piece.
  3. Differentiate each piece (f, g, or the outside/inside pair) on its own — this is where the chain rule usually shows up again, one layer in.
  4. Combine and simplify last, not first.
Worked example — product + chain

Differentiate x²·sin(3x).

Step 1 — outermost structure: this is a product

\(f=x^2\), \(g=\sin(3x)\). Skeleton: \(f'g+fg'\).

Step 2 — f' is plain power rule

\(f'=2x\)

Step 3 — g' needs the chain rule (sin outside, 3x inside)

\(g'=\cos(3x)\cdot3=3\cos(3x)\)

Result — assemble the product-rule skeleton

\(2x\sin(3x) + x^2\cdot3\cos(3x) = 2x\sin(3x)+3x^2\cos(3x)\)

Worked example — quotient rule, chain rule where it's actually needed

Differentiate sin(x)/(x²+1).

Step 1 — outermost structure: this is a quotient

High: \(f=\sin x\). Low: \(g=x^2+1\).

Step 2 — differentiate each piece

\(f'=\cos x\) (technically the chain rule applied to \(\sin(x)\) with inner function \(x\) — trivial here, since \(d/dx[x]=1\), but worth noticing: the chain rule is always in play, sometimes with an inner derivative of exactly 1, which is why it can look invisible). \(g'=2x\) (plain power/sum rules, no chain needed — the "inside" of \(x^2+1\) is just \(x\) itself).

Result — low-d-high minus high-d-low, over low-squared (Ch. 7)

\(\dfrac{\cos(x)(x^2+1) - \sin(x)(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2}\)

Common mistake

When three rules could plausibly stack in one problem, two things tend to go wrong: applying rules in the wrong order (starting with an inner chain-rule step before deciding whether the outermost structure is even a product or a quotient), or simply forgetting one factor entirely once the algebra gets busy — most often the inner derivative from a chain-rule step buried inside a product or quotient. The discipline from the Method box above is the fix: name the outermost structure first, write its skeleton down before computing anything, and only then fill in each piece — including checking every single piece for whether it itself needs the chain rule, not just the ones that obviously look complicated.

Try it yourself

Differentiate (x+1)²·x.

Practice

  1. Differentiate x·(x+3)⁴.
    Product: \(1\cdot(x+3)^4 + x\cdot4(x+3)^3 = (x+3)^4+4x(x+3)^3\)
  2. Differentiate cos(x)/x².
    Quotient: \(\dfrac{-\sin(x)\cdot x^2 - \cos(x)\cdot2x}{x^4}\)
  3. Differentiate x²·(2x-1)³.
    Product + chain: \(2x(2x-1)^3 + x^2\cdot3(2x-1)^2\cdot2 = 2x(2x-1)^3+6x^2(2x-1)^2\)
  4. Differentiate sin(x)·cos(x).
    Product, no chain needed (both inner arguments are just x): \(\cos(x)\cos(x)+\sin(x)(-\sin x) = \cos^2x-\sin^2x\)
  5. Differentiate (x²+1)/(x²-1).
    Quotient, no chain needed internally: \(\dfrac{2x(x^2-1)-(x^2+1)(2x)}{(x^2-1)^2} = \dfrac{-4x}{(x^2-1)^2}\)
  6. Differentiate tan(x)·x³.
    Product: \(\sec^2(x)\cdot x^3 + \tan(x)\cdot3x^2\)
  7. bonus — Differentiate √x·(x+1). (Rewrite √x first.)
    \(f=x^{1/2},f'=\frac12x^{-1/2}\); product: \(\frac12x^{-1/2}(x+1)+x^{1/2}(1) = \dfrac{x+1}{2\sqrt{x}}+\sqrt{x}\)
  8. bonus — Differentiate (sin x)/(cos(2x)).
    Quotient, chain needed on the bottom: \(g'=-\sin(2x)\cdot2=-2\sin(2x)\). Result: \(\dfrac{\cos(x)\cos(2x) - \sin(x)(-2\sin(2x))}{\cos^2(2x)} = \dfrac{\cos(x)\cos(2x)+2\sin(x)\sin(2x)}{\cos^2(2x)}\)
  9. bonus — Differentiate (3x+1)²·(x-2)³. (Product of two chain-rule pieces.)
    \(f=(3x+1)^2,f'=2(3x+1)\cdot3=6(3x+1)\); \(g=(x-2)^3,g'=3(x-2)^2\). Product: \(6(3x+1)(x-2)^3 + (3x+1)^2\cdot3(x-2)^2\)
  10. bonus — Before differentiating x/(x+1)³ at all, identify its outermost structure (product? quotient? composition?) and name which sub-piece will additionally need the chain rule.
    Outermost: quotient (x on top, (x+1)³ on bottom). The bottom piece, \((x+1)^3\), needs the chain rule internally when differentiated (outside: cube; inside: x+1).
  11. challenge — Differentiate x²·sin(x)/cos(2x) — a product living inside the numerator of a quotient, with a chain rule buried in the denominator. Identify the outermost structure first, then work outside-in.
    Outermost: quotient, with high \(=x^2\sin x\) (itself a product) and low \(=\cos(2x)\). High': product rule gives \(2x\sin x+x^2\cos x\). Low': chain rule gives \(-2\sin(2x)\). Quotient rule: \(\dfrac{(2x\sin x+x^2\cos x)\cos(2x) - x^2\sin(x)(-2\sin(2x))}{\cos^2(2x)} = \dfrac{(2x\sin x+x^2\cos x)\cos(2x)+2x^2\sin(x)\sin(2x)}{\cos^2(2x)}\).
