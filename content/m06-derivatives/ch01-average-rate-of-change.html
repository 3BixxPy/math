Average Rate of Change & Secant Lines

Module 5 spent twelve chapters building limits without much use for them yet. This module is where that machinery finally earns its keep — and it starts with a quantity you already know how to compute: an ordinary slope.

Why this matters

If a trip covers 150 miles in 3 hours, the average speed is 50 mph — even though the speedometer almost certainly never sat still at exactly 50 the whole way. Average rate of change is that same idea for any function: not "what is f doing at one instant," but "over this whole interval, how much did f change per unit of x, on average." It's a completely ordinary slope calculation. What's new is the question it sets up: what happens to that average as the interval itself shrinks to nothing? That question is the entire subject of this module.

Definition — Average rate of change
\[ \text{avg. rate of change of } f \text{ on } [a,b] = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \]
(6.1.1)

This is nothing more than Module 1 Chapter 5's slope formula, (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁), applied to the two points (a, f(a)) and (b, f(b)) on the graph of f. The line through those two points is called the secant line — "secant" just means a line that cuts through a curve at two points, rather than touching it at one. So (6.1.1) has two equivalent readings: it's the average rate f changed over [a,b], and it's the slope of the secant line joining the endpoints. Same number, two ways of describing it.

Curve: f(x) = x². The green line is the secant through the two marked points. Drag Δx down toward 0 and watch the secant's slope — that's where Chapter 2 is headed.

Worked example

Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² on [1,3], then on [1,1.1].

Step 1 — on [1,3]: evaluate f at both endpoints

\(f(1)=1\), \(f(3)=9\).

Step 2 — apply (6.1.1)

\(\dfrac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1} = \dfrac{9-1}{2} = 4\)

Step 3 — now on [1,1.1]: same formula, a much narrower interval

\(f(1)=1\), \(f(1.1)=1.21\). \(\dfrac{f(1.1)-f(1)}{1.1-1} = \dfrac{1.21-1}{0.1} = \dfrac{0.21}{0.1} = 2.1\)

Result — notice what happened

Widening the interval to \([1,3]\) gave \(4\); narrowing it to \([1,1.1]\) gave \(2.1\) — much closer to \(2\). That's not a coincidence: as the interval keeps shrinking toward width \(0\) around \(x=1\), this average rate keeps sliding toward some specific limiting number. Finding that number exactly, for any \(f\) and any point, is the entire subject of Chapter 2.

Common mistake

Two separate errors show up constantly with this formula. First, a sign or order slip: b and a have to appear in the same order top and bottom — (f(b)-f(a))/(b-a), never (f(b)-f(a))/(a-b). Flipping just the denominator flips the sign of the whole answer. Second, and more conceptually: average rate of change is not the same thing as a function's average value. Average rate of change measures how fast f changed per unit of x over the interval (a slope, in units of "output per input"); average value is a completely different question about the typical height of the graph (a different calculation entirely, using areas — that's a Module 8 topic). Don't reach for one when the question means the other.

Try it yourself

Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² on [1,4].

Practice

  1. Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² on [0,2].
    \((4-0)/(2-0)=2\)
  2. Find the average rate of change of f(x) = 2x + 3 on [1,5].
    \((13-5)/(5-1)=2\) — a line's average rate of change is always its own slope, everywhere.
  3. Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x³ on [0,2].
    \((8-0)/(2-0)=4\)
  4. Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² - 1 on [-1,2].
    \(f(-1)=0,\ f(2)=3\); \((3-0)/(2-(-1))=1\)
  5. Find the average rate of change of f(x) = 1/x on [1,2].
    \(f(1)=1,\ f(2)=1/2\); \((1/2-1)/(2-1)=-1/2\)
  6. A tank's volume is V(t) = t² + 3t liters at time t minutes. Find the average rate of change of V on [2,5], and state its units.
    \(V(2)=10,\ V(5)=40\); \((40-10)/(5-2)=10\) liters per minute.
  7. bonus — Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² on [1,1.01], and compare it to the [1,1.1] result from the worked example.
    \(f(1.01)=1.0201\); \((1.0201-1)/0.01=2.01\) — even closer to 2 than the 2.1 from width 0.1, confirming the interval keeps sliding toward a limit of 2.
  8. bonus — Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² - 4x on [0,4]. Why might this answer feel surprising given the function isn't a straight line?
    \(f(0)=0,\ f(4)=0\); \((0-0)/(4-0)=0\). Surprising only if you picture a straight secant needing a nonzero slope — but the curve dips below the x-axis and comes back up to the same height, so the net change over the whole interval is genuinely zero, even though the function was far from constant in between.
  9. bonus — Find the average rate of change of f(x) = √x on [4,9].
    \(f(4)=2,\ f(9)=3\); \((3-2)/(9-4)=1/5\)
  10. bonus — True or false: the average rate of change of any linear function f(x)=mx+k on any interval [a,b] is always exactly m. Justify briefly.
    True. \(\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=\dfrac{(mb+k)-(ma+k)}{b-a}=\dfrac{m(b-a)}{b-a}=m\) — the \(k\)'s cancel and the \((b-a)\) factors cancel, leaving \(m\) regardless of which interval is chosen. This is exactly why a line's slope is a single fixed number rather than something that depends on where you measure it.
  11. challenge — For f(x) = x², find the average rate of change on [a, a+h] in terms of a and h (don't plug in numbers yet), then simplify as much as possible.
    \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \dfrac{(a+h)^2-a^2}{h} = \dfrac{a^2+2ah+h^2-a^2}{h} = \dfrac{2ah+h^2}{h} = 2a+h\) (for \(h\neq0\)). Keep this exact expression in mind — Chapter 2 asks what happens to it as \(h\to0\).
