Exponent Laws II — Power Rules, Zero & Negative Exponents

On the diagnostic, (a²b³)² came out as a⁵b⁶ from careful-but-miscounted manual expansion. The correct answer is a⁴b⁶. This chapter gives you a rule that makes the miscount impossible.

Why this matters

You had the right instinct — "squaring means writing it out twice and multiplying" — and that instinct is exactly correct. The error was a bookkeeping slip while doing it by hand (one extra a crept into the second copy). The rules below are that same correct instinct, turned into a shortcut that removes the manual counting entirely.

Power of a power

Rule — Power of a power
\[ (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \]
(0.4.1)

Why: \((a^3)^2\) means \(a^3\) multiplied by itself 2 times: \((a\cdot a\cdot a)(a\cdot a\cdot a)\) — that's 6 total factors of \(a\), i.e. \(a^6 = a^{3\times2}\).

Rule — Power of a product / quotient
\[ (ab)^n = a^n b^n \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n} \]
(0.4.2)

The exponent on the outside applies to every factor inside — same idea as the distributive property, but for multiplication instead of addition.

Worked example — the exact diagnostic problem, done two ways

Simplify: (a²b³)²

Step 1 — the shortcut: apply the exponent to each factor

Using (0.4.2), the outer 2 applies to both \(a^2\) and \(b^3\) separately: \((a^2)^2 (b^3)^2\).

Step 2 — apply power-of-a-power to each

\((a^2)^2 = a^{2\times2}=a^4\), and \((b^3)^2 = b^{3\times2}=b^6\).

Step 3 — cross-check by full manual expansion

Write \(a^2b^3\) twice and multiply: \((a\cdot a\cdot b\cdot b\cdot b)(a\cdot a\cdot b\cdot b\cdot b)\). Count carefully: 4 factors of \(a\), 6 factors of \(b\). That's \(a^4b^6\) — matching the shortcut, and correcting the earlier miscount (which had 5 a's because one copy accidentally got an extra factor).

Result
\[ (a^2b^3)^2 = a^4b^6 \]
(0.4.3)
Try it yourself

In the fully simplified form of (a²b³)², what's the exponent on a (the digit that got miscounted on the diagnostic)?

Zero and negative exponents

Definition — Zero exponent (\(a \neq 0\))
\[ a^0 = 1 \]
(0.4.4)

Why: from the quotient rule, \(\dfrac{a^n}{a^n} = a^{n-n} = a^0\). But anything divided by itself is 1. So \(a^0\) must equal 1.

Definition — Negative exponent
\[ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \qquad (a \neq 0) \]
(0.4.5)

Why: \(\dfrac{a^0}{a^n} = a^{0-n} = a^{-n}\) by the quotient rule, but also \(\dfrac{a^0}{a^n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}\). So a negative exponent just means "put it on the bottom of a fraction instead." It does not mean the number is negative.

Worked example

Write 2⁻³ as a fraction, and simplify (x⁻²y³)⁻¹.

Step 1

\(2^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{2^3} = \dfrac{1}{8}\)

Step 2 — apply the outer exponent to each factor

\((x^{-2}y^3)^{-1} = x^{(-2)(-1)}y^{(3)(-1)} = x^{2}y^{-3}\)

Step 3 — rewrite the negative exponent as a fraction

\(x^2 y^{-3} = \dfrac{x^2}{y^3}\)

Common mistake

\(a^{-n}\) is not a negative number — it's a positive fraction (as long as \(a\) is positive). \(2^{-3} = \frac18\), not \(-8\). Also, when applying an exponent to a product like \((2x^3)^2\), the exponent hits every factor including any plain numbers: \((2x^3)^2 = 2^2 x^6 = 4x^6\), not \(2x^6\).

Practice

  1. Simplify: (x³)⁴
    \(x^{12}\)
  2. Simplify: (2y)³
    \(2^3y^3 = 8y^3\)
  3. Simplify: (x/y)²
    \(x^2/y^2\)
  4. Evaluate: 7⁰
    \(1\)
  5. Write as a fraction: 3⁻²
    \(1/9\)
  6. Simplify: (x²y)³
    \(x^6y^3\)
  7. bonus — Simplify: (a⁻²)³
    \(a^{-6} = 1/a^6\)
  8. bonus — Simplify: (2x³)⁻²
    \(2^{-2}x^{-6} = \dfrac{1}{4x^6}\)
  9. bonus — Simplify: (a²b⁻¹)⁻²
    \(a^{-4}b^{2} = \dfrac{b^2}{a^4}\)
  10. bonus — Evaluate as a single fraction: 5⁰ + 2⁻¹
    \(1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\)
  11. challenge — Fully hand-expand (2x²)³ factor by factor (don't use the shortcut), then confirm it matches what the power-of-a-product rule gives.
    \((2x^2)(2x^2)(2x^2) = 2\cdot2\cdot2 \cdot x^2\cdot x^2\cdot x^2 = 8x^6\), matching \(2^3x^6=8x^6\).
