Rational Expressions II — Multiply, Divide, Add, Subtract

Same rules as ordinary numeric fractions — 2/3, 5/7 — just with polynomials in place of numbers. If a rule feels unfamiliar, try it first with plain numbers; it'll match.

Why this matters

This closes out the algebra toolkit before we start solving actual equations with it. Multiplying/dividing rational expressions shows up any time you simplify a complex fraction; adding/subtracting them is exactly the skill behind combining terms in later derivative and limit calculations.

Multiply and divide

Rule — Multiplying and dividing
\[ \frac{A}{B}\cdot\frac{C}{D} = \frac{AC}{BD} \qquad\qquad \frac{A}{B}\div\frac{C}{D} = \frac{A}{B}\cdot\frac{D}{C} = \frac{AD}{BC} \]
(0.11.1)

Multiply straight across. To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply — exactly like numeric fractions. Factor everything before multiplying so you can cancel across the two fractions, not just within each one.

Worked example — multiply

Simplify: (x² − 4)/(x + 3) · (x + 3)/(x − 2)

Step 1 — factor everything first

\(\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x+3}\cdot\dfrac{x+3}{x-2}\)

Step 2 — cancel across both fractions

\((x+3)\) cancels, \((x-2)\) cancels, leaving \(x+2\).

Result

\(x+2\), with \(x\neq-3\) and \(x\neq2\) (both original denominators).

Add and subtract

Rule — Common denominator required
\[ \frac{A}{D} + \frac{B}{D} = \frac{A+B}{D} \]
(0.11.2)

Only valid with a shared denominator. If the denominators differ, find the least common denominator (LCD) first — multiply each fraction by whatever "missing factor" turns its denominator into the LCD (this is multiplying by 1 in disguise, same trick as rationalizing in Chapter 5).

Worked example — unlike denominators

Simplify: 1/x + 1/(x+1)

Step 1 — LCD is the product of both denominators (they share no common factor)

LCD \(= x(x+1)\)

Step 2 — rewrite each fraction over the LCD

\(\dfrac{1}{x}=\dfrac{x+1}{x(x+1)}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{x+1}=\dfrac{x}{x(x+1)}\)

Step 3 — add numerators

\(\dfrac{x+1}{x(x+1)}+\dfrac{x}{x(x+1)} = \dfrac{2x+1}{x(x+1)}\), with \(x\neq0,-1\)

Common mistake

1/a + 1/b ≠ 1/(a+b). Check with plain numbers: \(\frac12+\frac13=\frac56\), but \(\frac{1}{2+3}=\frac15\). Not equal, not even close. You can never add fractions by adding their denominators — always find a common denominator first.

Practice

  1. Multiply, with restriction: (x/3) · (6/x²)
    \(\dfrac{2}{x}\), \(x\neq0\)
  2. Multiply, with restrictions: [(x+2)/(x−1)] · [(x−1)/(x+5)]
    \(\dfrac{x+2}{x+5}\), \(x\neq1,-5\)
  3. Divide, with restrictions: (x² − 9)/(x+2) ÷ (x−3)/(x+2)
    \(x+3\), \(x\neq-2,3\)
  4. Add, with restriction: 2/x + 3/x
    \(\dfrac{5}{x}\), \(x\neq0\)
  5. Add, with restrictions: 1/x + 2/(x+3)
    \(\dfrac{3x+3}{x(x+3)}\), \(x\neq0,-3\)
  6. Subtract, with restriction: 3/(x−1) − 1/(x−1)
    \(\dfrac{2}{x-1}\), \(x\neq1\)
  7. bonus — Multiply, with restrictions: [(x² − 1)/(x+4)] · [(x+4)/(x−1)]
    \(x+1\), \(x\neq-4,1\)
  8. bonus — Divide, with restrictions: (x+5)/(2x) ÷ (x+5)/(4x)
    \(2\), \(x\neq0,-5\)
  9. bonus — Subtract, with restrictions: 1/(x+1) − 1/(x+2)
    \(\dfrac{1}{(x+1)(x+2)}\), \(x\neq-1,-2\)
  10. bonus — Using a different numeric example than the one above, show 1/a + 1/b ≠ 1/(a+b).
    e.g. \(a=1,b=4\): \(\frac11+\frac14=\frac54\), but \(\frac{1}{1+4}=\frac15\).
  11. challenge — Combine into a single fraction, with restrictions: 2/(x−1) + 3/(x+1)
    LCD \((x-1)(x+1)\): \(\dfrac{2(x+1)+3(x-1)}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \dfrac{5x-1}{(x-1)(x+1)}\), \(x\neq1,-1\)
