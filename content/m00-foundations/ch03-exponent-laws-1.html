Exponent Laws I — Product & Quotient Rules

On the diagnostic, 2⁻³ · 2⁵ got "no idea." By the end of this chapter it's a one-line answer, derived from something you already fully understand: repeated multiplication.

Why this matters

Exponent rules aren't a separate thing to memorize — they're direct, mechanical consequences of what an exponent is. If you rebuild them from the definition once, you stop needing to memorize them at all; you can always re-derive them in five seconds. That's the reductionist approach: one small idea (repeated multiplication), reused everywhere.

What an exponent actually is

Definition — Exponent (positive integer case)
\[ a^n = \underbrace{a \times a \times \cdots \times a}_{n \text{ factors}} \]
(0.3.0)

e.g. \(a^3 = a\cdot a\cdot a\). Everything below falls out of this one fact just by counting factors.

Product rule

Rule — Product of powers (same base)
\[ a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \]
(0.3.1)

Why: \(a^3 \cdot a^2 = (a\cdot a\cdot a)(a \cdot a)\) — that's 5 copies of \(a\) multiplied together, i.e. \(a^5 = a^{3+2}\). You're just counting total factors. This still works even when an exponent is negative or zero — you don't need to know what \(a^{-3}\) "looks like" to add exponents correctly.

Quotient rule

Rule — Quotient of powers (same base, \(a \neq 0\))
\[ \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \]
(0.3.2)

Why: \(\dfrac{a^5}{a^2} = \dfrac{a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a}{a\cdot a}\). Two of the top factors cancel with the two on the bottom, leaving \(a^3 = a^{5-2}\).

Worked example — the exact diagnostic problem

Simplify: 2⁻³ · 2⁵

Step 1 — same base, so apply the product rule

Same base (2) being multiplied → add the exponents. You don't need to know what \(2^{-3}\) equals yet.

Step 2 — add exponents

\(-3 + 5 = 2\), so \(2^{-3}\cdot 2^5 = 2^2\)

Result
\[ 2^{-3}\cdot 2^5 = 2^2 = 4 \]
(0.3.3)
Try it yourself

The exact diagnostic problem, one more time, no steps shown: 2⁻³ · 2⁵ = ?

Worked example

Simplify: x⁷ / x³

Step 1 — apply the quotient rule

\(x^{7-3} = x^4\)

Common mistake

These rules only apply when the base is the same. \(x^3 \cdot y^2\) does not simplify to anything with combined exponents — different bases just stay as they are. Also: \(a^m \cdot a^n \ne a^{mn}\) — that's a different rule (power of a power), coming next chapter. Multiplying same-base powers adds exponents; it never multiplies them.

Practice

  1. Simplify: x⁴ · x³
    \(x^7\)
  2. Simplify: y² · y⁵ · y
    \(y^8\) (remember \(y=y^1\))
  3. Simplify: a⁹ / a⁴
    \(a^5\)
  4. Simplify: 5⁷ / 5⁷
    \(5^0 = 1\) — a preview of next chapter's zero-exponent rule
  5. Simplify: 2³ · 2⁻¹
    \(2^{3-1}=2^2=4\)
  6. Simplify: m⁶ / m⁹
    \(m^{6-9}=m^{-3}\) — a preview of next chapter's negative-exponent rule
  7. bonus — Simplify: (x² · x⁵) / x³
    \(x^{2+5-3}=x^4\)
  8. bonus — Simplify: 3⁻² · 3⁵
    \(3^{3}=27\)
  9. bonus — Simplify: b¹⁰ / (b³ · b²)
    \(b^{10-3-2}=b^5\)
  10. bonus — Simplify (general letters as exponents): xᵃ · xᵇ · xᶜ
    \(x^{a+b+c}\)
  11. challenge — Using only the "count the factors" definition, explain in your own words why \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\) for positive whole numbers \(m,n\).
    \(a^m\) is \(m\) copies of \(a\) multiplied, \(a^n\) is \(n\) more copies; multiplying them together lines up all the copies into one product with \(m+n\) total factors of \(a\), which is exactly the definition of \(a^{m+n}\).
