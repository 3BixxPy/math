Factoring I — GCF & Difference of Squares

This is the chapter that fixes the diagnostic problem (x²−9)/(x−3) for good.

Why this matters

Factoring is expanding (Chapter 6) run in reverse. Every skill from that chapter converts directly into a factoring pattern here. This isn't a new topic — it's the same patterns, read right-to-left instead of left-to-right.

Definition — Factoring

Factoring means rewriting a sum as a product. If expanding turns (x−3)(x+3) into x²−9, then factoring turns x²−9 back into (x−3)(x+3).

Greatest common factor (GCF)

Rule — Factor out the GCF first, always
\[ ab + ac = a(b+c) \]
(0.7.1)

This is the distributive property (0.1.1) read backward. Find the largest factor common to every term, and pull it out front. Do this before trying any other factoring pattern — it simplifies everything that follows and sometimes reveals a pattern that wasn't visible before.

Worked example

Factor: 6x³ − 9x²

Step 1 — find the GCF of 6x³ and 9x²

Numbers: GCF(6,9)=3. Variables: smaller power of \(x\) is \(x^2\). So GCF \(= 3x^2\).

Step 2 — divide each term by the GCF, write it outside

\(6x^3 \div 3x^2 = 2x\), and \(9x^2 \div 3x^2 = 3\). So: \(3x^2(2x - 3)\).

Difference of squares

Rule — Difference of squares
\[ a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) \]
(0.7.2)

Exactly equation (0.6.2) from last chapter, reversed. Whenever you see "something squared minus something squared," this pattern applies immediately.

Drag the slider — cut the corner off a big square, and the leftover L-shape always rearranges into a rectangle of exactly the same area. Watch both numbers at the bottom stay equal.

Worked example — the exact diagnostic problem, done correctly

Simplify: (x² − 9) / (x − 3)

Step 1 — recognize the top as a difference of squares

\(x^2 - 9 = x^2 - 3^2\), which matches (0.7.2) with \(a=x, b=3\).

Step 2 — factor it

\(x^2-9 = (x-3)(x+3)\)

Step 3 — cancel the common factor with the denominator

\(\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x+3\), valid whenever \(x\neq3\) (dividing by zero is never allowed — more on this in Chapter 10).

Result
\[ \frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = x+3 \quad (x\neq3) \]
(0.7.3)

The original guesswork approach — splitting the fraction term-by-term — doesn't work for division the way it works for a numerator that's already a single product. Factor first, then cancel.

Common mistake — skipping the GCF check

Given 2x² − 18, it's tempting to try difference-of-squares directly — but \(2x^2\) isn't a perfect square (2 isn't a perfect square coefficient), so the pattern doesn't apply yet. Factor out the GCF first: \(2x^2-18 = 2(x^2-9)\), and now the difference of squares applies inside: \(2(x-3)(x+3)\). Always check GCF before anything else.

Practice

  1. Factor: 8x² + 12x
    \(4x(2x+3)\)
  2. Factor: 5x³ − 15x²
    \(5x^2(x-3)\)
  3. Factor: x² − 16
    \((x-4)(x+4)\)
  4. Factor: 4x² − 25
    \((2x-5)(2x+5)\) — here \(a=2x, b=5\)
  5. Factor fully: 2x² − 18
    \(2(x-3)(x+3)\)
  6. Simplify and state the excluded value: (x² − 25)/(x − 5)
    \(x+5\), \(x\neq5\)
  7. bonus — Factor: 9x² − 1
    \((3x-1)(3x+1)\)
  8. bonus — Factor fully: 3x³ − 27x
    \(3x(x^2-9) = 3x(x-3)(x+3)\)
  9. bonus — Simplify and state the excluded value: (x² − 4)/(x + 2)
    \(x-2\), \(x\neq-2\)
  10. bonus — Factor: 100 − y²
    \((10-y)(10+y)\)
  11. challenge — Factor completely: 2x⁴ − 32 (hint: you'll use difference of squares twice).
    \(2(x^4-16) = 2(x^2-4)(x^2+4) = 2(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)\). Note \(x^2+4\) doesn't factor further over real numbers — it's a sum, not a difference, of squares.
