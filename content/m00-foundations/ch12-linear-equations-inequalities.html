Solving Linear Equations & Inequalities

Time to close the loop on the diagnostic's very first problem — this time expanded correctly from the start.

Why this matters

Your equation-solving mechanics were never the problem — isolating a variable, combining like terms, that was all correct on the diagnostic. What needed fixing was the arithmetic feeding into it (Chapter 1) and the toolkit around it (exponents, factoring). This chapter is where all of it comes together on real equations — and introduces one genuinely new rule for inequalities.

Rule — Solving equations: keep both sides balanced

Whatever operation you apply to one side of an equation, apply the same operation to the other side. Your goal: isolate the variable using inverse operations (undo addition with subtraction, undo multiplication with division, etc.), working from the outside in.

Worked example — the diagnostic equation, solved correctly end-to-end

Solve: 3(x − 4) + 2 = 5x − 6

Step 1 — expand the left side (Chapter 1's fix)

\(3x - 12 + 2 = 3x - 10\)

Step 2 — rewrite the equation

\(3x - 10 = 5x - 6\)

Step 3 — gather the x-terms on one side

Subtract \(3x\) from both sides: \(-10 = 2x - 6\)

Step 4 — isolate x

Add 6 to both sides: \(-4 = 2x\). Divide by 2: \(x=-2\).

Check

Left: \(3(-2-4)+2 = 3(-6)+2=-18+2=-16\). Right: \(5(-2)-6=-10-6=-16\). Equal ✓

Result
\[ 3(x-4)+2 = 5x-6 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = -2 \]
(0.12.1)
Try it yourself

The original diagnostic problem, cold: solve 3(x − 4) + 2 = 5x − 6 for x.

Inequalities — one new rule

Rule — Flip the sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative

Everything about solving an inequality works exactly like an equation, except: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the inequality's direction.

Why: \(2 < 5\) is true. Multiply both sides by \(-1\): naively you'd write \(-2 < -5\), but that's false — \(-2\) is actually bigger than \(-5\) on the number line. The correct statement after multiplying by \(-1\) is \(-2 > -5\). The inequality had to flip to stay true.

Worked example

Solve: −2x + 3 > 7

Step 1 — isolate the x-term

Subtract 3: \(-2x > 4\)

Step 2 — divide by −2, and flip the inequality

\(x < -2\)

Sanity check

Try \(x=-3\) (satisfies \(x<-2\)): \(-2(-3)+3 = 9 > 7\) ✓. Try \(x=0\) (violates it): \(3 > 7\) is false, correctly excluded.

Common mistake

Forgetting to flip the inequality — this is the single new rule in this chapter and the only thing that differs from equation-solving. It only triggers on multiply/divide by a negative, never on addition/subtraction. When in doubt, plug in a test value afterward (as above) to confirm the direction is right.

Practice

  1. Solve: 4x − 7 = 2x + 9
    \(x=8\)
  2. Solve: 5(x + 2) = 3x + 16
    \(x=3\)
  3. Solve: 2(3x − 1) − x = 4x + 5
    \(x=7\)
  4. Solve: 3x − 5 < 10
    \(x<5\)
  5. Solve: −4x + 1 ≥ 9
    \(-4x\ge8 \Rightarrow x\le-2\) (flip on divide by \(-4\))
  6. Solve from scratch (don't peek above): 3(x − 4) + 2 = 5x − 6
    \(x=-2\)
  7. bonus — Solve: −2(x − 3) = 4(x + 1)
    \(x=\tfrac13\)
  8. bonus — Solve: 5 − 2x > −1
    \(-2x>-6 \Rightarrow x<3\)
  9. bonus — Solve: (x + 3)/2 = (2x − 1)/3
    Cross-multiply: \(3(x+3)=2(2x-1) \Rightarrow 3x+9=4x-2 \Rightarrow x=11\)
  10. bonus — Solve: −3(x + 2) ≤ 9
    \(-3x-6\le9 \Rightarrow -3x\le15 \Rightarrow x\ge-5\) (flip)
  11. challenge — Solve 4(x − 2) − 3(2x + 1) = −5, showing every step, then verify by substituting your answer back into the original equation.
