Order of Operations & Arithmetic Care

Your algebra mechanics are already solid. This chapter fixes the one thing that actually cost you points on the diagnostic: careless arithmetic during expansion.

Why this matters

On the diagnostic you solved 3(x − 4) + 2 = 5x − 6 by expanding the left side to 3x − 2. Your equation-solving from there was flawless — but 3(x−4)+2 does not equal 3x − 2. It equals 3x − 10. That single slip changed the final answer. This isn't a "you don't understand algebra" problem — it's a "the order you do operations in is a strict, mechanical rule, and skipping a step under time pressure loses a sign" problem. Every calculus rule you'll learn later assumes this step is automatic and error-free, so we fix it now, permanently.

The rule

Definition — Order of Operations

When an expression mixes several operations, evaluate in this order:

  1. Parentheses / brackets — innermost first
  2. Exponents
  3. Multiplication and Division — left to right, same priority
  4. Addition and Subtraction — left to right, same priority

Multiplication doesn't outrank division, and addition doesn't outrank subtraction — within a tier, you just go left to right.

Rule — Distributive Property
\[ a(b + c) = ab + ac \]
(0.1.1)

Multiplying a sum by a means multiplying every term inside by a — not just the first one. This is where most "I understand algebra but keep getting the wrong answer" errors come from: one term inside the parentheses gets forgotten.

Worked example — the exact diagnostic problem

Worked example

Fully expand and simplify: 3(x − 4) + 2

Step 1 — distribute the 3 into both terms

\( 3(x - 4) = 3 \cdot x - 3 \cdot 4 = 3x - 12 \)

Not 3x − 4. The 3 multiplies the 4 too.

Step 2 — rewrite the full expression

\( 3x - 12 + 2 \)

Step 3 — combine the constants carefully

\( -12 + 2 = -10 \), not \(-2\). Think of it as starting at \(-12\) on a number line and moving 2 steps to the right (more positive): you land on \(-10\).

Result
\[ 3(x-4)+2 = 3x - 10 \]
(0.1.2)

So the diagnostic equation was really 3x − 10 = 5x − 6, which solves to x = −2, not x = 2. Your solving method was correct — only the expansion was off.

Try it yourself

Worked example — order of operations with exponents

Evaluate: 5 + 3(2 + 4)² ÷ 6

Step 1 — parentheses first

\( 2+4=6 \), so we have \( 5 + 3(6)^2 \div 6 \)

Step 2 — exponent

\( 6^2 = 36 \), so \( 5 + 3(36) \div 6 \)

Step 3 — multiplication and division, left to right

\( 3 \times 36 = 108 \), then \( 108 \div 6 = 18 \)

Step 4 — addition

\( 5 + 18 = 23 \)

Common mistake

The failure mode isn't "not knowing the rule" — it's rushing the last combine step. After distributing, you have a simple addition/subtraction of two numbers (like −12 + 2), and under time pressure it's tempting to eyeball it. Don't. Say it out loud or write it as its own mini-step every time, especially when signs are involved. This single habit would have caught the diagnostic error.

Practice

  1. Evaluate: 4 + 2(3 + 1)
    \(4 + 2(4) = 4+8 = 12\)
  2. Evaluate: (5 − 2)² + 3·4
    \(3^2 + 12 = 9+12 = 21\)
  3. Expand and simplify: 2(x + 5) − 3
    \(2x+10-3 = 2x+7\)
  4. Expand and simplify: −3(2x − 4) + 5x
    \(-6x+12+5x = -x+12\)
  5. Evaluate: 10 − 2[4 + (6 − 4)]
    \(10-2[4+2]=10-2(6)=10-12=-2\)
  6. Expand and simplify: 4(x − 2) + 3(x + 1)
    \(4x-8+3x+3 = 7x-5\)
  7. bonus — Evaluate: 3 + 5(2 + 3²) ÷ 5
    \(3+5(2+9)\div5 = 3+55\div5=3+11=14\)
  8. bonus — Expand and simplify: −(2x − 5) + 3(x − 1)
    \(-2x+5+3x-3 = x+2\)
  9. bonus — Evaluate: [(8−3)² − 1] ÷ 4 + 6
    \([25-1]\div4+6 = 24\div4+6=6+6=12\)
  10. bonus — Expand and simplify: 6 − 2(3x − [x + 2])
    \(6-2(2x-2)=6-4x+4=10-4x\)
