Radicals & Rational Exponents

A square root isn't a new operation — it's just an exponent of 1/2. Once you see that, every radical rule is just an exponent rule in disguise.

Why this matters

You'll meet radicals constantly: the quadratic formula, distance between two points, later on the derivative of \(\sqrt{x}\). If you treat radicals as a totally separate topic from exponents, you have two sets of rules to memorize. If you see \(\sqrt{a} = a^{1/2}\), you already know every rule — they're the exponent rules from the last two chapters, unchanged.

The definition

Definition — Roots as fractional exponents
\[ a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a} \qquad a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \]
(0.5.1)

\(\sqrt{a}\) (no small number written) means the square root, i.e. \(n=2\). The numerator of the fractional exponent is the "power," the denominator is the "root."

Rule — Root of a product
\[ \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} \qquad (a,b \ge 0) \]
(0.5.2)

This is just the power-of-a-product rule from Chapter 4, with \(n=1/2\). It's the tool for simplifying radicals: split off any perfect-square factor.

Worked example

Rewrite √(x³) using a rational exponent, and simplify √50.

Step 1a — rewrite the root as a 1/2 power

\(\sqrt{x^3} = (x^3)^{1/2}\) — the square root of the whole \(x^3\) is the same as raising that whole thing to the power \(\tfrac12\) (definition 0.5.1). Not two separate operations stacked up — one power, applied to \(x^3\).

Step 1b — apply the power-of-a-power rule (Chapter 4)

\((x^3)^{1/2}\) is exactly the shape \((x^m)^n\) from equation (0.4.1): multiply the exponents. \(3 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\).

Step 1c — result, and a numeric check

\(\sqrt{x^3} = x^{3/2}\). Check with \(x=4\): \(\sqrt{4^3}=\sqrt{64}=8\), and \(4^{3/2}=(4^{1/2})^3=2^3=8\) — same answer either way.

Step 2 — for √50, find the largest perfect-square factor of 50

\(50 = 25 \times 2\), and 25 is a perfect square.

Step 3 — split using the product rule

\(\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = \sqrt{25}\cdot\sqrt2 = 5\sqrt2\)

Worked example — rationalizing a denominator

Simplify: 1/√2 so there's no radical on the bottom.

Step 1 — multiply top and bottom by √2 (this is multiplying by 1, in disguise)

\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}\). Since \(\sqrt2/\sqrt2=1\), the value doesn't change — only its form.

Step 2 — multiply the fractions: top×top, bottom×bottom

\(\dfrac{1 \times \sqrt2}{\sqrt2 \times \sqrt2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2 \times \sqrt2}\)

Step 3 — resolve √2 × √2

\(\sqrt2\) means "the number that squares to 2" — multiplying it by itself is squaring it, which undoes the root: \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt2=2\). (Same thing via exponents, Chapter 3: \(2^{1/2}\cdot2^{1/2}=2^{1/2+1/2}=2^1=2\).)

Result

\(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\) — radical now on top only, nothing left in the denominator to simplify.

Common mistake

\(\sqrt{a+b} \neq \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\). Radicals distribute over multiplication, never over addition. Check with real numbers: \(\sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\), but \(\sqrt9+\sqrt{16} = 3+4=7\). Not equal. If you ever find yourself "splitting" a square root across a plus sign, stop — that step is illegal.

Practice

  1. Write as a rational exponent: √(x⁵)
    \(x^{5/2}\)
  2. Simplify: √72
    \(\sqrt{36\times2}=6\sqrt2\)
  3. Simplify (\(x\ge0\)): √(x⁴)
    \(x^2\)
  4. Simplify: √8 · √2
    \(\sqrt{16}=4\)
  5. Rationalize: 3/√5
    \(\dfrac{3\sqrt5}{5}\)
  6. Evaluate: 16^(1/2) and 27^(1/3)
    \(4\) and \(3\)
  7. bonus — Simplify: √48
    \(\sqrt{16\times3}=4\sqrt3\)
  8. bonus — Write x^(2/3) as a radical
  9. bonus — Simplify (\(x\ge0\)): (√x)⁴
    \(x^2\)
  10. bonus — Rationalize: 1/(2√3)
    \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{6}\)
  11. challenge — Come up with your own numeric counterexample (different from the one above) proving \(\sqrt{a+b}\neq\sqrt a+\sqrt b\) in general.
    Any \(a,b>0\) where neither is 0 works, e.g. \(a=4,b=4\): \(\sqrt8\approx2.83\), but \(\sqrt4+\sqrt4=4\).

(Item 8's answer: \(\sqrt[3]{x^2}\), i.e. cube root of \(x^2\).)
