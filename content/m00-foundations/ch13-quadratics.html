Solving Quadratics — Factoring, Completing the Square, and Deriving the Formula

On the diagnostic you used the quadratic formula correctly in structure but had to look it up, and slipped on the discriminant (writing 25−26 instead of 25−24). By the end of this chapter you can rebuild the formula from scratch, any time, and the discriminant step becomes routine.

Why this matters

There are three ways to solve ax²+bx+c=0, and they're not really three separate things to memorize — factoring is a shortcut when it's available, completing the square is the general method underneath everything, and the quadratic formula is just completing the square done once, in general, and packaged for reuse. Learn the middle one properly and the other two stop needing separate memorization.

Method 1 — Factoring (fastest, when it works)

Reminder

Chapters 8–9 already cover this: factor, set each factor to zero. E.g. \(x^2-5x+6=0 \Rightarrow (x-2)(x-3)=0 \Rightarrow x=2 \text{ or } 3\). Fast, but only works when the quadratic factors nicely with integers — not every quadratic does.

Method 2 — Completing the square (always works)

Identity — Completing the square
\[ x^2+bx = \left(x+\frac b2\right)^2 - \left(\frac b2\right)^2 \]
(0.13.1)

Take half of \(b\), square it, add and subtract it. This turns \(x^2+bx\) into a perfect square (Chapter 6) minus a constant.

The dashed corner is the piece you add to turn an "almost square" shape into an actual square — that's why it's called completing the square. Drag the slider to see the corner's exact area track "half of b, squared" as b changes.

Worked example

Solve by completing the square: x² + 6x + 1 = 0

Step 1 — move the constant to the other side

\(x^2+6x = -1\)

Step 2 — complete the square (half of 6 is 3, squared is 9)

\((x+3)^2 - 9 = -1\)

Step 3 — isolate the square

\((x+3)^2 = 8\)

Step 4 — take the square root of both sides (don't forget ±)

\(x+3 = \pm\sqrt8 = \pm2\sqrt2\)

Result

\(x = -3\pm2\sqrt2\)

Method 3 — The quadratic formula (completing the square, done once for every case)

Derivation — from ax² + bx + c = 0
Step 1 — divide everything by a (a ≠ 0)

\(x^2 + \dfrac ba x + \dfrac ca = 0\)

Step 2 — move the constant over, complete the square

\(x^2+\dfrac ba x = -\dfrac ca\), then \(\left(x+\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \left(\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = -\dfrac ca\)

Step 3 — isolate the square

\(\left(x+\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2} - \dfrac ca = \dfrac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}\)

Step 4 — square root both sides

\(x+\dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\)

Result — the quadratic formula
\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} \]
(0.13.2)

This works for every quadratic — factoring and completing-the-square are special cases of what this formula does automatically.

Worked example — the exact diagnostic problem, discriminant done carefully

Solve using the formula: x² − 5x + 6 = 0

Step 1 — identify a, b, c

\(a=1,\ b=-5,\ c=6\)

Step 2 — compute the discriminant b² − 4ac carefully, as its own mini-step

\(b^2 = (-5)^2 = 25\). \(4ac = 4(1)(6) = 24\). Discriminant \(= 25 - 24 = 1\). (Compute \(4ac\) fully before subtracting — combining it all in your head in one go is exactly where a slip like 25−26 creeps in.)

Step 3 — plug into the formula

\(x = \dfrac{-(-5)\pm\sqrt1}{2(1)} = \dfrac{5\pm1}{2}\)

Result

\(x = \dfrac{6}{2}=3\) or \(x=\dfrac{4}{2}=2\) — matching both the factoring method (Chapter 8) and your original diagnostic answer.

Common mistake — the exact one from the diagnostic

Computing \(b^2-4ac\) in one mental step invites sign errors. Split it into two separate mini-computations every time: first \(b^2\) alone, then \(4ac\) alone, then subtract. This single habit change is what would have turned 25−26 into the correct 25−24.

Practice

  1. Solve by factoring: x² + 3x − 10 = 0
    \((x+5)(x-2)=0 \Rightarrow x=-5,2\)
  2. Solve by completing the square: x² + 4x − 1 = 0
    \((x+2)^2=5 \Rightarrow x=-2\pm\sqrt5\)
  3. Solve using the formula: 2x² − 3x − 5 = 0
    \(a=2,b=-3,c=-5\): disc\(=9+40=49\), \(x=\dfrac{3\pm7}{4} \Rightarrow x=2.5, -1\)
  4. Compute only the discriminant for 3x² + 2x + 5 = 0 and state how many real solutions it has.
    disc\(=4-60=-56\), negative → no real solutions
  5. Solve by completing the square: x² − 2x − 8 = 0
    \((x-1)^2=9 \Rightarrow x=1\pm3 \Rightarrow x=4,-2\)
  6. Solve using the formula, from scratch: x² − 5x + 6 = 0
    disc\(=25-24=1\), \(x=\dfrac{5\pm1}{2} \Rightarrow x=3,2\)
  7. bonus — Solve by factoring: x² − x − 12 = 0
    \((x-4)(x+3)=0 \Rightarrow x=4,-3\)
  8. bonus — Solve using the formula: x² + 2x − 1 = 0
    disc\(=4+4=8\), \(x=\dfrac{-2\pm2\sqrt2}{2}=-1\pm\sqrt2\)
  9. bonus — Solve by completing the square: x² + 10x + 21 = 0
    \((x+5)^2=4 \Rightarrow x=-5\pm2 \Rightarrow x=-3,-7\)
  10. bonus — Without fully solving, use the discriminant to say how many real solutions 4x² − 4x + 1 = 0 has.
    disc\(=16-16=0\) → exactly one repeated real root
  11. challenge — Without looking above, re-derive the quadratic formula from ax²+bx+c=0 using completing the square, writing every step. Then use it to solve 3x² − 2x − 1 = 0.
    Formula: \(x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\). For \(3x^2-2x-1=0\): \(a=3,b=-2,c=-1\), disc\(=4+12=16\), \(x=\dfrac{2\pm4}{6} \Rightarrow x=1,-\tfrac13\)
