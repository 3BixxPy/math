Chapter 8 · concept:eigenspaces

Eigenspaces

Chapter 7 found individual eigenvectors. This chapter names the whole set they belong to — a genuine subspace (Module 11, Chapter 2), because it's exactly the null space of A − λI (Module 11, Chapter 10) for that particular λ.

Definition — Eigenspace

For an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\), the eigenspace \(E_\lambda\) is:

\[ E_\lambda = \operatorname{Nul}(A-\lambda I) = \{v : (A-\lambda I)v = \vec0\} \]

the set of all eigenvectors for \(\lambda\), plus the zero vector. The zero vector has to be thrown in to make \(E_\lambda\) a genuine subspace — Module 11, Chapter 2 required every subspace to contain \(\vec 0\), and \(\vec0\) always trivially solves \((A-\lambda I)v=\vec0\), even though it's never itself called "an eigenvector." The dimension of \(E_\lambda\) is called the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda\).

Worked example

State the eigenspaces for the running example \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\), using the eigenvectors found in Chapter 7.

Step 1 — λ = 2

\(E_2 = \operatorname{span}\{\langle2,1\rangle\}\) — a line through the origin, dimension 1.

Step 2 — λ = −1

\(E_{-1} = \operatorname{span}\{\langle1,2\rangle\}\) — also a line through the origin, dimension 1.

Result

Both eigenvalues here have geometric multiplicity 1 — each eigenspace is a single line, not a plane. That won't always be the case (see the practice problems below and Chapter 11).

Common mistake

Confusing "the eigenspace" with "an eigenvector." An eigenspace is a subspace — potentially more than one-dimensional for a repeated eigenvalue — while an eigenvector is one specific nonzero vector living inside it. Saying "the eigenspace is \(\langle2,1\rangle\)" is technically wrong phrasing: the eigenspace is the entire span, \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle2,1\rangle\}\) — an infinite set of vectors — and \(\langle2,1\rangle\) is just one representative member of it, no more special than \(\langle4,2\rangle\) or \(\langle-6,-3\rangle\).

Practice

  1. State the eigenspace for \(\lambda=5\) of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) (Chapter 7, problem 1).
    \(E_5=\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0\rangle\}\)
  2. What is the dimension of \(E_5\) from the previous problem?
    1 (its geometric multiplicity).
  3. State the eigenspace for \(\lambda=1\) of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) (Chapter 7, problem 3).
    \(E_1=\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,-1\rangle\}\)
  4. The scalar matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}6&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}\) has a single repeated eigenvalue \(\lambda=6\) (Chapter 6, bonus 7). Find its FULL eigenspace \(E_6\) — is it just a line, or bigger?
    Solve \((A-6I)v=\vec0\): \(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}v=\vec0\) — every vector solves this. \(E_6\) is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), dimension 2 — a whole plane's worth of eigenvectors for one eigenvalue.
  5. True or false: every eigenspace (for an actual eigenvalue) contains infinitely many vectors.
    True — any nonzero scalar multiple of a member is also a member. A subspace is either just \(\{\vec0\}\) or infinite, and an eigenspace always contains at least one nonzero eigenvector.
  6. Can two DIFFERENT eigenvalues of the same matrix ever share the exact same eigenspace?
    No — if they did, every vector in that space would satisfy \(Av=\lambda_1v\) and \(Av=\lambda_2v\) simultaneously, forcing \(\lambda_1=\lambda_2\), a contradiction.
  7. bonus — What is the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda=2\) in the running example \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\)?
    1 — \(E_2\) is a single line, \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle2,1\rangle\}\).
  8. bonus — If an eigenspace of a 2×2 matrix turns out to be 2-dimensional (the whole plane), what does that say about the matrix?
    It must be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix — that's the only way a 2×2 matrix's eigenspace can be all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).
  9. bonus — Is \(\{\vec0\}\) alone ever a valid "eigenspace"?
    No — an eigenspace only exists for an actual eigenvalue, and being an eigenvalue requires at least one nonzero eigenvector in the first place. \(\{\vec0\}\) alone would mean \(\lambda\) wasn't really an eigenvalue at all.
  10. bonus — Explain why an eigenspace is always a genuine subspace (closed under addition and scalar multiplication), not just some arbitrary set of vectors.
    Because it's defined as \(\operatorname{Nul}(A-\lambda I)\), and null spaces of any matrix are always subspaces (Module 11, Chapter 10's proof applies directly — just to the matrix \(A-\lambda I\) instead of \(A\) itself).
  11. challenge — A 3×3 matrix \(A\) has eigenvalue \(\lambda=4\) with eigenspace \(E_4=\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0,1\rangle,\langle0,1,1\rangle\}\) (two independent vectors, Module 11 Chapter 5). What is the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda=4\), and give one more eigenvector for \(\lambda=4\) that isn't a scalar multiple of either spanning vector.
    Geometric multiplicity = 2 (the dimension of the span). Another eigenvector: any nontrivial combination, e.g. \(\langle1,0,1\rangle+\langle0,1,1\rangle=\langle1,1,2\rangle\) — inside \(E_4\), but not a scalar multiple of either single spanning vector.
