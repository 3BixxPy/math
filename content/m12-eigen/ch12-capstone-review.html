Module 12 Capstone — Linear Algebra Complete

Eleven chapters of eigenvalues and transformations, shuffled together — plus a diagnostic retake spanning all four Linear Algebra modules (9–12), the real vector intuition from game-dev you started with, now backed by the full formal machinery from raw vectors through eigenvalues.

Why mixed, not grouped by chapter

Same reasoning as every capstone since Module 0: a problem that doesn't announce its own chapter is the real test of whether the idea moved into durable memory, not just short-term pattern matching against a chapter title. This capstone raises the stakes one more time — it isn't just Module 12's eleven chapters shuffled together, it's the closing move of an entire four-module arc that started with nothing more than "a vector is an arrow with direction and magnitude" and ends with eigenvalues and diagonalization.

Mixed review (all of Module 12)

  1. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}8&0\\0&-3\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\lambda=8,-3\) — diagonal matrix, read straight off the diagonal.
  2. Is \(T(\langle x,y\rangle) = \langle x,\ 2y+1\rangle\) a linear transformation?
    No — \(T(\langle0,0\rangle)=\langle0,1\rangle\neq\langle0,0\rangle\).
  3. Given that \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalue \(\lambda=5\), find a corresponding eigenvector.
    \(A-5I=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow -x+y=0\Rightarrow y=x\). Eigenvector: \(\langle1,1\rangle\) (or any nonzero multiple).
  4. Find the kernel of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&6\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\).
    Row 2 is half of row 1: \(x+3y=0\Rightarrow x=-3y\). Kernel \(=\operatorname{span}\{\langle-3,1\rangle\}\).
  5. Find the standard matrix of \(T(\langle x,y\rangle)=\langle-x,\ 4y\rangle\).
    \(\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix}\)
  6. True or false: a 2×2 matrix with two distinct eigenvalues is always diagonalizable.
    True — eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically independent.
  7. A 3×3 matrix has eigenvalue \(\lambda=0\) with eigenspace \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,-1,0\rangle,\langle2,0,-1\rangle\}\). What is the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda=0\)?
    2 — the two spanning vectors aren't scalar multiples of each other, so they're independent, giving a 2-dimensional eigenspace.
  8. Does \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}\) have real or complex eigenvalues?
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2+4=0\) — complex, \(\lambda=\pm2i\).
  9. \(S\) has matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(T\) has matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\). Find the matrix for "\(T\), then \(S\)."
    \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\)
  10. If \(D=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&7\end{bmatrix}\), what are the diagonal entries of \(D^3\)?
    8 and 343.
  11. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\) via the characteristic equation.
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2=\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)\Rightarrow\lambda=5,2\) (matches problem 3's given eigenvalue).
  12. Is \(v=\langle0,1\rangle\) an eigenvector of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\)? If so, find \(\lambda\).
    \(Av=\langle0,5\rangle=5\langle0,1\rangle\) — yes, \(\lambda=5\).

Synthesis — concepts combining, not just shuffled

Two problems that each need multiple chapters' tools working together, not just one technique per problem.

Synthesis 1
Ch. 6 — characteristic equationCh. 7 — eigenvectorsCh. 9 — diagonalization

Fully diagonalize \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\), start to finish: find its eigenvalues, find an eigenvector for each, then write out \(P\), \(D\), and \(P^{-1}\) explicitly.

Step 1 — characteristic equation

\(\det(A-\lambda I)=(5-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-1)\Rightarrow\lambda=6,1\).

Step 2 — eigenvector for λ = 6

\(A-6I=\begin{bmatrix}-1&4\\1&-4\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow -x+4y=0\Rightarrow x=4y\). Eigenvector: \(\langle4,1\rangle\).

Step 3 — eigenvector for λ = 1

\(A-I=\begin{bmatrix}4&4\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow x+y=0\). Eigenvector: \(\langle1,-1\rangle\).

Result — assemble P, D, P⁻¹

\(P=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(D=\begin{bmatrix}6&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\). \(\det(P)=4(-1)-1(1)=-5\), so \(P^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{-5}\begin{bmatrix}-1&-1\\-1&4\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac15\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-4\end{bmatrix}\). Check: \(P\cdot P^{-1}=\dfrac15\begin{bmatrix}4(1)+1(1)&4(1)+1(-4)\\1(1)+(-1)(1)&1(1)+(-1)(-4)\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac15\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}=I\) ✓.

Synthesis 2
Ch. 2 — standard matrixCh. 3 — compositionCh. 6 — eigenvalues

A transformation reflects every vector over the line \(y=x\), then stretches the result by a factor of 2 in the x-direction only. Find its standard matrix, then find its eigenvalues.

Step 1 — standard matrix of "reflect over y = x"

\(e_1=\langle1,0\rangle\mapsto\langle0,1\rangle\), \(e_2=\langle0,1\rangle\mapsto\langle1,0\rangle\): \(R=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 2 — standard matrix of "stretch x by 2"

\(S=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 3 — combine: reflect happens first, so it's rightmost (Ch. 3)

\(A = S\cdot R = \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0&2\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\).

Result — find the eigenvalues

\(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-2=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm\sqrt2\) — real, but irrational eigenvalues, a good reminder that eigenvalues don't have to come out as clean integers.

Diagnostic retake

The original diagnostic flagged genuine vector intuition from 3D game-dev coding — but no formal names or machinery. Here's that same intuition, retested across all four Linear Algebra modules, now fully backed by names, proofs, and a general method.

1. Two forces act on a game object as vectors \(\langle3,4\rangle\) and \(\langle-1,2\rangle\). What is the net force vector, and its magnitude?

Originally: solved correctly by "adding the arrows tip-to-tail," with no formal notation for it.

Net \(=\langle3,4\rangle+\langle-1,2\rangle=\langle2,6\rangle\). Magnitude \(=\sqrt{2^2+6^2}=\sqrt{40}=2\sqrt{10}\). See Module 9 (Vectors).

2. Are \(\langle1,2\rangle\) and \(\langle2,4\rangle\) linearly independent? Do they span all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

Originally: intuited "these point the same way, so no" without the formal vocabulary.

They're scalar multiples of each other — linearly dependent (Module 11, Chapter 5) — so together they span only a 1-dimensional line through the origin, not all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

3. A game engine rotates a mesh by applying a matrix to every vertex. If two rotations happen back to back, how do you compute the combined transformation as a single matrix?

Originally: knew "you multiply the matrices" from using an engine's API, but hadn't derived why that's the correct operation.

Multiply the two matrices, second-applied on the left (Module 10's matrix multiplication defines the operation; this module's Chapter 3 proved why composition equals multiplication in the first place).

4. A transformation stretches some directions and rotates others. Is there a systematic way to find the directions it only stretches, without visualizing the whole transformation?

Originally: "yes, some direction probably stays put" — a correct hunch, but no method behind it.

Yes — solve the characteristic equation \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) for the eigenvalues, then \((A-\lambda I)v=\vec0\) for the eigenvectors of each (Chapters 5–7). Those eigenvector directions are exactly the ones that only stretch, never rotate.
Linear Algebra complete

Four modules, one continuous arc: Module 9 gave vectors their formal notation and operations — magnitude, dot products, angles — attached to intuition you already had. Module 10 built matrices and the machinery to solve systems of equations with them. Module 11 pulled back to the abstract vector space itself — subspaces, span, independence, basis, rank-nullity. And this module tied it all together: linear transformations as the geometric meaning of a matrix, composition as multiplication, and eigenvalues/eigenvectors as the special directions that reveal a transformation's true structure, powerful enough to compute a matrix raised to the thousandth power in three steps. That's the entire Linear Algebra sequence — the exact machinery behind every transform, animation, and simulation system you've ever called through a library without seeing what was underneath.
