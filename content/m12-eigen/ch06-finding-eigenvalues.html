Finding Eigenvalues — The Characteristic Equation

Chapter 5 could only check whether a GIVEN vector happens to be an eigenvector. This chapter finds every eigenvalue of a matrix from scratch, turning Module 10, Chapter 11's determinant test for singularity into an equation to solve.

Theorem — The characteristic equation

Start from the eigenvalue equation and rearrange:

\[ Av=\lambda v \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; Av-\lambda v = \vec0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; (A-\lambda I)v = \vec0 \]

(the \(I\) is essential here — see the mistake block below.) An eigenvector \(v\) must be nonzero, so this equation needs a nontrivial solution. By Module 10, Chapter 11, a homogeneous system \(Mx=\vec0\) has a nonzero solution exactly when \(M\) is singular — that is, when \(\det(M)=0\). Applying that here:

\[ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \]
(12.6.1)

This is the characteristic equation. Its solutions in \(\lambda\) are exactly \(A\)'s eigenvalues — no guessing or checking individual vectors required.

Worked example

Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\) — the running example from Chapter 5.

Step 1 — form A − λI

\(A-\lambda I = \begin{bmatrix}3-\lambda&-2\\2&-2-\lambda\end{bmatrix}\)

Step 2 — compute the determinant

\(\det(A-\lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(-2-\lambda) - (-2)(2)\)

\((3-\lambda)(-2-\lambda) = -6-3\lambda+2\lambda+\lambda^2 = \lambda^2-\lambda-6\), so the determinant is \(\lambda^2-\lambda-6+4 = \lambda^2-\lambda-2\).

Step 3 — solve the characteristic equation (Module 0 factoring)

\(\lambda^2-\lambda-2=0 \;\Rightarrow\; (\lambda-2)(\lambda+1)=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda=2 \text{ or } \lambda=-1\)

Result

The eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda=2\) and \(\lambda=-1\). Notice \(\lambda=2\) matches the eigenvalue found by hand for \(v=\langle2,1\rangle\) in Chapter 5 — consistent, as it must be. This matrix and these two eigenvalues carry forward through Chapters 7–10.

Common mistake

Forgetting the \(I\) and computing \(\det(A-\lambda)\) instead of \(\det(A-\lambda I)\). This isn't just sloppy — it's not even a defined operation: \(A\) is a matrix and \(\lambda\) is a plain number, and you can't subtract a number from a matrix directly. The identity matrix \(I\) is what lets \(\lambda\) be "matrix-shaped" first (\(\lambda I\) scales every diagonal entry by \(\lambda\) and leaves everything else zero), so that the subtraction only touches \(A\)'s diagonal entries. Always scale by \(I\) before subtracting.

Practice

  1. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\lambda=5,3\) — for a diagonal matrix, the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries themselves, no work required.
  2. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)\) → \(\lambda=1,3\)
  3. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-1=(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)\) → \(\lambda=1,-1\) (matches Chapter 5's problems on this exact matrix).
  4. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) (upper triangular).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-0\) → \(\lambda=1,3\). Shortcut: for any triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are simply its diagonal entries.
  5. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2=\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)\) → \(\lambda=5,2\)
  6. Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-8=\lambda^2-4\lambda-5=(\lambda-5)(\lambda+1)\) → \(\lambda=5,-1\)
  7. bonus — Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}6&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\lambda=6\) (a repeated root — both solutions of the characteristic equation equal 6, previewed further in Chapter 11).
  8. bonus — Without fully solving, state the sum and product of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\)'s two eigenvalues using its trace and determinant, and check against the worked example's answer (\(\lambda=2,-1\)).
    Sum \(=\operatorname{trace}(A)=3+(-2)=1=2+(-1)\) ✓. Product \(=\det(A)=3(-2)-(-2)(2)=-6+4=-2=2\times(-1)\) ✓. A handy shortcut for checking (or even finding) 2×2 eigenvalues.
  9. bonus — Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}7&-4\\4&-1\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(7-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)+16=\lambda^2-6\lambda+9=(\lambda-3)^2\) → \(\lambda=3\) (a repeated root — previewed in Chapter 11 as a case that can fail to diagonalize).
  10. bonus — Find the eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) — the 90° rotation matrix from Chapter 2.
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2+1=0 \Rightarrow \lambda^2=-1\) — no real solutions. A 90° rotation has no real eigenvalues (previewed fully in Chapter 11) — which makes total sense: it doesn't leave any real direction unrotated.
  11. challenge — For a general 2×2 matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\), derive its characteristic equation in terms of \(a,b,c,d\), and show it always has the form \(\lambda^2-(\operatorname{trace} A)\lambda+\det(A)=0\). Explain why this proves the "sum = trace, product = det" shortcut from the bonus problem above always works for 2×2 matrices.
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc = ad-a\lambda-d\lambda+\lambda^2-bc = \lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc) = \lambda^2-(\operatorname{trace}A)\lambda+\det(A)\). By Vieta's formulas (Module 0), for any quadratic \(\lambda^2-p\lambda+q=0\), the sum of its roots is \(p\) and the product is \(q\) — here \(p=\operatorname{trace}(A)\) and \(q=\det(A)\), which is exactly the shortcut.
