Special Cases — Repeated & Complex Eigenvalues

Every example so far conveniently diagonalized. Real matrices aren't always so polite. This is a shorter, lighter chapter — a quick tour of the two genuine ways diagonalization can fail, not a deep derivation.

Definition — Algebraic vs. geometric multiplicity

A repeated eigenvalue is a root of the characteristic equation with multiplicity greater than 1 — its algebraic multiplicity. Whether it still yields enough independent eigenvectors to diagonalize depends on its geometric multiplicity (Chapter 8's eigenspace dimension). Diagonalization only works if geometric multiplicity equals algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue — a repeated root doesn't automatically break anything, but it does remove the free pass that distinct eigenvalues get (Chapter 9's "distinct ⟹ automatically diagonalizable" shortcut no longer applies).

Worked example A — repeated, but still diagonalizable

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}6&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}\) has \(\lambda=6\) as a double root (algebraic multiplicity 2). Its eigenspace, found in Chapter 8, is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) — geometric multiplicity 2. The two multiplicities match, so \(A\) IS diagonalizable (trivially — it's already diagonal, with \(P\) any invertible matrix).

Worked example B — repeated, and NOT diagonalizable

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}7&-4\\4&-1\end{bmatrix}\) has characteristic equation \((\lambda-3)^2=0\) (Chapter 6, bonus 9) — \(\lambda=3\), algebraic multiplicity 2. Find its eigenspace:

Step 1 — solve (A − 3I)v = 0

\(A-3I=\begin{bmatrix}4&-4\\4&-4\end{bmatrix}\) — one independent equation, \(x-y=0\).

Result

\(E_3=\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1\rangle\}\), dimension 1 — geometric multiplicity 1. This does not match the algebraic multiplicity of 2: there's only a 1-dimensional worth of eigenvectors, not enough to fill a 2×2 \(P\). \(A\) is not diagonalizable, despite being a perfectly well-defined, useful matrix.

Complex eigenvalues

The characteristic equation of a real matrix is just a polynomial — and, exactly like Module 0's quadratic formula, a negative discriminant means no real solutions. The matrix still has eigenvalues; they're just complex. Geometrically, this corresponds to a transformation that rotates — no real direction is left unrotated (nothing just stretches in place).

Worked example — the 90° rotation, revisited

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) — the rotation matrix from Chapter 2, previewed again in Chapter 6, bonus 10.

Step 1 — characteristic equation

\(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2+1=0\). As a quadratic \(\lambda^2+0\lambda+1=0\), the discriminant is \(0^2-4(1)(1)=-4<0\) — no real solutions.

Result — algebra meets geometry

\(\lambda=\pm i\) (complex). This lines up perfectly with the geometry: a 90° rotation genuinely doesn't leave any real vector pointing the same direction it started — every nonzero vector gets turned, none just stretched — so it makes complete sense that no real eigenvalue exists for it.

Common mistake

Assuming every matrix is diagonalizable. There are two genuinely distinct ways it can fail: (1) a repeated eigenvalue whose geometric multiplicity falls short of its algebraic multiplicity (Worked example B), and (2) complex eigenvalues over the reals (the rotation example). A transformation can be perfectly well-defined, useful, and important — a 90° rotation is about as ordinary as transformations get — without ever being diagonalizable over the real numbers.

Try it yourself

A 2×2 matrix's characteristic equation has a negative discriminant. What does that tell you about its eigenvalues?

Practice

  1. Find the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\).
    2 — a double root at \(\lambda=5\).
  2. Find the eigenspace and geometric multiplicity for the previous problem. Is A diagonalizable?
    \(E_5=\mathbb{R}^2\) (whole plane), geometric multiplicity 2 — matches the algebraic multiplicity, so yes, diagonalizable.
  3. \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) has characteristic equation \((3-\lambda)^2=0\), so \(\lambda=3\) (algebraic multiplicity 2). Find its eigenspace by solving \((A-3I)v=0\). Is A diagonalizable?
    \(A-3I=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow y=0\), \(x\) free. \(E_3=\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0\rangle\}\), geometric multiplicity 1 — doesn't match algebraic multiplicity 2, so NOT diagonalizable.
  4. Does \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-4\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) have real or complex eigenvalues?
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2+4=0\Rightarrow\lambda^2=-4\), discriminant \(0-16=-16<0\) — complex, \(\lambda=\pm2i\).
  5. Does \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) have real or complex eigenvalues?
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2+1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+5=0\), discriminant \(16-20=-4<0\) — complex.
  6. Does \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) have real or complex eigenvalues? Find them if real.
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=(5-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-1)\), discriminant \(49-24=25>0\) — real, \(\lambda=6,1\) (distinct, so automatically diagonalizable — Chapter 9's shortcut).
  7. bonus — Can a matrix with complex eigenvalues still represent a perfectly legitimate linear transformation on real vectors (like rotating a game object)?
    Yes — complex eigenvalues just mean it's not diagonalizable over the reals. The transformation itself (a rotation, say) remains completely well-defined and useful.
  8. bonus — A repeated eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity EQUAL to its algebraic multiplicity. Is the matrix diagonalizable?
    Yes — that equality is precisely the diagonalizability condition (Worked example A).
  9. bonus — Could a 2×2 real matrix have one real eigenvalue and one complex eigenvalue?
    No — for a real matrix, complex eigenvalues always come in conjugate pairs (if \(a+bi\) is an eigenvalue, so is \(a-bi\)). You can't have just one alone.
  10. bonus — Geometrically, what does it mean for a real 2×2 matrix to have complex eigenvalues, in terms of what it does to the plane?
    It rotates (possibly combined with scaling) — there is no real direction that stays on its own line; every nonzero real vector gets turned to point somewhere genuinely different.
  11. challenge — Show that \(A=\begin{bmatrix}7&-4\\4&-1\end{bmatrix}\) (Worked example B, the non-diagonalizable repeated-eigenvalue case) satisfies \(\operatorname{trace}(A)=2\times3=6\) and \(\det(A)=3^2=9\), consistent with a repeated eigenvalue of 3 (using Chapter 6's sum/product-of-eigenvalues shortcut) — even though it fails to actually diagonalize.
    \(\operatorname{trace}(A)=7+(-1)=6=3+3\) ✓. \(\det(A)=7(-1)-(-4)(4)=-7+16=9=3\times3\) ✓. Both check out — the trace/determinant shortcut correctly identifies that BOTH eigenvalues equal 3, even though this particular matrix ultimately fails to diagonalize. The shortcut only reveals the eigenVALUES; it says nothing about whether enough independent eigenVECTORS exist to actually build \(P\).
