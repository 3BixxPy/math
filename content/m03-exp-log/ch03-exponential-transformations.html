Chapter 3 · concept:exponential-transformations

Transforming Exponential Graphs

Nothing new to learn here about how transformations work — Module 1, Chapter 6 already covered shifts in full generality. This chapter is just that same rule, applied to the new shape from Chapter 1, with one twist worth catching early: the asymptote moves too.

Why this matters

The whole point of learning transformations once, generally, in Module 1 was to never have to re-derive them for every new function family. Exponentials are the first real test of that payoff: concept:vertical-shift and concept:horizontal-shift apply here completely unchanged. The only genuinely new fact is what a vertical shift does to an exponential's asymptote — something a parabola never had to worry about.

Rule — General transformed exponential
\[ f(x) = a\cdot b^{\,x-h} + k \]
(3.3.1)

Same shift rules as Module 1, Chapter 6: h shifts horizontally (right if h > 0), k shifts vertically (up if k > 0) — reused directly, not re-derived. a still stretches/reflects exactly as in Module 1, Chapter 7.

Rule — The asymptote moves with k
\[ y = k \]
(3.3.2)

The base exponential bˣ has asymptote y=0 (Chapter 1). Adding +k shifts the entire graph up or down by k — asymptote included. So the transformed function's horizontal asymptote is y = k, not y = 0. A horizontal shift (the h) does not move the asymptote — it only slides points left/right along the same eventual height.

y = 2ˣ (baseline)y = 2^(x−h) + k
y = 2^(x−2) + 1 — asymptote y=1
Worked example

Graph y = 3^(x+2) − 1: state its horizontal asymptote and y-intercept.

Step 1 — identify h and k by matching the general form a·b^(x−h)+k

\(3^{x+2} = 3^{x-(-2)}\), so \(h=-2\) (shift left 2) and \(k=-1\) (shift down 1).

Step 2 — the asymptote follows k directly

Horizontal asymptote: \(y = -1\) (equation 3.3.2, not \(y=0\) — the vertical shift moved it).

Step 3 — find the y-intercept by evaluating at x=0

\(y = 3^{0+2}-1 = 3^2-1 = 9-1 = 8\). Y-intercept: \((0,8)\).

Common mistake

Forgetting that the horizontal asymptote itself moves with a vertical shift — writing "asymptote y=0" out of habit for any exponential, even y=5^x-3. The base exponential's asymptote is y=0 only when there's no +k at all. As soon as a vertical shift is present, the asymptote is y=k, full stop — read it directly off the equation, don't default to zero.

Practice

  1. State the horizontal asymptote of y = 2ˣ + 5.
    \(y=5\)
  2. State the horizontal asymptote of y = 3^(x-4).
    \(y=0\) — only a horizontal shift, no k term
  3. Find the y-intercept of y = 2ˣ + 3.
    \(2^0+3=4\) → \((0,4)\)
  4. Describe how y = 4^(x-1) relates to y = 4ˣ.
    Shifted right 1 — same horizontal-shift rule as Module 1, Chapter 6.
  5. Find the horizontal asymptote of y = 7ˣ − 2.
    \(y=-2\)
  6. Find the y-intercept of y = 5^(x+1) − 3.
    \(5^{0+1}-3=5-3=2\) → \((0,2)\)
  7. bonus — Write the equation of y = 2ˣ shifted left 3 and down 1.
    \(y=2^{x+3}-1\)
  8. bonus — Does a horizontal shift ever change the horizontal asymptote? Why or why not?
    No — only the vertical shift (k) moves the asymptote. A horizontal shift only slides the curve's points left/right along the same eventual height.
  9. bonus — Find the asymptote and y-intercept of y = -2ˣ + 4 (the negative sign reflects the curve — Module 1, Chapter 7).
    Asymptote \(y=4\); y-intercept: \(-2^0+4=-1+4=3\) → \((0,3)\)
  10. bonus — Two students disagree about the asymptote of y = 3^(x-5) + 2: one says y=0, the other says y=2. Who's right, and why?
    \(y=2\) is right — only \(k\) (the vertical shift) moves the asymptote; the "−5" is a horizontal shift and has no effect on it.
  11. challenge — Find a so that f(x) = a·(1/2)ˣ − 4 passes through the point (0, -1). What is the resulting horizontal asymptote?
    \(f(0)=a\cdot(1/2)^0-4=a-4=-1 \Rightarrow a=3\). So \(f(x)=3\cdot(1/2)^x-4\); asymptote \(y=-4\) (from the \(-4\), unaffected by the value of a).
