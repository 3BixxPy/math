The Natural Base e

Chapter 1 let b be almost any positive number. This chapter singles out one particular base — not a "nice" round number at all — that shows up so often in nature and later in calculus that it gets its own letter.

Why this matters

Imagine a bank paying 100% annual interest, compounded once a year: $1 becomes $2. Compound it twice a year (50% each time) and it becomes \((1.5)^2 = \$2.25\). Compound monthly, daily, every second — keep splitting the year into more and more pieces — and the final amount keeps rising, but not without bound. It settles down, closing in on a specific number just past 2.718. That number is e, the natural base. It's the base that appears whenever something grows continuously — every instant compounding a little more, forever — which is exactly the situation Calculus I spends an entire unit on. Meeting e now, as a constant you can compute with, means it's not a strange new symbol when calculus needs it.

Definition — e (not proven here — full justification is a calculus topic)
\[ e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n} \approx 2.71828\ldots \]
(3.2.1)

e is defined as the number that \((1+1/n)^n\) gets closer and closer to as n grows without bound. It's irrational (like π) — its decimal expansion never repeats or terminates — so "2.71828" is always an approximation, never the exact value. For now, treat e the same way you already treat π: a specific fixed constant with its own symbol, not a variable and not something you round off mid-calculation.

Worked example — watching the limit converge

Evaluate e⁰, note the value of e¹, then compute (1 + 1/n)ⁿ for n = 1, 12, 365 to see the convergence toward e directly.

Step 1 — the easy values first

\(e^0 = 1\) (any nonzero base to the 0 power is 1 — Module 0's zero-exponent rule still applies). \(e^1 = e \approx 2.71828\).

Step 2 — n = 1 (compounding once a year)

\(\left(1+\dfrac11\right)^1 = 2^1 = 2\)

Step 3 — n = 12 (monthly)

\(\left(1+\dfrac1{12}\right)^{12} \approx (1.0833)^{12} \approx 2.613\)

Step 4 — n = 365 (daily)

\(\left(1+\dfrac1{365}\right)^{365} \approx (1.00274)^{365} \approx 2.7146\)

Result — the pattern

2, then 2.613, then 2.7146 — each step gets closer to \(e \approx 2.71828\) without ever quite reaching it for any finite n. Compounding more often always helps, but with rapidly diminishing returns; the "infinitely often" limit is e itself.

Common mistake

Treating e as something you can freely round mid-problem, or worse, as if it were a variable you could solve for. It's neither — e is one fixed, exact irrational number, exactly like π. Carry it symbolically (write e, e², 3e, etc.) through every intermediate step of a calculation, and only convert to a decimal approximation at the very last step, if a numeric answer is actually asked for. Rounding early — using 2.7 in one step and 2.72 in the next — introduces error that compounds (no pun intended) through the rest of the problem.

e ≈ ? (three or four decimal places is fine):

Practice

  1. Evaluate e⁰.
    \(1\)
  2. Is e closer to 2.7 or 3?
    \(2.7\) (\(e\approx2.71828\))
  3. Compute (1 + 1/1)¹.
    \(2^1=2\)
  4. Compute (1 + 1/4)⁴, rounded to 3 decimal places.
    \((1.25)^4\approx2.441\)
  5. True or false: e is a rational number (expressible as a fraction of integers).
    False — like \(\pi\), \(e\) is irrational.
  6. Write e to 5 decimal places, from memory.
    \(2.71828\)
  7. bonus — As n → ∞, what does (1 + 1/n)ⁿ approach?
    \(e\), by definition (equation 3.2.1)
  8. bonus — Using e ≈ 2.718, which is larger: e² or 2ᵉ?
    \(e^2\approx7.389\) vs \(2^e\approx2^{2.718}\approx6.58\) — \(e^2\) is larger.
  9. bonus — A bank compounds interest continuously at a 100% annual rate on $1. After one year, how much money is there, in terms of e?
    \(\$e\) (\(\approx\$2.72\)) — continuous compounding at rate 1 for time 1 is exactly \(e^1\).
  10. bonus — Why can't you round e to 2.7 partway through a multi-step calculation and still expect an exact final answer?
    Rounding early throws away precision that later steps (especially multiplication or exponentiation) can amplify — the error compounds. Keep \(e\) symbolic until the last step.
  11. challenge — Compute (1 + 1/100)¹⁰⁰ to 4 decimal places by hand-reasoning (you may use a calculator for the arithmetic, not an "e" button), and explain why it should land close to, but strictly less than, e.
    \((1.01)^{100}\approx2.7048\) — less than \(e\approx2.71828\) because the sequence \((1+1/n)^n\) increases toward \(e\) from below; \(n=100\) is large but still finite, so it hasn't fully closed the gap yet.
