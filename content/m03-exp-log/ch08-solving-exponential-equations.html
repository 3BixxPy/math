Solving Exponential Equations

Chapters 1–3 graphed exponentials; Chapters 4–7 built the log toolbox. This chapter puts them together to actually solve for a variable trapped in an exponent — which is precisely what logarithms were invented to do.

Why this matters

An exponential equation like 2ˣ = 32 can't be solved by any Module 0 technique — there's no way to "undo" an exponent using addition, multiplication, or even square roots, because x isn't being multiplied or raised to a fixed power; it is the power. A logarithm is the one operation built specifically to pull a variable exponent back down to ground level. This is the payoff chapter for the entire module so far.

Method 1 — Same base (when both sides share one)

If both sides of the equation can be written as powers of the same base, the exponents themselves must be equal (an exponential function is one-to-one — Chapter 4's horizontal-line-test argument, since it has an inverse). Rewrite both sides with a common base, then just set the exponents equal and solve like an ordinary equation.

Method 2 — General method (take the log of both sides)
\[ a^{x}=b \ \Longrightarrow\ x=\log_a(b)=\frac{\ln b}{\ln a} \]
(3.8.1)

When the two sides don't share a common base, take ln (or log) of both sides, then use the power rule (Chapter 7) to bring the exponent down out of its trapped position — that's the whole strategy, every time.

Worked example — same base

Solve 2ˣ = 32.

Step 1 — rewrite 32 as a power of 2

\(32 = 2^5\), so the equation becomes \(2^x = 2^5\).

Step 2 — same base on both sides, so set exponents equal

\(x = 5\)

Worked example — no shared base, general method

Solve 5ˣ = 12.

Step 1 — 12 isn't a clean power of 5, so take ln of both sides

\(\ln(5^x) = \ln(12)\)

Step 2 — apply the power rule to bring x down

\(x\ln5 = \ln12\)

Step 3 — isolate x

\(x = \dfrac{\ln12}{\ln5} \approx \dfrac{2.4849}{1.6094} \approx 1.544\)

Worked example — a growth/decay word problem

A population of bacteria grows as P(t) = 200·(1.15)ᵗ (t in hours). How long until the population reaches 1000?

Step 1 — set up the equation

\(1000 = 200(1.15)^t \Rightarrow 5 = (1.15)^t\) (divide both sides by 200 first — isolate the exponential term before taking a log)

Step 2 — take ln of both sides, apply the power rule

\(\ln5 = t\ln(1.15)\)

Result

\(t = \dfrac{\ln5}{\ln1.15} \approx \dfrac{1.6094}{0.1398} \approx 11.5\) hours.

Common mistake

Two versions of the same slip: taking the log of only one side of the equation (logs must be applied to both sides, exactly like any other operation — you can't add 3 to only one side of an equation either), and forgetting to apply the power rule afterward to actually pull the exponent down. Writing ln(5ˣ) = ln(12) and then getting stuck is a sign the power rule step (equation 3.7.1) got skipped — that's the step that turns "x trapped in an exponent" into "x as an ordinary multiplied variable," which is the entire point of taking the log in the first place.

Practice

  1. Solve using same-base: 2ˣ = 16
    \(16=2^4 \Rightarrow x=4\)
  2. Solve using same-base: 5^(x-1) = 25
    \(25=5^2 \Rightarrow x-1=2 \Rightarrow x=3\)
  3. Solve using logs: 3ˣ = 20 (round to 3 decimals)
    \(x=\ln20/\ln3\approx2.996/1.099\approx2.727\)
  4. Solve using logs: 10ˣ = 7 (round to 3 decimals)
    \(x=\log(7)\approx0.845\)
  5. Solve using same-base: 4ˣ = 1
    \(1=4^0 \Rightarrow x=0\)
  6. Solve using logs: e^x = 15
    \(x=\ln15\approx2.708\)
  7. bonus — Solve using same-base: 2^(3x) = 8
    \(8=2^3 \Rightarrow 3x=3 \Rightarrow x=1\)
  8. bonus — Solve using logs: 7^(x+1) = 50 (round to 3 decimals)
    \((x+1)\ln7=\ln50 \Rightarrow x+1\approx2.010 \Rightarrow x\approx1.010\)
  9. bonus — An investment grows as A(t) = 500·(1.06)ᵗ. How many years until it doubles (round to 2 decimals)?
    \(1000=500(1.06)^t \Rightarrow 2=(1.06)^t \Rightarrow t=\ln2/\ln1.06\approx0.6931/0.0583\approx11.90\) years
  10. bonus — Why does Method 1 (same base) fail on 3ˣ = 20, forcing Method 2?
    20 isn't a whole-number (or clean rational) power of 3 — there's no way to rewrite it as \(3^{\text{something nice}}\), so the exponents can't be matched directly; logs are the only way in.
  11. challenge — Solve 2^x = 3^(x-1) for x, showing every log-law step (this has variables in the exponent on both sides — same overall strategy still applies).
    Take ln of both sides: \(x\ln2=(x-1)\ln3\). Expand: \(x\ln2=x\ln3-\ln3\). Collect x terms: \(x\ln2-x\ln3=-\ln3 \Rightarrow x(\ln2-\ln3)=-\ln3 \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{-\ln3}{\ln2-\ln3}=\dfrac{\ln3}{\ln3-\ln2}\approx\dfrac{1.0986}{0.4055}\approx2.710\)
