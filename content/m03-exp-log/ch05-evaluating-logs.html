Evaluating Logarithms by Hand

Chapter 4 defined what a logarithm means. This chapter is pure fluency — turning that definition into a fast mental habit, plus the two notation shortcuts (log and ln) you'll see everywhere from here on.

Why this matters

Every logarithm evaluation reduces to one question: "b to what power gives x?" That's it — no separate technique to learn, just the Chapter 4 definition applied directly, as a question instead of an equation to convert. The two notations below (log and ln) exist purely because base-10 and base-e logs are so common that writing the subscript every time would be tedious — they're shorthand, not new concepts.

Method — Evaluating log_b(x)

Ask: "b to what power gives x?" If you can answer that directly, that power is the value of the log. log_2(8): 2 to what power gives 8? \(2^3=8\), so \(\log_2(8)=3\).

Notation — Common log
\[ \log(x) \equiv \log_{10}(x) \]
(3.5.1)

An unlabeled log (no subscript) means base 10, by convention, in this course and in most STEM contexts. It's called the common log because base-10 came from a decimal world (slide rules, pre-calculator tables) where it was the most useful base to tabulate.

Notation — Natural log
\[ \ln(x) \equiv \log_e(x) \]
(3.5.2)

ln ("el-en") always means base e (Chapter 2), never anything else. It's called the natural log because e is the base that shows up naturally in continuous-growth situations — and, starting in Calculus I, ln is by far the most common log you'll see.

Worked example — direct evaluation

Evaluate log_2(8), log_3(1/9), and ln(e⁴).

Step 1 — log₂(8): 2 to what power gives 8?

\(2^3=8\), so \(\log_2(8)=3\)

Step 2 — log₃(1/9): 3 to what power gives 1/9?

\(3^{-2}=1/9\) (negative exponents give reciprocals — Module 0, Chapter 4), so \(\log_3(1/9)=-2\)

Step 3 — ln(e⁴): use the cancellation identity directly

\(\ln(e^4)=\log_e(e^4)=4\) by equation 3.4.2 — no computation needed at all, just the inverse relationship.

Worked example — estimating when there's no exact answer

Estimate log_10(50) without a calculator.

Step 1 — bracket it between two powers of 10 you know exactly

\(\log_{10}(10)=1\) and \(\log_{10}(100)=2\). Since \(10 < 50 < 100\), and log is increasing (bigger input → bigger output, same as the exponential it undoes), \(\log_{10}(50)\) is strictly between 1 and 2.

Result

\(1 < \log_{10}(50) < 2\) — and since 50 is closer to 100 than to 10 on a multiplicative scale (\(50 = \sqrt{10\cdot250}\)... more simply, 50 is the geometric middle-ish of 10 and 100), a reasonable guess lands near 1.7 — a calculator confirms \(\log_{10}(50)\approx1.699\).

Common mistake

Assuming an unlabeled "log" means natural log, because that's the one you'll use most starting in calculus. It doesn't — by the standard convention this course follows (equations 3.5.1–3.5.2), log with no subscript always means base 10, and ln always means base e. If a different base is meant, it's written as an explicit subscript (log_2, log_5, etc.). When in doubt about a source outside this course, check its own stated convention — but within these chapters, the rule above is fixed.

Practice

  1. Evaluate log_3(9).
    \(2\)
  2. Evaluate log_5(125).
    \(3\)
  3. Evaluate log(1) (common log).
    \(0\) (\(10^0=1\))
  4. Evaluate ln(e).
    \(1\)
  5. Evaluate log_4(1/16).
    \(-2\) (\(4^{-2}=1/16\))
  6. Between which two consecutive integers does log(600) fall?
    Between 2 and 3, since \(\log(100)=2\) and \(\log(1000)=3\)
  7. bonus — Evaluate log_2(1/8).
    \(-3\)
  8. bonus — Evaluate ln(e⁰).
    \(0\)
  9. bonus — True or false: writing "log(1000)" with no subscript means base e in this course.
    False — unlabeled \(\log\) always means base 10 here (equation 3.5.1); base e is always written \(\ln\).
  10. bonus — Evaluate log_7(7).
    \(1\) (\(b^1=b\) for any valid base)
  11. challenge — Without a calculator, is log(9000) closer to 3 or 4? Justify with bracketing, the way the worked example did for log(50).
    Bracket: \(\log(1000)=3\), \(\log(10000)=4\), so \(\log(9000)\) is between 3 and 4. Since 9000 is far closer to 10000 than to 1000, it should land close to 4 — a calculator gives \(\log(9000)\approx3.954\), confirming the estimate.
