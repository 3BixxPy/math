Chapter 11 · concept:vector-applications

Applications — Work, Distance, and Combining Techniques

No new rule this chapter — everything here is Chapters 1–10's toolkit, pointed at two problems worth solving by name: how much energy a force actually transfers, and how far a point sits from a line or plane.

Why this matters

Physics engines compute work constantly — how much energy a force actually delivers along a motion — and it's a direct, named use of the dot product, not a new idea. Distance from a point to a wall, a floor, or a rail is another constant physics-engine query, and it falls straight out of Chapter 6's projection once you frame it right: project the vector from a known point on the surface to your target point onto the surface's normal (or perpendicular) direction, and the length of that projection is the distance.

Definition — Work (a dot-product application)
\[ W = \vec F \cdot \vec d \]
(9.11.1)

Work done by a constant force \(\vec F\) over a displacement \(\vec d\) is exactly their dot product — nothing more than Chapter 4's formula, applied to physics quantities instead of abstract vectors. By equation 9.5.1, this equals \(|\vec F||\vec d|\cos\theta\): full force magnitude times full displacement magnitude only when they point the same way (\(\theta=0\)); less whenever they don't; zero when the force is perpendicular to the motion; negative when the force opposes the motion (like friction).

Method — distance from a point to a plane
\[ \text{distance from } Q \text{ to the plane} \;=\; \frac{|\vec n\cdot(Q-P_0)|}{|\vec n|} \]
(9.11.2)

where \(P_0\) is any known point on the plane and \(\vec n\) is its normal (Chapter 10). This is exactly the absolute value of Chapter 6's scalar projection (equation 9.6.2) of \(\vec v = Q-P_0\) onto \(\vec n\) — the "shadow" \(\vec v\) casts along the direction that already points straight out of the plane, which is precisely how far off the plane \(Q\) sits. The same idea handles a point-to-line distance in 2D: project the vector from a known point on the line onto a direction perpendicular to the line instead of onto the line's own direction.

Worked example — work done, and a distance to a plane

Find the work done by force \(\vec F=\langle3,4\rangle\) over displacement \(\vec d=\langle6,0\rangle\). Then find the distance from \(Q=(2,2,2)\) to the plane \(x+y+z=1\) (Chapter 10's worked-example plane, with \(P_0=(1,0,0)\) and \(\vec n=\langle1,1,1\rangle\)).

Step 1 — work, via the dot product (equation 9.11.1)

\(W = \langle3,4\rangle\cdot\langle6,0\rangle = 3(6)+4(0) = 18\)

Step 2 — for the distance, find v = Q − P₀

\(\vec v = Q-P_0 = (2,2,2)-(1,0,0) = \langle1,2,2\rangle\)

Step 3 — apply equation 9.11.2

\(\vec n\cdot\vec v = 1(1)+1(2)+1(2) = 5\), \(\ |\vec n| = \sqrt3\). Distance \(= \dfrac{|5|}{\sqrt3} \approx 2.89\)

Result

The force does \(18\) units of work. \(Q=(2,2,2)\) sits about \(2.89\) units off the plane \(x+y+z=1\) — a single projection, straight from Chapter 6, answers a question that sounds like it needs something new.

Common mistake

Computing work as \(|\vec F||\vec d|\) — multiplying magnitudes instead of taking the dot product. For the worked example's numbers, that would give \(|\langle3,4\rangle||\langle6,0\rangle| = 5(6) = 30\) instead of the correct \(18\) — silently wrong by a full third, because the force and displacement in that example don't point the same way. This error gives the right answer only in the one special case where \(\vec F\) and \(\vec d\) are perfectly aligned (\(\theta=0\), so \(\cos\theta=1\)); for any other angle, multiplying magnitudes overcounts the work, since it silently assumes 100% of the force acted along the motion.

Try it yourself

Find the work done by force \(\langle3,0\rangle\) over displacement \(\langle4,0\rangle\).

Practice

  1. Compute the work done by force \(\langle2,5\rangle\) over displacement \(\langle3,0\rangle\).
    \(W=2(3)+5(0)=6\)
  2. A force \(\langle0,10\rangle\) (straight up) acts over a purely horizontal displacement \(\langle4,0\rangle\). Find the work done, and explain why the result makes physical sense.
    \(W=0(4)+10(0)=0\). A force perpendicular to the motion (Chapter 5's orthogonality test) transfers no energy along that motion at all — lifting hard doesn't help you move sideways.
  3. Find the distance from the point \((3,0,0)\) to the plane \(2x+2y+z=6\) (a point on the plane: \((0,3,0)\)).
    \(\vec v=(3,0,0)-(0,3,0)=\langle3,-3,0\rangle\); \(\vec n=\langle2,2,1\rangle\), \(|\vec n|=3\). \(\vec n\cdot\vec v = 6-6+0=0\) — the point is actually ON the plane.
  4. Find the distance from the origin to the plane \(2x+2y+z=6\) (use the point \((3,0,0)\) on the plane).
    \(\vec v=(0,0,0)-(3,0,0)=\langle-3,0,0\rangle\); \(\vec n\cdot\vec v=-6\); \(|\vec n|=3\). Distance \(=6/3=2\).
  5. Compute the work done by force \(\langle1,1\rangle\) over displacement \(\langle1,-1\rangle\). Explain why the answer is exactly zero even though neither vector is the zero vector.
    \(W=1(1)+1(-1)=0\). The two vectors are perpendicular (dot product zero), so none of the force acts along the displacement's direction.
  6. A constant 10 N force acts at a \(60°\) angle to a straight-line 5 m displacement. Find the work done.
    \(W=|F||d|\cos\theta = 10(5)\cos60° = 50(0.5)=25\) J.
  7. bonus — A force \(\vec F=\langle-2,0\rangle\) acts exactly opposite a displacement \(\vec d=\langle5,0\rangle\) (like friction). Compute the work done. What does the negative sign mean physically?
    \(W=-2(5)+0(0)=-10\). Negative work means the force removes energy from the motion (decelerates it) rather than adding to it.
  8. bonus — Find the distance from \((5,5,5)\) to the plane \(z=0\) using equation 9.11.2, and check it matches what you'd expect just by reading the z-coordinate.
    \(\vec n=\langle0,0,1\rangle\), \(P_0=(0,0,0)\), \(\vec v=\langle5,5,5\rangle\). \(\vec n\cdot\vec v=5\), \(|\vec n|=1\). Distance \(=5\) — exactly the z-coordinate, as expected, since the xy-plane's normal is purely vertical.
  9. bonus — Explain why \(|\vec F||\vec d|\) overestimates work whenever force and displacement aren't perfectly aligned, but gives the exact right answer when they are.
    \(W=|\vec F||\vec d|\cos\theta\) always. \(\cos\theta=1\) only when \(\theta=0\) (perfect alignment), making \(|\vec F||\vec d|\cos\theta=|\vec F||\vec d|\) exactly. For any other angle, \(\cos\theta<1\), so the true work is strictly less than the naive product of magnitudes.
  10. bonus — A robot arm applies force \(\langle0,0,8\rangle\) (straight up) while a payload moves along displacement \(\langle3,3,0\rangle\) (purely horizontal). Compute the work done. Does the size of the force change the answer here?
    \(W=0(3)+0(3)+8(0)=0\), regardless of how large the vertical force is — a purely vertical force does zero work on a purely horizontal motion, no matter its magnitude.
  11. challenge — A force \(\vec F=\langle6,8\rangle\) (magnitude 10) acts over a displacement of magnitude 4 that makes a \(30°\) angle with \(\vec F\). Find the exact work done. Then find what displacement magnitude, applied by a force of magnitude 5 perfectly aligned with it instead (\(\theta=0\)), would produce the same amount of work.
    \(W = 10(4)\cos30° = 40\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = 20\sqrt3 \approx 34.64\). For a perfectly aligned 5 N force to match that: \(20\sqrt3 = 5\,d \Rightarrow d = 4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93\) m.
