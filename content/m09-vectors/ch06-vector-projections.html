Chapter 6 · concept:vector-projection

Vector Projections

Chapter 5's diagram already showed a hint of this: the dashed "shadow" a vector casts onto another vector's line. This chapter turns that shadow into a formula you can compute exactly.

Why this matters

Think about a top-down collision response: an object moving with velocity \(\vec u\) hits a wall running in direction \(\vec v\). To make it slide along the wall instead of stopping dead, you need to split \(\vec u\) into two pieces — the part running along the wall (which should survive the collision) and the part running into the wall (which should get cancelled). That "part of \(\vec u\) running along \(\vec v\)" is exactly a projection: how much of one vector points in another vector's direction, expressed as an actual vector (or, if you only need the length of that piece, a single signed number).

Rule — vector and scalar projection
\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) \;=\; \left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\right)\vec v \]
(9.6.1)

The vector projection of \(\vec u\) onto \(\vec v\) — the actual "along-\(\vec v\)" piece of \(\vec u\), still a vector, still pointing along \(\vec v\)'s line.

\[ \operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u) \;=\; \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|} \]
(9.6.2)

The scalar projection — just the signed length of that shadow (positive if \(\vec u\) leans toward \(\vec v\)'s direction, negative if it leans away). Notice \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\cdot\hat v\): the scalar projection is a length, \(\hat v\) is a direction, and (length)\(\times\)(direction) rebuilds the full vector projection.

u = ⟨1,4⟩, v = ⟨4,1⟩ → comp_v(u) = 1.94, proj_v(u) = ⟨1.88,0.47⟩

Green: \(u\), adjustable. Blue: fixed \(v=\langle4,1\rangle\), with its full line dashed through the origin. Dashed red: the "shadow" dropped straight from \(u\)'s tip onto that line. Purple: \(\operatorname{proj}_v(u)\), the vector from the origin to where the shadow lands.

Worked example — an axis-aligned case, then a general one

Project \(\langle3,4\rangle\) onto \(\langle1,0\rangle\). Then project \(\langle2,3\rangle\) onto \(\langle1,1\rangle\).

Step 1 — the axis-aligned case: project ⟨3,4⟩ onto ⟨1,0⟩

\(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 3(1)+4(0)=3\), \(|\vec v|^2=1\). \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \left(\dfrac{3}{1}\right)\langle1,0\rangle = \langle3,0\rangle\) — exactly \(\vec u\)'s x-component, and nothing else. That's a good sanity check: projecting onto a coordinate axis should just isolate that coordinate, and it does.

Step 2 — a non-axis-aligned case: project ⟨2,3⟩ onto ⟨1,1⟩

\(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 2(1)+3(1)=5\), \(|\vec v|^2 = 1^2+1^2=2\). \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \left(\dfrac{5}{2}\right)\langle1,1\rangle = \langle2.5,\ 2.5\rangle\)

Result

\(\operatorname{proj}_{\langle1,0\rangle}\langle3,4\rangle = \langle3,0\rangle\), and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\langle1,1\rangle}\langle2,3\rangle = \langle2.5,2.5\rangle\). Same formula both times — the axis-aligned case just makes it easy to check by eye first.

Common mistake

Dividing by \(|\vec v|\) once instead of \(|\vec v|^2\) in equation 9.6.1 — an easy formula to misremember. Here's why it needs the square: one factor of \(1/|\vec v|\) turns \(\vec v\) into a unit direction (\(\hat v\)); the second factor of \(1/|\vec v|\) scales that direction by the correct signed length, \(\operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\). Drop one factor and you get a vector pointing the right way but the wrong length — off by a factor of \(|\vec v|\), silently, unless you check by eye against a simple case (like the axis-aligned example above).

Try it yourself

Find the scalar projection of \(\langle3,0\rangle\) onto \(\langle1,1\rangle\).

Practice

  1. Find the scalar projection of \(\langle5,0\rangle\) onto \(\langle0,3\rangle\).
    Dot \(=0\), so \(\operatorname{comp}=0\) — perpendicular vectors cast no shadow at all.
  2. Find the vector projection of \(\langle2,5\rangle\) onto \(\langle1,0\rangle\).
    Dot \(=2\), \(|v|^2=1\): \(\operatorname{proj}=\langle2,0\rangle\) — again, just the x-component.
  3. Find the vector projection of \(\langle1,2\rangle\) onto \(\langle3,4\rangle\).
    Dot \(=3+8=11\), \(|v|^2=25\): \(\operatorname{proj}=\dfrac{11}{25}\langle3,4\rangle=\langle1.32,1.76\rangle\).
  4. Find the scalar projection of \(\langle-2,3\rangle\) onto \(\langle4,0\rangle\). What does the sign tell you?
    Dot \(=-8\), \(|v|=4\): \(\operatorname{comp}=-2\). Negative means \(\langle-2,3\rangle\) leans away from \(\langle4,0\rangle\)'s direction, not toward it.
  5. Find the scalar projection of \(\langle6,8\rangle\) onto \(\langle0,1\rangle\).
    Dot \(=8\), \(|v|=1\): \(\operatorname{comp}=8\) — the y-component, isolated.
  6. A wall runs in direction \(\langle1,1\rangle\). An incoming velocity is \(\langle2,-2\rangle\), heading straight at the wall perpendicular to it. Find the vector projection of the velocity onto the wall direction, and explain what a result of \(\langle0,0\rangle\) means physically.
    Dot \(=2(1)+(-2)(1)=0\), so \(\operatorname{proj}=\langle0,0\rangle\). Physically: none of this velocity runs along the wall — it's moving straight into it, so a wall-sliding collision response would leave zero along-wall motion.
  7. bonus — Explain, in your own words, why the vector projection formula divides by \(|\vec v|^2\) rather than \(|\vec v|\).
    One factor of \(1/|\vec v|\) turns \(\vec v\) into the unit direction \(\hat v\); the other scales that direction by the correct signed length \(\operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\). Together: \(\left(\dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|}\right)\cdot\dfrac{\vec v}{|\vec v|}=\left(\dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\right)\vec v\).
  8. bonus — Show that \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\cdot\hat v\), and explain why that's exactly "a length times a direction."
    \(\operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\hat v = \dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|}\cdot\dfrac{\vec v}{|\vec v|} = \dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\vec v = \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\). Any vector decomposes as (its length) \(\times\) (its unit direction) — this is that decomposition applied to the projection.
  9. bonus — Can a scalar projection ever come out negative? What does that mean geometrically?
    Yes, whenever the angle between \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) exceeds \(90°\) (so \(\vec u\cdot\vec v<0\)). Geometrically, \(\vec u\)'s shadow falls on the opposite ray from \(\vec v\), not on \(\vec v\)'s own side of the origin.
  10. bonus — Project \(\langle5,5\rangle\) onto itself. What do you get, and why does that make sense?
    Dot \(=50\), \(|v|^2=50\): \(\operatorname{proj}=\langle5,5\rangle\) — itself, unchanged. A vector's shadow onto its own line is the whole vector; there's nothing of it pointing off that line.
  11. challenge — Let \(\vec u=\langle3,4\rangle\), \(\vec v=\langle1,0\rangle\). Compute \(\vec u - \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u)\), then verify with a dot product that this "leftover" vector is perpendicular to \(\vec v\). Why does this always work, for any \(\vec u,\vec v\)?
    \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u)=\langle3,0\rangle\) (Worked example, Step 1). \(\vec u - \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \langle3,4\rangle-\langle3,0\rangle=\langle0,4\rangle\). Check: \(\langle0,4\rangle\cdot\langle1,0\rangle=0\) — perpendicular. This always works because the projection is built to capture exactly the part of \(\vec u\) that lies along \(\vec v\); whatever's left over, by construction, has zero component in that direction — which is precisely what a dot product of 0 with \(\vec v\) means (Chapter 5).
