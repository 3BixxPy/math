Magnitude & Unit Vectors

Chapter 2 combined vectors. This chapter measures one: how "long" a vector is, and what it means to keep only its direction and throw the length away.

Why this matters

rigidbody.velocity.magnitude. Vector3.Distance(a, b). direction.normalized. You've called all three, probably without thinking hard about what's underneath them. Magnitude is the vector's own length — how fast, how far, how much. Normalizing — dividing a vector by its own magnitude to get a length-1 version pointing the same way — is the operation behind almost every direction-only calculation in a game: aiming, lighting, "which way should this thing move," all need pure direction with the speed/distance information stripped out first. Get magnitude and normalizing exactly right here and Chapters 4–8 (dot products, cross products, angles) all lean on them directly.

Rule — magnitude (length) of a vector
\[ |\vec v| \;=\; \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} \]
(9.3.1)

This is the Pythagorean theorem, restated with as many terms as the vector has dimensions. In 2D, \(|\langle v_1,v_2\rangle| = \sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2}\) is literally "the hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs \(v_1\) and \(v_2\)" — Module 0's theorem, unchanged. In 3D it's the same idea one dimension up (the 3D diagonal-of-a-box formula, if you've seen it); in \(n\) dimensions it's the exact same pattern, no new idea required, just more terms under the root.

Definition — Unit vector & normalizing

A unit vector is any vector with magnitude exactly 1. To normalize a nonzero vector \(\vec v\) — the exact term used by Vector3.normalized and every game-math library — means dividing it by its own magnitude:

\[ \hat v \;=\; \frac{\vec v}{|\vec v|} \]
(9.3.2)

(The "hat" notation \(\hat v\) specifically means "this is a unit vector.") Dividing a vector by a scalar is scalar multiplication by \(1/|\vec v|\) (Chapter 2), applied to every component. The result always has magnitude exactly 1 and points in the exact same direction as the original — normalizing changes only the length, never the direction. This is why the zero vector can never be normalized: there's no direction to preserve, and equation 9.3.2 would divide by zero.

Worked example — magnitude and normalizing

Find the magnitude of \(\langle3,4\rangle\), then normalize it.

Step 1 — apply equation 9.3.1

\(|\langle3,4\rangle| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\) — the familiar 3-4-5 right triangle, now read as a vector's length.

Step 2 — divide every component by that magnitude (equation 9.3.2)

\(\hat v = \dfrac{\langle3,4\rangle}{5} = \left\langle \dfrac35,\ \dfrac45\right\rangle = \langle0.6,\ 0.8\rangle\)

Result

\(|\langle3,4\rangle| = 5\), and its unit vector is \(\langle0.6,0.8\rangle\). Check: \(\sqrt{0.6^2+0.8^2}=\sqrt{0.36+0.64}=\sqrt1=1\) — exactly length 1, as required.

Common mistake

Two ways this goes wrong. First: stopping at the sum of squares and forgetting the square root — \(3^2+4^2=25\) is not the magnitude, \(\sqrt{25}=5\) is. Second, when normalizing: dividing by the wrong thing — dividing every component by just one component (say, dividing both \(3\) and \(4\) by \(3\)) instead of dividing both by the magnitude \(5\). Only dividing by the actual magnitude guarantees the result has length exactly 1; dividing by anything else gives a vector that still points the same way but isn't actually a unit vector, which will quietly break any downstream formula that assumes one.

Find \(|\langle3,4\rangle|\).

Practice

  1. Find \(|\langle6,8\rangle|\).
    \(\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10\)
  2. Find \(|\langle1,1\rangle|\).
    \(\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2\approx1.414\)
  3. Find \(|\langle2,3,6\rangle|\).
    \(\sqrt{4+9+36}=\sqrt{49}=7\)
  4. Normalize \(\langle0,5\rangle\).
    \(|\langle0,5\rangle|=5\), so \(\hat v=\langle0,1\rangle\).
  5. Normalize \(\langle1,1\rangle\).
    \(|\langle1,1\rangle|=\sqrt2\), so \(\hat v=\left\langle\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\approx\langle0.707,0.707\rangle\).
  6. A direction vector \(\langle-3,4\rangle\) is used before normalizing. Find its magnitude and its normalized form.
    \(|\langle-3,4\rangle|=\sqrt{9+16}=5\); normalized: \(\langle-0.6,0.8\rangle\).
  7. bonus — Why is \(|\vec v|\) always \(\ge0\)? When is it exactly 0?
    Every term under the root is a square, never negative, and a square root of a nonnegative number is nonnegative. It equals exactly 0 only for the zero vector \(\langle0,\dots,0\rangle\).
  8. bonus — A physics call expects a unit direction vector, but a script passes \(\langle2,0,0\rangle\) (length 2) instead. In general terms, what kind of bug does this cause downstream?
    Any formula that assumes length 1 (angle formulas, "move exactly this far along the ray") gets silently scaled — results come out roughly twice as large as intended, in the right direction but the wrong amount.
  9. bonus — What is \(|\langle0,0\rangle|\)? Can the zero vector be normalized? Explain.
    \(|\langle0,0\rangle|=0\). It cannot be normalized — equation 9.3.2 would divide by 0, and the zero vector has no direction to preserve in the first place.
  10. bonus — Find the unit vector pointing in the same direction as \(\langle5,12\rangle\).
    \(|\langle5,12\rangle|=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}=13\), so \(\hat v=\left\langle\frac{5}{13},\frac{12}{13}\right\rangle\).
  11. challenge — Normalize both \(\langle3,4\rangle\) and \(\langle6,8\rangle\). What do you get, and what does this prove about what normalizing keeps and what it discards?
    \(\langle3,4\rangle\) has magnitude 5 → \(\langle0.6,0.8\rangle\). \(\langle6,8\rangle\) has magnitude 10 → \(\langle6/10,8/10\rangle=\langle0.6,0.8\rangle\) — identical. This proves normalizing discards magnitude completely and keeps only direction: any positive scalar multiple of a vector normalizes to the exact same unit vector.
