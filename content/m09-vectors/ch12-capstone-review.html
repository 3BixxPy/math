Module 9 Capstone — Mixed Review & Diagnostic Retake

Eleven chapters, shuffled together, plus a look back at the vector intuition this whole module started from — now with formal notation, and two named products attached to skills that were already there.

Why mixed, not grouped by chapter

Same reasoning as every capstone since Module 0: a problem that doesn't announce its own chapter is the real test of whether an idea moved into durable memory, not just short-term pattern matching against a chapter title. This module built three threads in sequence — the vector itself (Chapters 1–3: notation, arithmetic, length), the two products it supports (Chapters 4–8: dot for alignment and angles, cross for area and normals), and then both threads put to work describing actual geometric objects (Chapters 9–11: lines, planes, work, distance). Shuffling the review is one more chance to notice how naturally those three threads lean on each other now.

Mixed review (all of Module 9)

  1. Find the vector from point \((2,5)\) to point \((7,1)\).
    \(\langle5,-4\rangle\) (Chapter 1).
  2. Compute \(\langle4,-1\rangle + 3\langle1,2\rangle\).
    \(\langle4,-1\rangle+\langle3,6\rangle=\langle7,5\rangle\) (Chapter 2).
  3. Normalize \(\langle0,-7\rangle\).
    Magnitude \(7\), so \(\hat v=\langle0,-1\rangle\) (Chapter 3).
  4. Compute \(\langle2,-3,1\rangle \cdot \langle1,1,1\rangle\).
    \(2-3+1=0\) (Chapter 4).
  5. Are \(\langle3,-1\rangle\) and \(\langle1,3\rangle\) perpendicular?
    \(3(1)+(-1)(3)=0\) — yes (Chapter 5).
  6. Find the angle between \(\langle2,0\rangle\) and \(\langle1,\sqrt3\rangle\).
    Dot \(=2\), \(|u|=2\), \(|v|=2\). \(\cos\theta=2/4=0.5 \Rightarrow \theta=60°\) (Chapter 5).
  7. Find the scalar projection of \(\langle4,3\rangle\) onto \(\langle0,1\rangle\).
    Dot \(=3\), \(|v|=1\): \(\operatorname{comp}=3\) (Chapter 6).
  8. Compute \(\langle2,1,0\rangle \times \langle0,1,2\rangle\).
    \(\langle 1(2)-0(1),\ 0(0)-2(2),\ 2(1)-1(0)\rangle = \langle2,-4,2\rangle\) (Chapter 7).
  9. Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\langle3,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,0,5\rangle\).
    Cross product \(=\langle0,-15,0\rangle\), magnitude \(15\) (Chapter 8).
  10. For \(\vec r(t)=(2,1,0)+t\langle0,1,3\rangle\), find the point at \(t=3\).
    \((2,\ 1+3,\ 0+9) = (2,4,9)\) (Chapter 9).
  11. Find the normal vector to the plane \(5x-2y+z=8\).
    \(\langle5,-2,1\rangle\) (Chapter 10).
  12. Compute the work done by force \(\langle7,0\rangle\) over displacement \(\langle0,7\rangle\).
    \(7(0)+0(7)=0\) — perpendicular force and motion (Chapter 11).

Synthesis — concepts combining, not just shuffled

Two problems that each need multiple chapters' tools working together, not just one technique per problem.

Synthesis 1
Ch. 1 — vector between pointsCh. 7 — cross productCh. 10 — plane equation

Find the equation of the plane through \(A=(1,2,3)\), \(B=(4,0,3)\), and \(C=(1,5,0)\), start to finish.

Step 1 — find two edge vectors from A (Ch. 1)

\(\overrightarrow{AB}=B-A=\langle3,-2,0\rangle\), \(\overrightarrow{AC}=C-A=\langle0,3,-3\rangle\)

Step 2 — cross them to get a normal (Ch. 7)

\(\vec n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \big\langle (-2)(-3)-0(3),\ \ 0(0)-3(-3),\ \ 3(3)-(-2)(0)\big\rangle = \langle6,9,9\rangle\), or simplified, \(\langle2,3,3\rangle\).

Step 3 — plug into the plane equation (Ch. 10)

Using \(A=(1,2,3)\): \(2(x-1)+3(y-2)+3(z-3)=0 \Rightarrow 2x+3y+3z=17\)

Result

\(2x+3y+3z=17\). Check: \(B=(4,0,3)\Rightarrow8+0+9=17\) ✓, \(C=(1,5,0)\Rightarrow2+15+0=17\) ✓. Three separate chapters — finding a displacement, crossing it with another, plugging the result into a dot-product plane equation — chained into one routine calculation.

Synthesis 2
Ch. 4/5 — dot product & angleCh. 6 — projection

For \(\vec u=\langle3,4\rangle\) and \(\vec v=\langle4,3\rangle\): find the angle between them, then find the scalar and vector projection of \(\vec u\) onto \(\vec v\).

Step 1 — one dot product and both magnitudes, needed for everything that follows

\(\vec u\cdot\vec v = 3(4)+4(3)=24\). \(|\vec u|=\sqrt{9+16}=5\), \(|\vec v|=\sqrt{16+9}=5\).

Step 2 — the angle (Ch. 5)

\(\cos\theta = \dfrac{24}{5(5)} = \dfrac{24}{25} = 0.96 \ \Rightarrow\ \theta = \cos^{-1}(0.96) \approx 16.26°\)

Step 3 — the projections (Ch. 6), reusing the same dot product

\(\operatorname{comp}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \dfrac{24}{5} = 4.8\). \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}(\vec u) = \dfrac{24}{25}\langle4,3\rangle = \langle3.84,\ 2.88\rangle\)

Result

The angle is about \(16.26°\); the scalar projection is \(4.8\); the vector projection is \(\langle3.84,2.88\rangle\). Notice the exact same dot product, \(24\), fed both formulas — Chapters 5 and 6 aren't two unrelated tricks, they're two different follow-up questions asked of the same underlying computation.

Diagnostic retake

The original diagnostic flagged real vector intuition from years of 3D game-dev coding and physics — magnitude and direction, normalizing, and both products, all already used correctly, just called through a library function instead of derived. These three ask you to do, by hand, exactly what that library function was doing underneath.

1. A call to Vector3.Dot(a, b) returns \(0.5\), and both \(a\) and \(b\) happen to be unit vectors. What angle does that correspond to?

Originally: this exact computation has been running in your code for years — the diagnostic found the intuition, just not the formal machinery behind it.

\(\cos\theta=0.5 \Rightarrow \theta=60°\). See Chapters 4–5.

2. Two edge vectors of a mesh triangle are \(\langle1,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,0,-1\rangle\). Compute their cross product and state what it represents.

Originally: Vector3.Cross called constantly for exactly this purpose, with no need (until now) to know how it worked.

\(\langle1,0,0\rangle\times\langle0,0,-1\rangle=\langle0,1,0\rangle\) — a normal vector pointing straight up (\(+y\)), perpendicular to the triangle spanned by those two edges. See Chapters 7–8.

3. A raw direction vector \(\langle6,8,0\rangle\) needs to drive a movement-direction-only calculation (speed handled separately). What single operation has to happen first, and what's the result?

Originally: .normalized, called reflexively, whenever only direction mattered.

Normalize it: \(|\langle6,8,0\rangle|=10\), so \(\hat v = \langle0.6,0.8,0\rangle\). See Chapter 3.
Module 9 complete

From "a vector is a magnitude and a direction, exactly like a Vector2 or Vector3" to a full toolkit built on top of that: component form and the vector/point distinction, addition/subtraction/scalar multiplication, magnitude and normalizing, the dot product both algebraically and geometrically (with the orthogonality test it gives for free), projections, the cross product both algebraically and geometrically (with the normal-vector and area readings it gives for free), and using all of it to write down lines and planes and to solve work and distance problems by name. Twelve chapters that took existing, correct intuition and gave it formal notation, proof, and a few genuinely new tools (the cross product chief among them) built directly on top of it.
