Chapter 8 · concept:cross-product-geometry

Cross Product — Geometric Meaning

Chapter 7 gave the cross product a formula and a direction rule. This chapter explains what its length actually measures, and nails down exactly why "perpendicular to both inputs" is the property that makes it so useful for surface normals.

Why this matters

This is the exact technique behind computing a surface normal from a triangle or polygon's own edges: take two edge vectors of the face, cross them, and the result is perpendicular to the whole flat surface — automatically, with no separate "figure out what's perpendicular" step. Every mesh-normal calculation you've ever seen a game engine do under the hood works this way. This chapter makes precise both halves of why that works: what direction the result points (proven, not just asserted), and what its length tells you (the area of the surface those two edges bound).

Rule — magnitude of the cross product = parallelogram area
\[ |\vec u \times \vec v| \;=\; |\vec u|\,|\vec v|\sin\theta \]
(9.8.1)

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\). This number is exactly the area of the parallelogram with \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) as two of its sides — a nice mirror of Chapter 5's \(\cos\theta\) formula, but with \(\sin\theta\) instead, and a length instead of a scalar with a sign. Half of that parallelogram is a triangle, so \(\frac12|\vec u\times\vec v|\) gives the area of the triangle spanned by \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) from a shared vertex — a direct, practical way to get a triangle's area (in 3D, where the shoelace-formula-style 2D tricks don't apply) straight from two of its edge vectors.

Rule — the cross product is perpendicular to both inputs
\[ (\vec u\times\vec v)\cdot\vec u = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (\vec u\times\vec v)\cdot\vec v = 0 \]
(9.8.2)

\(\vec u\times\vec v\) is perpendicular to both \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) — checkable directly with Chapter 5's orthogonality test, since a dot product of zero is exactly what "perpendicular" means. This is precisely why crossing two edge vectors of a flat surface gives you a normal to that surface: any vector lying in the plane of the surface can be written as some combination of the two edges, and \(\vec u\times\vec v\) is perpendicular to both of them at once.

Worked example — verifying perpendicularity, and a parallelogram's area

Verify that \(\langle1,0,0\rangle\times\langle0,1,0\rangle=\langle0,0,1\rangle\) (Chapter 7's worked example) is perpendicular to both inputs. Then find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\langle2,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,3,0\rangle\).

Step 1 — dot the result with each original vector

\(\langle0,0,1\rangle\cdot\langle1,0,0\rangle = 0\). \(\langle0,0,1\rangle\cdot\langle0,1,0\rangle = 0\). Both zero — perpendicular to both, confirming equation 9.8.2.

Step 2 — cross the two new vectors

\(\langle2,0,0\rangle\times\langle0,3,0\rangle = \langle 0(0)-0(3),\ 0(0)-2(0),\ 2(3)-0(0)\rangle = \langle0,0,6\rangle\)

Step 3 — take its magnitude

\(|\langle0,0,6\rangle| = 6\)

Result

The perpendicularity checks out exactly. The parallelogram spanned by \(\langle2,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,3,0\rangle\) has area \(6\) — which also checks out by eye: it's literally a \(2\times3\) rectangle sitting in the xy-plane.

Common mistake

Trying to take the cross product of two 2D vectors directly, the same way you'd take their dot product. It isn't defined — "perpendicular to a flat plane" requires a third dimension to point into, which a 2D vector simply doesn't have. What some engines and textbooks call the "2D cross product" (Chapter 7's bonus problem: the single number \(u_1v_2-u_2v_1\)) is a genuinely different, related convention — a signed area, not a vector — and it's really just the \(\mathbf k\)-component of the true 3D cross product once both vectors are extended into the xy-plane with a zero \(z\). Don't treat the two as interchangeable notation for the same operation.

Try it yourself

Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\langle1,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,1,0\rangle\).

Practice

  1. Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\langle4,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,1,0\rangle\).
    Cross product is \(\langle0,0,4\rangle\), magnitude \(4\).
  2. Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\langle1,1,0\rangle\) and \(\langle1,0,1\rangle\) (reuse Chapter 7's cross product, \(\langle1,-1,-1\rangle\)).
    \(|\langle1,-1,-1\rangle|=\sqrt{1+1+1}=\sqrt3\)
  3. Confirm \(\langle2,0,0\rangle\times\langle0,3,0\rangle=\langle0,0,6\rangle\) (worked example) is perpendicular to both \(\langle2,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,3,0\rangle\), using dot products.
    \(\langle0,0,6\rangle\cdot\langle2,0,0\rangle=0\); \(\langle0,0,6\rangle\cdot\langle0,3,0\rangle=0\). Both zero, confirming equation 9.8.2.
  4. Two vectors are perpendicular, with \(|\vec u|=5\) and \(|\vec v|=2\). Find \(|\vec u\times\vec v|\).
    \(\sin90°=1\), so \(|\vec u\times\vec v| = 5(2)(1)=10\).
  5. Two vectors point in exactly the same direction (\(\theta=0°\)). What is \(|\vec u\times\vec v|\), and why does that match the parallelogram picture?
    \(\sin0°=0\), so \(|\vec u\times\vec v|=0\). Two parallel vectors span a degenerate "parallelogram" with zero height — zero area, matching the formula.
  6. A triangular mesh face has edge vectors \(\langle3,0,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,4,0\rangle\) from a shared vertex. Find the triangle's area.
    Cross product \(=\langle0,0,12\rangle\), magnitude \(12\) (parallelogram area). Triangle area \(=\frac12(12)=6\).
  7. bonus — Floor-polygon edge vectors are \(e_1=\langle1,0,0\rangle\) and \(e_2=\langle0,0,1\rangle\), with "up" defined as \(+y\). Compute \(e_1\times e_2\). Does it point up or down — and what would fix it if it's the wrong way?
    \(e_1\times e_2=\langle0,-1,0\rangle\) — points straight down (\(-y\)), the wrong way for an upward-facing floor normal. Swapping the order, \(e_2\times e_1\), flips the sign (Chapter 7's anticommutativity) and points it up instead.
  8. bonus — Confirm \(|\mathbf i\times\mathbf j|\), \(|\mathbf j\times\mathbf k|\), and \(|\mathbf k\times\mathbf i|\) are each exactly \(1\). Why does that make sense?
    Each pair is two perpendicular unit vectors, so equation 9.8.1 gives \(1\cdot1\cdot\sin90° = 1\) every time — geometrically, each spans a literal \(1\times1\) unit square.
  9. bonus — Explain why the "2D cross product" \(u_1v_2-u_2v_1\) (Chapter 7) can be read as a signed area, using equation 9.8.1.
    When both vectors lie in the xy-plane, the true 3D cross product's only nonzero component is that same number (the \(\mathbf k\)-component); its absolute value is the parallelogram's area by equation 9.8.1, and its sign encodes orientation (positive when \(\vec v\) is counterclockwise from \(\vec u\)).
  10. bonus — Could \(|\vec u\times\vec v|\) ever come out negative? Explain using equation 9.8.1.
    No. \(|\vec u|\) and \(|\vec v|\) are never negative, and \(\theta\) (the angle between two vectors) is always taken in \([0°,180°]\), where \(\sin\theta\ge0\) throughout. The product of three non-negative numbers can't be negative — and a magnitude couldn't be negative anyway.
  11. challenge — A triangular mesh face has vertices \(A=(0,0,0)\), \(B=(4,0,0)\), \(C=(0,3,0)\). Using edge vectors from \(A\), find a normal vector to the triangle and the triangle's area.
    \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\langle4,0,0\rangle\), \(\overrightarrow{AC}=\langle0,3,0\rangle\). \(\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=\langle0,0,12\rangle\) — a normal pointing along \(+z\). Parallelogram area \(=|\langle0,0,12\rangle|=12\), so the triangle's area is \(6\).
