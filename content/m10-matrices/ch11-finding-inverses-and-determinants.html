Finding Inverses & Determinants

Chapter 10 handed you pairs of matrices and asked you to check they were inverses. This chapter answers the harder question it left open: given just one matrix, how do you find its inverse — and how do you know in advance whether one even exists?

Why this matters

Chapter 10 previewed that some square matrices are "singular" — no inverse exists — without giving a way to tell which ones. The determinant is that test: a single number computed from a matrix's entries that is zero exactly when the matrix is singular. For \(2\times2\) matrices, the determinant also plugs directly into a shortcut formula for the inverse itself, closing the loop this whole module has been building toward since Chapter 1.

Definition — Determinant of a 2×2 matrix
\[ \det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad-bc \]
(10.11.1)

Multiply the main diagonal (\(a,d\)), multiply the anti-diagonal (\(b,c\)), subtract. For a \(3\times3\) matrix, the determinant is computed by cofactor expansion: pick a row (or column), and for each entry, multiply it by the determinant of the smaller \(2\times2\) matrix left after deleting that entry's row and column, alternating signs \((+,-,+,\ldots)\) across the row. That full \(3\times3\) machinery is beyond what this chapter needs — the \(2\times2\) case below is what the rest of this chapter builds on.

Rule — 2×2 inverse shortcut
\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}, \quad A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} \]
(10.11.2)

Swap the two diagonal entries (\(a\) and \(d\) trade places), negate the two off-diagonal entries (\(b\) and \(c\)), then scale everything by \(1/\det(A)\). This is a genuine shortcut — no row reduction needed for the \(2\times2\) case.

Theorem — Invertibility test

A square matrix \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(\det(A) \neq 0\). This is precisely why the formula above breaks down (division by zero) exactly when a matrix is singular — the determinant isn't just a related fact, it's the actual gatekeeper Chapter 10 promised.

Worked example — finding, then verifying, an inverse

Find \(A^{-1}\) for \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) (the same matrix Chapter 10 handed you the inverse of, ready-made).

Step 1 — compute the determinant

\(\det(A) = 2(1)-1(1) = 1\). Nonzero, so \(A\) is invertible.

Step 2 — apply the shortcut formula: swap a,d; negate b,c

\(A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\)

Step 3 — verify by multiplying back to I

\(AA^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}2(1)+1(-1)&2(-1)+1(2)\\1(1)+1(-1)&1(-1)+1(2)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix} = I\) ✓

Result

\(A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\) — the exact matrix Chapter 10 asked you to check, now derived from scratch instead of handed to you.

Common mistake

Forgetting which entries move where in the \(2\times2\) inverse formula — the pattern (swap \(a\) and \(d\), negate \(b\) and \(c\)) is easy to misremember under pressure as "negate everything" or "swap everything." For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\), an incorrect "negate everything" attempt would give \(\begin{bmatrix}-2&-1\\-1&-1\end{bmatrix}\) (scaled), which multiplied by \(A\) does not give \(I\) — check: \(2(-2)+1(-1)=-5\neq1\). The correct swap-and-negate pattern is worth memorizing as a single visual: picture the \(2\times2\) box, trade the two corners on the main diagonal, flip the sign of the other two.

Try it yourself

Find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\).

Practice

  1. Find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{bmatrix}\)
    \(3(4)-2(1)=10\)
  2. Find \(\det\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\)
    \(5(1)-3(2)=-1\)
  3. Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\), and verify by multiplying back to I.
    \(\det=3(1)-1(2)=1\). \(A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-2&3\end{bmatrix}\). Check: \(\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-2&3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3-2&-3+3\\2-2&-2+3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) ✓
  4. Is \(\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\) invertible? Explain using the determinant.
    \(\det=4(1)-2(2)=0\). Not invertible — singular. (Notice row 2 is exactly half of row 1, matching Chapter 10's singular example pattern.)
  5. Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\)
    \(\det=1(3)-2(1)=1\). \(A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\)
  6. Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\), and verify it matches the diagonal-reciprocal shortcut from Chapter 10.
    \(\det=2(5)-0(0)=10\). \(A^{-1}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12&0\\0&\tfrac15\end{bmatrix}\) — matches Chapter 10's observation that a diagonal matrix's inverse just takes the reciprocal of each diagonal entry.
  7. bonus — Is \(\begin{bmatrix}6&3\\-2&-1\end{bmatrix}\) singular? Show why.
    \(\det=6(-1)-3(-2)=-6+6=0\). Singular — no inverse exists.
  8. bonus — Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}\)
    \(\det=0(0)-1(-1)=1\). \(A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\)
  9. bonus — Find the inverse of \(\begin{bmatrix}4&7\\2&6\end{bmatrix}\)
    \(\det=4(6)-7(2)=24-14=10\). \(A^{-1}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}6&-7\\-2&4\end{bmatrix}\)
  10. bonus — Without fully computing the inverse, determine whether \(\begin{bmatrix}k&2\\4&8\end{bmatrix}\) is invertible when \(k=4\), and when \(k=5\).
    \(\det = 8k-8\). At \(k=4\): \(8(4)-8=24\neq0\), invertible. At \(k=5\): \(8(5)-8=32\neq0\), also invertible — this matrix is only singular at \(k=1\) (where \(8k-8=0\)).
  11. challenge — Find the inverse of \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\5&-2\end{bmatrix}\), verify it fully by computing both \(AA^{-1}\) and \(A^{-1}A\), and then use it to solve \(A\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\).
    \(\det=3(-2)-(-1)(5)=-6+5=-1\). \(A^{-1}=\frac{1}{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\-5&3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\5&-3\end{bmatrix}\). Check \(AA^{-1}\): \(\begin{bmatrix}3(2)+(-1)(5)&3(-1)+(-1)(-3)\\5(2)+(-2)(5)&5(-1)+(-2)(-3)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) ✓. Check \(A^{-1}A\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2(3)+(-1)(5)&2(-1)+(-1)(-2)\\5(3)+(-3)(5)&5(-1)+(-3)(-2)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) ✓. Solve: \(\mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2-1\\5-3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\). Check: \(3(1)-1(2)=1\) ✓, \(5(1)-2(2)=1\) ✓.
