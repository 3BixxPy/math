Elementary Row Operations & Row Echelon Form

Chapter 5 turned a system into a matrix. Now: the exact moves you're allowed to make on that matrix without changing what system it represents, and the tidy target shape those moves are aimed at.

Why this matters

Every legal move on an augmented matrix corresponds to a legal move on the original equations — the three operations below are just Chapter 1's "add a multiple of one equation to another" and "swap which equation you look at first," relabeled as row moves. What's new is the restriction: only these three moves are allowed, which is exactly what makes the procedure mechanical rather than clever. Chapter 7 is nothing more than applying these three moves in a fixed order.

Rule — The three elementary row operations
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{1. Swap: } R_i \leftrightarrow R_j \\ &\text{2. Scale: } R_i \to k \cdot R_i \quad (k \neq 0) \\ &\text{3. Add a multiple: } R_i \to R_i + k \cdot R_j \end{aligned} \]
(10.6.1)

These are the only operations allowed. Each one is reversible (swap back; scale by \(1/k\); subtract the same multiple back off), and each corresponds to a valid algebraic step on the original equations (reordering equations doesn't change their shared solution; multiplying both sides of one equation by a nonzero number doesn't change its solutions; adding a multiple of one true equation to another produces another true equation). Because every step is reversible and solution-preserving, none of them can ever introduce a new solution or lose a real one — the row-reduced matrix represents exactly the same system you started with.

Definition — Row echelon form (REF)

A matrix is in row echelon form when: (1) each row's leading (leftmost nonzero) entry sits strictly to the right of the leading entry in the row above it, and (2) any all-zero rows are at the bottom. The result is a "staircase" of leading entries stepping down and to the right:

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{2} & 3 & -1 & 5 \\ 0 & \boxed{4} & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{1} & 3 \end{array}\right] \]
(10.6.2)

Boxed entries are the leading entries — each one strictly right of the one above, forming the staircase shape row echelon form is named for.

Worked example

Apply \(R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1\) to the augmented matrix for x + 2y = 5, 2x + 5y = 12.

Step 1 — write the starting augmented matrix

\(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&2&5\\2&5&12\end{array}\right]\) — the goal is to zero out the leading entry of row 2, using row 1.

Step 2 — compute the new row 2, entry by entry, including the constant column

\(R_2 - 2R_1 = [2-2(1),\ 5-2(2)\ |\ 12-2(5)] = [0,\ 1\ |\ 2]\)

Result

\(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&2&5\\0&1&2\end{array}\right]\) — now in row echelon form (row 2's leading entry, column 2, is right of row 1's leading entry, column 1). Translating row 2 back: \(y=2\); back-substituting into row 1 gives \(x+2(2)=5\Rightarrow x=1\).

Common mistake

Applying a row operation to only the coefficient columns and forgetting the augmented (constants) column. In the worked example, \(R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1\) must subtract \(2\times\) row 1's entire row, including the \(5\) on the far side of the bar — someone in a hurry sometimes updates \([2,5]\to[0,1]\) but leaves the constant \(12\) untouched, silently changing the matrix into a system with a different solution. The bar is a label, not a wall (Chapter 5) — every row operation is a full-row operation, no exceptions.

Try it yourself

What row operation turns \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}\) into \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) (second row)?

Practice

  1. Apply \(R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&1&4\\3&2&9\end{array}\right]\)
    New row 2: \([3-3(1),\ 2-3(1)\ |\ 9-3(4)]=[0,-1|-3]\). Result: \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&1&4\\0&-1&-3\end{array}\right]\)
  2. Apply \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}0&5&10\\1&2&3\end{array}\right]\)
    \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&2&3\\0&5&10\end{array}\right]\) — swapped so row 1 has a nonzero leading entry.
  3. Apply \(R_2 \to \frac{1}{2}R_2\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&0&5\\0&2&6\end{array}\right]\)
    \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&0&5\\0&1&3\end{array}\right]\)
  4. Is \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&3&4\\0&0&5&1\\0&1&0&2\end{array}\right]\) in row echelon form? Why or why not.
    No — row 3's leading entry is in column 2, but row 2's leading entry is in column 3. Row 3's staircase step must be strictly to the right of row 2's, and it isn't (it's to the left).
  5. Apply \(R_3 \to R_3 - R_1\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\0&2&1&4\\1&0&1&3\end{array}\right]\)
    New row 3: \([1-1,0-1,1-1\ |\ 3-6]=[0,-1,0\ |\ -3]\). Result: \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\0&2&1&4\\0&-1&0&-3\end{array}\right]\)
  6. Is \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&3&2\\0&0&0\end{array}\right]\) in row echelon form?
    Yes — the all-zero row is at the bottom, and the only nonzero row's leading entry needs no row below it to compare against.
  7. bonus — Apply \(R_2 \to R_2 + 4R_1\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&-1&2\\-4&3&1\end{array}\right]\)
    New row 2: \([-4+4(1),\ 3+4(-1)\ |\ 1+4(2)]=[0,-1|9]\). Result: \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&-1&2\\0&-1&9\end{array}\right]\)
  8. bonus — What single row operation would you apply first to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}0&2&4\\3&1&5\end{array}\right]\) to begin reducing it, and why?
    \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\) — row 1 currently has a zero leading entry, which blocks using it to eliminate below; swapping gives row 1 a nonzero leading entry to work with.
  9. bonus — Is \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}2&1&0&3\\0&1&1&2\\0&0&0&0\end{array}\right]\) in row echelon form?
    Yes — leading entries step right (column 1, then column 2), and the zero row sits at the bottom.
  10. bonus — Apply \(R_2 \to R_2 - \frac{1}{2}R_1\) to \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}2&4&6\\1&5&7\end{array}\right]\)
    New row 2: \([1-\tfrac12(2),\ 5-\tfrac12(4)\ |\ 7-\tfrac12(6)]=[0,3|4]\). Result: \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}2&4&6\\0&3&4\end{array}\right]\)
  11. challenge — A matrix has 4 rows. Row 1's leading entry is in column 1, row 2's in column 2, row 3's in column 2 as well, and row 4 is all zeros. Is this matrix in row echelon form? If not, name specifically which condition fails.
    No. Row 3's leading entry (column 2) is not strictly to the right of row 2's leading entry (also column 2) — the staircase condition requires strictly right, and tying in the same column violates it, even though the all-zero-rows-at-the-bottom condition is satisfied.
