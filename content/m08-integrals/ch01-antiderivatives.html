Antiderivatives

Modules 6 and 7 spent twenty-eight chapters building one machine: given a function, produce its derivative. This chapter runs that machine backward for the first time — given a derivative, can you recover the function it came from?

Why this matters

Reverse operations show up everywhere in math, and each one has needed its own careful treatment: squaring is easy, square-rooting needs a ± and a domain restriction; multiplying is easy, dividing needs the divisor to be nonzero. Differentiation is no different. Running it backward isn't automatic just because you know the forward rules cold — it's a genuinely new operation, with its own name, its own subtleties, and (starting next chapter) its own rulebook. This chapter is just the definition and the single most important subtlety it hides.

Definition — Antiderivative

A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval if

\[ F'(x) = f(x) \]

for every \(x\) in that interval. It's exactly the reverse of Module 6's whole project: there, you were handed \(F\) and asked for \(F'\); here, you're handed \(f\) and asked to find some \(F\) whose derivative is it.

Theorem — The General Antiderivative
\[ F'(x) = G'(x) = f(x) \ \Longrightarrow\ G(x) = F(x) + C \]
(8.1.1)

If \(F\) is one antiderivative of \(f\), every antiderivative of \(f\) on that interval has this exact form — some constant \(C\) added to \(F\). The "+C" is not optional bookkeeping tacked on out of habit: a genuinely different constant gives a genuinely different, still-valid antiderivative, because constants vanish under differentiation (Module 6's constant rule). \(x^3\), \(x^3+7\), and \(x^3-\pi\) are three different functions that all have the exact same derivative, \(3x^2\) — so "the" antiderivative of \(3x^2\) doesn't exist as a single function; only the whole family \(x^3+C\) does.

This family gets its own compact notation, written \(\int f(x)\,dx\), read "the integral of \(f\) with respect to \(x\)." For now treat it purely as shorthand for "the general antiderivative of \(f\)" — nothing more is being claimed about it yet.

Worked example

Find the general antiderivative of f(x) = x², then verify the answer by differentiating it back.

Step 1 — guess a candidate by pattern-matching against the power rule

Module 6's power rule says \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^3] = 3x^2\) — close, but it has an extra factor of 3 we don't want. Dividing the candidate by that same 3 should cancel it: try \(F(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{3}\).

Step 2 — verify by differentiating the candidate

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right] = \dfrac{3x^2}{3} = x^2\) ✓ — matches \(f(x)\) exactly.

Result — the general antiderivative

By (8.1.1), every antiderivative of \(x^2\) has the form \(\dfrac{x^3}{3}+C\). Written with integral notation: \(\displaystyle\int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3}+C\).

Common mistake

Two versions of the same slip. First: dropping the "+C" entirely — writing \(x^3/3\) as "the" answer, as if it were unique. Second, a reversed-power-rule error: dividing by the original exponent instead of the new one. For \(f(x)=x^2\), that would mean writing \(x^3/2\) (dividing by the old exponent, 2) instead of the correct \(x^3/3\) (dividing by the new exponent, 3). Always check by differentiating your answer back — if you don't recover the original \(f(x)\) exactly, something's wrong, and the check catches both mistakes instantly.

Practice

  1. Find the general antiderivative of f(x) = x⁵.
    \(\dfrac{x^6}{6}+C\)
  2. Find the general antiderivative of the constant function f(x) = 7.
    \(7x+C\) — check: \(\frac{d}{dx}[7x+C]=7\) ✓
  3. Is F(x) = x⁴/4 + C an antiderivative of f(x) = x³? Verify.
    Yes — \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^4/4]=x^3\) ✓, and the "+C" survives differentiation as 0 either way.
  4. Find the general antiderivative of f(x) = 1 (the constant function 1).
    \(x+C\)
  5. True or false: x³ + 5 and x³ − 2 are both valid antiderivatives of f(x) = 3x².
    True — they differ only by a constant (7), and both differentiate to \(3x^2\) (8.1.1).
  6. Find the general antiderivative of f(x) = x.
    \(\dfrac{x^2}{2}+C\)
  7. bonus — If F'(x) = 0 for every x, what can you conclude about F?
    \(F\) must be a constant function — \(F(x)=C\) for some fixed \(C\), since 0 is the derivative of every constant and (8.1.1) says that's the whole family.
  8. bonus — Find the general antiderivative of f(x) = x¹⁰.
    \(\dfrac{x^{11}}{11}+C\)
  9. bonus — A student says the general antiderivative of f(x) = 4x³ is x⁴. What's incomplete about that answer?
    It's missing the "+C." \(x^4\) alone is only one antiderivative; \(x^4+9\), \(x^4-\sqrt2\), and infinitely many others all differentiate to \(4x^3\) as well, by (8.1.1).
  10. bonus — Is G(x) = x³/3 + 5 an antiderivative of f(x) = x²?
    Yes — \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^3/3+5]=x^2+0=x^2\) ✓. The "+5" is just one particular choice of the "+C."
  11. challenge — Try differentiating F(x) = x³/3 + x and confirm it does not equal x². Explain, using Module 6's constant rule, exactly why "+C" is required to be a genuine constant and not a term like "+x."
    \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^3/3+x] = x^2+1 \neq x^2\) — off by 1, because \(\frac{d}{dx}[x]=1\), not 0. Module 6's constant rule (\(\frac{d}{dx}[C]=0\)) only kills terms with no \(x\) in them. "+C" works precisely because it contributes nothing to the derivative; any term that still depends on \(x\) — even something as simple as "+x" — changes the derivative and breaks the equality, so it can never be substituted for "+C."
