u-Substitution with Definite Integrals

Same technique as Chapter 9, one extra bookkeeping step: the bounds themselves have to switch from x-values to u-values.

Why this matters

A definite integral's bounds, a and b, are x-values — but once everything else in the integral has been rewritten in terms of u, those bounds are now describing the wrong variable. Two approaches both work: convert the bounds to u-values right away and never look back at x, or find the antiderivative in terms of u, substitute back to x, and evaluate at the original bounds. Both are valid — but converting the bounds is cleaner, since it means the entire rest of the problem stays in u, with no substitution-back step at all.

Method — Converting the Bounds
  1. Choose u = g(x) and compute du, exactly as in Chapter 9.
  2. Convert both bounds: plug the original lower bound into g(x) to get the new lower u-bound, and the original upper bound to get the new upper u-bound.
  3. Rewrite the whole integral — integrand, du, and bounds — entirely in u.
  4. Evaluate directly in u via FTC2 (Chapter 7) — no return trip to x needed.
Worked example

Evaluate ∫[0,1] 2x(x²+1)³ dx.

Step 1 — substitute

\(u=x^2+1\), \(du=2x\,dx\) — matches the integrand exactly.

Step 2 — convert the bounds

\(x=0 \Rightarrow u=0^2+1=1\). \(x=1 \Rightarrow u=1^2+1=2\). New bounds: \(u=1\) to \(u=2\).

Step 3 — rewrite entirely in u

\(\displaystyle\int_1^2 u^3\,du\)

Result — evaluate directly in u, no return to x needed

\(\Big[\dfrac{u^4}{4}\Big]_1^2 = \dfrac{16}{4}-\dfrac14 = 4-0.25 = \dfrac{15}{4}\)

Common mistake

Two distinct errors, both worth naming. First — and by far the more serious one: forgetting to convert the bounds at all, and plugging the original x-bounds straight into the u-antiderivative. In the worked example, that would mean evaluating \(\big[u^4/4\big]\) at \(u=0\) and \(u=1\) instead of the correct \(u=1\) and \(u=2\) — simply wrong, not just messy. Second — harmless, just inefficient: converting the substitution correctly, but then switching the antiderivative back to x before evaluating at the original x-bounds anyway. That still gives the right answer, it's just the "long way" this chapter's Method box exists to skip.

Practice

  1. Evaluate ∫[0,1] 3x²(x³+2)² dx.
    \(u=x^3+2\), bounds \(2\to3\): \(\big[u^3/3\big]_2^3=9-8/3=19/3\)
  2. Evaluate ∫[0,2] x(x²+1)² dx.
    \(u=x^2+1,\ x\,dx=du/2\), bounds \(1\to5\): \(\frac12\big[u^3/3\big]_1^5=\frac12\left(\frac{125}{3}-\frac13\right)=\frac{62}{3}\)
  3. Evaluate ∫[0,π/2] cos(x)sin²(x) dx.
    \(u=\sin x\), bounds \(0\to1\): \(\big[u^3/3\big]_0^1=1/3\)
  4. Evaluate ∫[0,1] 2xeˣ² dx.
    \(u=x^2\), bounds \(0\to1\): \(\big[e^u\big]_0^1=e-1\)
  5. Evaluate ∫[1,2] x²(x³−1) dx.
    \(u=x^3-1,\ x^2dx=du/3\), bounds \(0\to7\): \(\frac13\big[u^2/2\big]_0^7=\frac{49}{6}\)
  6. For ∫[0,3] f(g(x))g′(x) dx with u=g(x), state in words (not symbols) how to find the new lower and upper bounds.
    Plug the original x-bounds into \(g(x)\) itself: the new lower bound is \(g(\text{lower }x)\), the new upper bound is \(g(\text{upper }x)\).
  7. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,1] x(1−x²)³ dx. (Notice what happens to the bound order.)
    \(u=1-x^2,\ x\,dx=-du/2\). Bounds: \(x=0\Rightarrow u=1\); \(x=1\Rightarrow u=0\) — the bounds flip order. \(\displaystyle-\frac12\int_1^0u^3du=\frac12\int_0^1u^3du=\frac12\cdot\frac14=\frac18\)
  8. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,4] √(2x+1) dx.
    \(u=2x+1,\ dx=du/2\), bounds \(1\to9\): \(\frac12\big[\frac23u^{3/2}\big]_1^9=\frac13(27-1)=\frac{26}{3}\)
  9. bonus — True or false: after converting both the integrand and the bounds to u, the final numeric answer must be converted back to x before it's valid.
    False — a definite integral's value is just a number. Once the bounds are in \(u\), evaluating entirely in \(u\) gives the correct final number directly; there's nothing left to convert back.
  10. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,1] 6x(x²+2)² dx two ways: (a) convert the bounds and stay in u, (b) find the antiderivative in x first, then evaluate at the original bounds. Confirm both agree.
    \(u=x^2+2,\ du=2x\,dx\), so \(6x\,dx=3\,du\). (a) Bounds \(2\to3\): \(3\displaystyle\int_2^3u^2du=\big[u^3\big]_2^3=27-8=19\). (b) Antiderivative in x: \(3\cdot\frac{u^3}{3}=(x^2+2)^3+C\); evaluate at the original bounds \(0\to1\): \(3^3-2^3=27-8=19\). Both agree: \(19\).
  11. challenge — Evaluate ∫[0,3] x/√(x²+16) dx via u-substitution with bound conversion.
    \(u=x^2+16,\ x\,dx=du/2\), bounds \(16\to25\): \(\displaystyle\frac12\int_{16}^{25}u^{-1/2}du=\Big[\sqrt u\Big]_{16}^{25}=5-4=1\)
