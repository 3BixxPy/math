Chapter 9 · concept:u-substitution

u-Substitution — Reversing the Chain Rule

Every rule so far reverses one specific differentiation rule directly, term by term. This one reverses Module 6's most tangled rule — the chain rule — and it needs an actual technique, not just a lookup table.

Why this matters

An integrand like 2x(x²+1)⁵ isn't a sum, and it isn't a bare power — it's the result of a composition, exactly Module 6 Chapter 9's f(g(x)) shape, already differentiated once. Reversing a chain-rule result can't work term by term the way Chapter 2's rules did, because a composed function's pieces are tangled together, not sitting side by side. What's needed is a way to untangle it back into a simple power-rule integral — that's exactly what substitution does.

Method — u-Substitution

If F is an antiderivative of f, the chain rule says d/dx[F(g(x))] = f(g(x))·g′(x) — so running that backward:

\[ \int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = F(g(x)) + C \]

  1. Spot the "inside function" — call it u = g(x).
  2. Compute du = g′(x)dx.
  3. Rewrite the entire integral in terms of u and du only — no x should remain.
  4. Integrate in terms of u (Chapters 1–2's ordinary rules now apply).
  5. Substitute u = g(x) back, to return the answer in terms of x.
Worked example

Evaluate ∫2x(x²+1)⁵ dx.

Step 1 — spot the inside function

Let \(u = x^2+1\).

Step 2 — compute du

\(du = 2x\,dx\) — and \(2x\,dx\) is exactly what's sitting next to \((x^2+1)^5\) in the original integral.

Step 3 — rewrite fully in terms of u

\(\displaystyle\int (x^2+1)^5 \cdot 2x\,dx = \int u^5\,du\)

Step 4 — integrate (Chapter 2's power rule)

\(\displaystyle\int u^5\,du = \frac{u^6}{6}+C\)

Result — substitute back

\(\dfrac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}+C\). Verify by the chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{(x^2+1)^6}{6}\right] = \frac{6(x^2+1)^5\cdot2x}{6} = 2x(x^2+1)^5\) ✓ — exactly the original integrand.

Common mistake

Forgetting to actually solve for the leftover variable in terms of du — dropping a needed constant multiple along the way. For ∫x(x²+1)⁵ dx (no leading 2 this time), let u = x²+1, so du = 2x dx, meaning x dx = du/2 — not just du. Forgetting that "/2" and writing ∫u⁵ du directly gives an answer twice too large. The fix: after computing du, always solve it for whatever's actually sitting in the integral (here, x dx) before substituting — don't assume the leftover piece matches du exactly just because it looks similar.

    \(u=x^3\): \(-\cos(x^3)+C\)

    \(u=x^5,\ du=5x^4dx\) — matches the integrand exactly, no constant adjustment needed.
