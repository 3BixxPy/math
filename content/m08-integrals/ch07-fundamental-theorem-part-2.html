Chapter 7 · concept:ftc-part-2

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

Part 1 showed that differentiation undoes accumulation. Part 2 is the payoff for actual computation — it turns Chapter 4's intractable limit-of-a-sum into ordinary antiderivative arithmetic.

Why this matters

Chapter 4 wrote down the exact definition of a definite integral — and then admitted evaluating it by hand, term by term, is painful even for a simple polynomial. Part 2 sidesteps that entirely. It doesn't require re-deriving anything new about limits; it leans on Part 1, and on Chapters 1–2's antiderivative rules, to turn "evaluate this limit of a sum" into "find an antiderivative, then subtract."

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
(8.7.1)

where F is any antiderivative of f. This is the workhorse formula for the rest of the module. A convenient shorthand for "plug in the top, plug in the bottom, subtract" gets its own bracket notation, used from here on:

\[ \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b) - F(a) \]

Worked example

Evaluate ∫[0,2] x² dx via FTC2, and compare it to Chapter 3's rectangle estimates for the exact same function and interval.

Step 1 — find an antiderivative (Chapter 2's power rule)

\(F(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{3}\) is one antiderivative of \(x^2\).

Step 2 — apply (8.7.1)

\(\displaystyle\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = \Big[\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_0^2 = \frac{8}{3} - 0 = \frac{8}{3} \approx 2.667\)

Result — checking against Chapter 3

Chapter 3's 4-rectangle estimates for this exact function and interval were \(1.75\) (left) and \(3.75\) (right) — they bracketed \(1.75 \le \text{true area} \le 3.75\), and \(8/3\approx2.667\) sits comfortably inside that bracket. What Chapter 3 could only estimate, FTC2 computes exactly, with no limit taken by hand at all.

Common mistake

Carrying a "+C" into a definite-integral evaluation, out of habit from Chapters 1–2's indefinite integrals. It's harmless — but pointless, since it always cancels: using \(F(x)+C\) in (8.7.1) gives \(\big[F(b)+C\big]-\big[F(a)+C\big] = F(b)-F(a)\), the same \(C\) subtracting itself away every time. A definite integral's value never depends on which antiderivative you pick, so just drop the "+C" for definite integrals — its absence here (versus its required presence in Chapters 1–2) is exactly the kind of detail that trips learners up when first switching between the two.

Practice

  1. Evaluate ∫[0,1] x³ dx.
    \(\big[x^4/4\big]_0^1 = 1/4\)
  2. Evaluate ∫[1,3] 2x dx.
    \(\big[x^2\big]_1^3 = 9-1=8\)
  3. Evaluate ∫[π/2,π] cos(x) dx.
    \(\big[\sin x\big]_{\pi/2}^{\pi} = 0-1=-1\)
  4. Evaluate ∫[−1,1] x² dx.
    \(\big[x^3/3\big]_{-1}^{1} = 1/3-(-1/3)=2/3\)
  5. Evaluate ∫[0,2] (3x² − 1) dx.
    \(\big[x^3-x\big]_0^2 = (8-2)-0=6\)
  6. Evaluate ∫[1,2] (1/x) dx.
    \(\big[\ln|x|\big]_1^2 = \ln2-\ln1=\ln2\)
  7. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,1] eˣ dx.
    \(e-1\)
  8. bonus — Evaluate ∫[−2,2] x³ dx.
    \(\big[x^4/4\big]_{-2}^{2}=4-4=0\) — an odd function on a symmetric interval (Chapter 5's bonus idea, confirmed again).
  9. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,4] √x dx.
    Rewrite as \(x^{1/2}\): \(\big[\frac23 x^{3/2}\big]_0^4 = \frac23(8)-0=\frac{16}{3}\)
  10. bonus — Evaluate ∫[2,5] 4 dx (a constant function) using FTC2, and check it matches simple rectangle reasoning.
    \(\big[4x\big]_2^5=20-8=12\), matching a \(3\times4\) rectangle exactly.
  11. challenge — Evaluate ∫[0,2] (x² − 2x) dx. Then, using Chapter 5's signed-area idea, explain why a negative result makes sense here.
    \(\big[x^3/3-x^2\big]_0^2 = (8/3-4)-0=-4/3\). \(f(x)=x^2-2x=x(x-2)\) is \(\le0\) for every \(x\) in \([0,2]\) (between its two roots), so the entire region lies below the x-axis — a negative integral is exactly what (8.5.3) predicts.
