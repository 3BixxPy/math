Chapter 14 · concept:net-change-theorem

Net Change & Accumulation

FTC2, restated for interpretation rather than computation: integrating a rate of change gives the total net change — displacement from velocity, and the running theme for the rest of applied calculus.

Why this matters

f′ measures how fast f is changing at each instant; integrating it should, intuitively, add all those instantaneous changes back up into one total change. FTC2 already says exactly this — this chapter doesn't add new mathematics, it names the interpretation explicitly, because "integrating a rate gives total change" is the single most common way this module's tools get used in the real world.

Theorem — The Net Change Theorem
\[ \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a) \]
(8.14.1)

This is FTC2 (8.7.1) with \(F=f\) and the integrand relabeled as a derivative — same theorem, new emphasis: integrating a rate of change over [a,b] gives the total net change in the original quantity over that interval.

Worked example — net displacement vs. total distance

An object's velocity is v(t) = t − 2 (m/s) on [0, 4]. Find its net displacement, then its total distance traveled.

Step 1 — net displacement, directly via (8.14.1)

\(\displaystyle\int_0^4(t-2)\,dt = \Big[\frac{t^2}{2}-2t\Big]_0^4 = (8-8)-0 = 0\). The object ends up exactly where it started.

Step 2 — notice the velocity changes sign at t = 2

\(v(t)<0\) on \([0,2)\) (moving backward) and \(v(t)>0\) on \((2,4]\) (moving forward). Total distance needs each piece's unsigned contribution, so the interval must be split at \(t=2\).

Step 3 — integrate |v(t)| on each piece separately

On \([0,2]\): \(\displaystyle\int_0^2(2-t)\,dt = \Big[2t-\frac{t^2}{2}\Big]_0^2 = 4-2=2\). On \([2,4]\): \(\displaystyle\int_2^4(t-2)\,dt=\Big[\frac{t^2}{2}-2t\Big]_2^4=(8-8)-(2-4)=2\).

Result — a striking contrast

Net displacement: \(0\). Total distance: \(2+2=4\) meters. The object traveled a full 4 meters — 2 backward, then 2 forward — yet its net change in position is exactly zero. Net change and total distance answer genuinely different questions whenever the object reverses direction.

Common mistake

Computing net displacement when a problem actually asks for total distance, or vice versa. In the worked example above, an object traveling v(t) = t − 2 on [0,4] has net displacement 0 but total distance 4 — reporting "0 meters" as the answer to "how far did it travel" would be flatly wrong, even though the net-change theorem (8.14.1) really does give 0 for the displacement question. The fix is always the same: check whether velocity changes sign anywhere in the interval. If it never does, net displacement and total distance are the same number. If it does, total distance needs the interval split at every sign change, with each piece's contribution taken as positive.

Practice

  1. v(t) = 4 (constant, m/s) on [0, 5]. Find the net displacement.
    \(\int_0^5 4\,dt=20\) m
  2. v(t) = 3t² (m/s) on [0, 2]. Find the net displacement.
    \(\big[t^3\big]_0^2=8\) m
  3. v(t) = t − 3 (m/s) on [0, 3]. Find the net displacement.
    \(\big[t^2/2-3t\big]_0^3=(4.5-9)-0=-4.5\) m — negative, meaning the object ends up behind where it started.
  4. Using the previous problem's v(t) = t − 3 on [0, 3]: does the object's velocity change sign on this interval? What does that mean for whether total distance differs from net displacement here?
    \(v(t)=t-3\le0\) throughout \([0,3]\) (it only reaches \(0\) right at the endpoint \(t=3\)) — no sign change, so the object moves in one direction the whole time. Total distance equals \(|{-4.5}|=4.5\) m exactly, no splitting needed.
  5. A pump fills a tank at rate r(t) = 2t + 1 (liters/min) for t in [0, 10]. Find the net change in volume.
    \(\big[t^2+t\big]_0^{10}=110\) liters — the net change theorem applies to any rate, not just velocity.
  6. True or false: total distance traveled is always ≥ the absolute value of net displacement, with equality exactly when the object never reverses direction.
    True.
  7. bonus — v(t) = t² − 9 (m/s) on [0, 4]. Find the net displacement.
    \(\big[t^3/3-9t\big]_0^4=(64/3-36)-0=-\frac{44}{3}\) m
  8. bonus — Using the previous problem's v(t) = t² − 9 on [0, 4], set up (don't fully evaluate) the total-distance integral, splitting correctly at the sign change (t = 3).
    \(v<0\) on \([0,3)\), \(v>0\) on \((3,4]\): total distance \(=\displaystyle\int_0^3(9-t^2)\,dt+\int_3^4(t^2-9)\,dt\)
  9. bonus — A leak drains water from a tank at the constant rate r(t) = −5 (liters/min) for t in [0, 6]. Find the net change in volume.
    \(\int_0^6(-5)\,dt=-30\) liters — a net decrease of 30 liters.
  10. bonus — If f′(x) = 6x² and f(1) = 5, find f(4) using the net change theorem (rather than finding the general antiderivative with "+C" first).
    \(\int_1^46x^2dx=f(4)-f(1)\). \(\big[2x^3\big]_1^4=128-2=126=f(4)-5 \Rightarrow f(4)=131\).
  11. challenge — A particle's velocity is v(t) = 3 − t² (m/s) for t in [0, 3]. Find (a) the net displacement, (b) the total distance traveled, and (c) explain in one sentence why they differ.
    \(v(t)=0\) at \(t=\sqrt3\), positive before, negative after. (a) Net: \(\big[3t-t^3/3\big]_0^3=(9-9)-0=0\). (b) Distance: split at \(\sqrt3\). On \([0,\sqrt3]\): \(\big[3t-t^3/3\big]_0^{\sqrt3}=3\sqrt3-\sqrt3=2\sqrt3\). On \([\sqrt3,3]\), \(v<0\), integrate \(|v|=t^2-3\): \(\big[t^3/3-3t\big]_{\sqrt3}^{3}=(9-9)-(\sqrt3-3\sqrt3)=2\sqrt3\). Total \(=4\sqrt3\approx6.93\) m. (c) They differ because the particle reverses direction at \(t=\sqrt3\), so the backward half of the trip cancels part of the forward half in the net total, but both halves still count fully toward distance traveled.
