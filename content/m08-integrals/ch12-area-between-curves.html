Chapter 12 · concept:area-between-curves

Area Between Two Curves

Chapters 6–7 turned "area under one curve" into ordinary antiderivative arithmetic. This chapter asks the natural next question: the area trapped between two curves instead of one.

Why this matters

"Area under one curve" is really "area between that curve and the x-axis" — the x-axis was quietly playing the role of a second curve the whole time, just a flat one, y = 0. Once that's visible, letting the "bottom" curve be something other than the x-axis is barely a generalization at all: same integral, same FTC2 machinery, just a different function subtracted off at the end.

Theorem — Area Between Two Curves
\[ \text{Area} = \int_a^b \big[\text{top}(x) - \text{bottom}(x)\big]\,dx \]
(8.12.1)

where a and b are the curves' intersection points — found first, by setting the two functions equal and solving (a Module 0/1 equation-solving callback) — and "top"/"bottom" is whichever curve sits higher between those two points.

Worked example

Find the area between y = x² and y = x + 2.

Step 1 — find the intersection points

\(x^2=x+2 \Rightarrow x^2-x-2=0 \Rightarrow (x-2)(x+1)=0 \Rightarrow x=-1,\ 2\).

Step 2 — decide which curve is on top (test a point between the bounds)

At \(x=0\): \(x+2=2\) vs. \(x^2=0\) — the line is on top.

Step 3 — set up and integrate, via (8.12.1)

\(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{2}\big[(x+2)-x^2\big]dx = \Big[\frac{x^2}{2}+2x-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-1}^{2}\)

Result

At \(x=2\): \(2+4-\tfrac83=\tfrac{10}{3}\). At \(x=-1\): \(\tfrac12-2+\tfrac13=-\tfrac76\). Area \(=\tfrac{10}{3}-\left(-\tfrac76\right)=\tfrac{20}{6}+\tfrac76=\tfrac{27}{6}=\tfrac92=4.5\).

Common mistake

Two ways this goes wrong. First: integrating bottom − top instead of top − bottom, which flips every sign and produces a negative "area." Second: skipping the intersection-solving step entirely and just guessing the bounds from a rough sketch — the bounds in (8.12.1) aren't optional inputs to choose freely, they're determined by exactly where the two curves cross.

Practice

  1. Find the area between y = x² and y = 4, for x in [−2, 2].
    \(\int_{-2}^2(4-x^2)dx=\big[4x-x^3/3\big]_{-2}^2=(8-8/3)-(-8+8/3)=32/3\)
  2. Find the area enclosed between y = x and y = x³, for x in [0, 1].
    On \([0,1]\), \(x\ge x^3\): \(\int_0^1(x-x^3)dx=\big[x^2/2-x^4/4\big]_0^1=1/2-1/4=1/4\)
  3. Find the intersection points of y = x² and y = 2x (set up only).
    \(x^2=2x \Rightarrow x^2-2x=0 \Rightarrow x(x-2)=0 \Rightarrow x=0,2\)
  4. For y = 6 − x² and y = x, find the intersection points and determine which function is on top between them.
    \(6-x^2=x \Rightarrow x^2+x-6=0 \Rightarrow (x+3)(x-2)=0 \Rightarrow x=-3,2\). Test \(x=0\): \(6\) vs. \(0\) — the parabola is on top.
  5. Using the previous problem's curves and bounds, find the area between them.
    \(\int_{-3}^2\big[(6-x^2)-x\big]dx=\big[6x-x^3/3-x^2/2\big]_{-3}^{2}\). At \(2\): \(12-8/3-2=22/3\). At \(-3\): \(-18+9-4.5=-13.5=-27/2\). Area \(=22/3+27/2=\frac{44+81}{6}=\frac{125}{6}\)
  6. In one sentence, explain why the intersection points must be found before setting up the bounds of a "between two curves" integral.
    The bounds in (8.12.1) have to be exactly where the curves cross — that's what makes "top" and "bottom" well-defined and unchanging across the whole interval.
  7. bonus — Set up (don't evaluate) the integral for the area between y = x² − 4 and the x-axis, for x in [−2, 2].
    Since \(x^2-4\le0\) on \([-2,2]\), the x-axis (\(y=0\)) is on top: \(\int_{-2}^2\big[0-(x^2-4)\big]dx=\int_{-2}^2(4-x^2)dx\)
  8. bonus — Evaluate the previous problem's setup.
    \(\big[4x-x^3/3\big]_{-2}^2=(8-8/3)-(-8+8/3)=32/3\)
  9. bonus — True or false: the area between two curves can always be found by taking |∫(f−g)dx| over the whole given interval, regardless of how many times the curves cross inside it.
    False in general — only safe if the curves don't cross again inside the interval. If top/bottom switches partway through, the interval must be split at every internal crossing and each piece handled with the correct top/bottom, then summed; a single \(|{\int}(f-g)dx|\) over the whole span can silently give a too-small answer.
  10. bonus — Find the area between y = x² and y = −x² + 8.
    Intersections: \(x^2=-x^2+8 \Rightarrow x^2=4 \Rightarrow x=\pm2\). Top: \(-x^2+8\) (test \(x=0\): 8 vs. 0). \(\int_{-2}^2(8-2x^2)dx=\big[8x-\tfrac23x^3\big]_{-2}^2=(16-16/3)-(-16+16/3)=64/3\)
  11. challenge — Find the area enclosed between y = x² − 1 and y = 1 − x². State the intersection points you find first, then evaluate.
    \(x^2-1=1-x^2 \Rightarrow 2x^2=2 \Rightarrow x=\pm1\). Test \(x=0\): \(1-x^2=1\) vs. \(x^2-1=-1\) — \(1-x^2\) on top. \(\int_{-1}^1\big[(1-x^2)-(x^2-1)\big]dx=\int_{-1}^1(2-2x^2)dx=\big[2x-\tfrac23x^3\big]_{-1}^1=(2-\tfrac23)-(-2+\tfrac23)=\tfrac83\)
