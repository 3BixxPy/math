The Definite Integral — Notation & Properties

Chapter 4 defined ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx as a limit. This chapter treats it as an object worth naming its own parts, with algebraic properties it inherits directly from being built out of ordinary sums.

Why this matters

A Riemann sum is, underneath the limit, an ordinary sum of ordinary numbers — and ordinary sums split apart, factor, and combine in predictable ways. Every property in this chapter is really just one of those familiar sum behaviors, carried through the limit unchanged. The one genuine surprise is the last one: this "area" isn't always the everyday kind.

Definition — The Definite Integral

In ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx, f(x) is the integrand, a and b are the limits of integration (lower and upper), and x is the variable of integration — a placeholder; ∫ₐᵇ f(t)dt means exactly the same thing. Unlike Chapter 1–2's indefinite integral (a family of functions), a definite integral's value — when the limit in (8.4.1) exists — is a single number, not a family, and carries no "+C."

Rule — Linearity
\[ \int_a^b\!\big[f(x)\pm g(x)\big]dx = \int_a^b\! f(x)\,dx \pm \int_a^b\! g(x)\,dx \qquad \int_a^b\! c\,f(x)\,dx = c\int_a^b\! f(x)\,dx \]
(8.5.1)

Both follow directly from the fact that a Riemann sum is an ordinary sum, and ordinary sums split and factor exactly this way — nothing about the limit changes that.

Rule — Additivity Over Adjacent Intervals
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_b^c f(x)\,dx = \int_a^c f(x)\,dx \]
(8.5.2)

Splitting (or joining) the interval doesn't change the total — the piece from a to b plus the piece from b to c is exactly the piece from a to c.

Rule — Signed Area
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = (\text{area above the x-axis}) \;-\; (\text{area below the x-axis}) \]
(8.5.3)

This is the property most worth sitting with. The definite integral computes signed area, not literal (always-positive) area — regions below the x-axis subtract instead of add. It only equals the plain, everyday notion of "area between the curve and the x-axis" when \(f(x)\ge0\) throughout \([a,b]\).

Worked example — using additivity

Given ∫[0,2] f = 5 and ∫[2,5] f = 3, find ∫[0,5] f.

Step 1 — recognize the adjacent-interval pattern of (8.5.2)

\([0,2]\) and \([2,5]\) share the endpoint \(2\) and together cover \([0,5]\).

Result

\(\displaystyle\int_0^5 f = \int_0^2 f + \int_2^5 f = 5+3 = 8\).

Worked example — when the integral isn't the visual area

Let f(x) = x − 1 on [0, 2] — a line that dips below the x-axis on part of the interval. Find ∫[0,2] f(x)dx using plain triangle geometry, and compare it to the region's unsigned area.

Step 1 — split at the zero, x = 1, where the line crosses the axis

On \([0,1]\), \(f\) runs from \(-1\) up to \(0\) — a triangle below the axis, base \(1\), height \(1\), area \(0.5\), subtracting from the integral (8.5.3). On \([1,2]\), \(f\) runs from \(0\) up to \(1\) — a triangle above the axis, base \(1\), height \(1\), area \(0.5\), adding.

Result — the integral and the unsigned area disagree

\(\displaystyle\int_0^2(x-1)\,dx = -0.5+0.5 = 0\). But the region between the curve and the x-axis visibly occupies unsigned area \(0.5+0.5=1\). The integral being \(0\) doesn't mean "no region" — it means the below-axis and above-axis pieces exactly cancel.

Common mistake

Treating "the definite integral's value" and "the area of the region" as always the same question — they're the same number only when f(x) ≥ 0 throughout the interval. For the constant function f(x) = −3 on [0, 4], the integral is −12 (a rectangle's signed area, entirely below the axis) — that negative number is correct, not a sign to drop. The region's unsigned area is 12, a different number answering a different question. Read the problem carefully: "evaluate the integral" and "find the area of the region" aren't always interchangeable requests.

Try it yourself

If ∫[0,3] f = 7 and ∫[3,8] f = 2, find ∫[0,8] f:

Practice

  1. Given ∫[1,4] f = 6, find ∫[1,4] 3f(x)dx.
    \(18\) (8.5.1)
  2. Given ∫[0,5] f = 10 and ∫[0,5] g = 4, find ∫[0,5] [f(x)−g(x)]dx.
    \(6\) (8.5.1)
  3. Given ∫[2,6] f = 9 and ∫[6,10] f = −2, find ∫[2,10] f.
    \(7\) (8.5.2)
  4. Given ∫[0,10] f = 15 and ∫[0,4] f = 6, find ∫[4,10] f.
    \(15-6=9\), solving (8.5.2) for the missing piece.
  5. If f(x) ≤ 0 for every x in [a,b], what can you say about the sign of ∫[a,b] f(x)dx?
    It's \(\le0\) — entirely below-axis area only subtracts (8.5.3), never adds.
  6. True or false: ∫[a,b] f(x)dx always equals the area of the region between the curve and the x-axis.
    False — only when \(f(x)\ge0\) throughout \([a,b]\) (8.5.3).
  7. bonus — Given ∫[−2,2] f = 0 and f is odd (f(−x) = −f(x)), does this make sense? Explain briefly using symmetry.
    Yes — for an odd function on a symmetric interval, the negative-side and positive-side contributions are exact mirror images with opposite sign, so by (8.5.3) they cancel exactly.
  8. bonus — Given ∫[0,3] f = 4 and ∫[0,3] g = −1, find ∫[0,3] (2f+3g)dx.
    \(2(4)+3(-1)=8-3=5\) (8.5.1)
  9. bonus — For the constant function f(x) = −5 on [0,6], find ∫[0,6] f(x)dx using only rectangle-area reasoning.
    \(-30\) — a \(5\times6=30\) rectangle, entirely below the axis, so it subtracts (8.5.3).
  10. bonus — Given ∫[1,5] f = 8, and ∫[1,3] f = 5, find ∫[3,5] f using additivity.
    \(8-5=3\) (8.5.2)
  11. challenge — For the worked example's f(x) = x − 1 on [0,2], compute the region's unsigned area (not the integral) using the two triangles, and explain in one sentence why it differs from ∫[0,2] f(x)dx = 0.
    Unsigned area \(=0.5+0.5=1\). It differs because the integral lets the below-axis piece subtract rather than add (8.5.3), while "area" always adds both pieces as positive.
