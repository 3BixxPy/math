Chapter 15 · concept:mixed-integration-technique-selection

Mixed Integration Practice

No new tool this chapter — the final fluency pass, mixing every technique from Chapters 1–14 with no chapter label to tell you which one applies. That's the actual skill being built here.

Why this matters

Every chapter so far has practically announced its own technique in its title. Real integrals don't come labeled. The one habit worth building before the capstone: scan the integrand for an "inside function and its derivative sitting right next to it" — that's the tell for u-substitution (Chapters 9–10). If it's not there, check whether it's a plain sum of power/trig/exponential terms (Chapters 1–2, 11) instead. Getting fast at that first triage step matters more, at this point, than any single technique alone.

Worked example — spotting the substitution

Evaluate ∫eˣcos(eˣ) dx.

Step 1 — the diagnostic habit: is there an "inside function and its derivative" here?

\(e^x\) sits inside \(\cos(\cdot)\), and a loose \(e^x\) is sitting right next to it — and \(\frac{d}{dx}[e^x]=e^x\), so the "inside's derivative" is already present. That's the u-substitution tell.

Step 2 — substitute and integrate

\(u=e^x,\ du=e^x dx\): \(\displaystyle\int\cos u\,du=\sin u+C\)

Result

\(\sin(e^x)+C\)

Worked example — a definite integral needing both substitution and bound conversion

Evaluate ∫[0,1] x²eˣ³ dx.

Step 1 — spot the substitution

\(u=x^3\), \(du=3x^2dx \Rightarrow x^2dx=du/3\).

Step 2 — convert the bounds (Chapter 10)

\(x=0\Rightarrow u=0\); \(x=1\Rightarrow u=1\).

Result

\(\displaystyle\frac13\int_0^1e^u\,du=\frac13\big[e^u\big]_0^1=\frac13(e-1)\)

Common mistake

Not recognizing which technique a given integrand actually calls for. Given ∫x·sin(x²) dx, a common wrong move is guessing an antiderivative outright — writing something like −cos(x²) without the necessary correction factor, or reaching for a product-rule-flavored guess entirely by instinct — instead of first running the diagnostic habit above: is there an inside function (x²) with its derivative (2x, matching the loose x up to a constant) sitting right next to it? Yes — that's the u-substitution signal, not a guess-and-check problem.

Practice

  1. Evaluate ∫[0,2] (3x² + 2x) dx.
    No substitution needed: \(\big[x^3+x^2\big]_0^2=8+4=12\)
  2. Evaluate ∫4x³(x⁴+1)⁵ dx.
    \(u=x^4+1\): \(\dfrac{(x^4+1)^6}{6}+C\)
  3. Evaluate ∫[0,π] sin(x/2) dx.
    \(u=x/2\), bounds \(0\to\pi/2\): \(2\big[-\cos u\big]_0^{\pi/2}=2(0-(-1))=2\)
  4. Evaluate ∫e⁻ˣ dx.
    \(u=-x\): \(-e^{-x}+C\)
  5. Evaluate ∫[1,e] (1/x) dx.
    \(\big[\ln|x|\big]_1^e=\ln e-\ln1=1\)
  6. Evaluate ∫x²cos(x³+1) dx.
    \(u=x^3+1\): \(\dfrac13\sin(x^3+1)+C\)
  7. bonus — Evaluate ∫[0,4] √(2x) dx.
    \(u=2x\), bounds \(0\to8\): \(\frac12\big[\frac23u^{3/2}\big]_0^8=\frac13(8^{3/2})=\frac{16\sqrt2}{3}\)
  8. bonus — Evaluate ∫(6x² − 4) dx.
    No substitution needed — a plain polynomial: \(2x^3-4x+C\)
  9. bonus — Evaluate ∫sec²(x)eᵗᵃⁿˣ dx.
    \(u=\tan x\): \(e^{\tan x}+C\)
  10. bonus — For ∫(x+1)/(x²+2x+5) dx, identify what you'd choose for u and explain the "tell" that signals u-substitution here (don't fully solve).
    \(u=x^2+2x+5\), \(du=(2x+2)dx=2(x+1)dx\) — the numerator, \(x+1\), is exactly half of \(du\)'s coefficient: the inside function's derivative is sitting right there, up to a constant multiple.
  11. challenge — Evaluate ∫[0,1] x·2ˣ² dx. (Recall Module 3: the antiderivative of bˣ is bˣ/ln b.)
    \(u=x^2,\ x\,dx=du/2\), bounds \(0\to1\): \(\displaystyle\frac12\int_0^12^u\,du=\frac12\Big[\frac{2^u}{\ln2}\Big]_0^1=\frac12\cdot\frac{2-1}{\ln2}=\frac{1}{2\ln2}\)
