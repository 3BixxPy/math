Related Rates

Module 6 spent sixteen chapters building a machine for computing derivatives of anything — polynomials, products, quotients, compositions, even relations you can't solve for y (implicit differentiation, Ch. 12). This chapter is the first time that machine gets pointed at a genuinely new kind of question: not "what's the slope," but "how fast is this actually changing, right now, in the real world."

Why this matters

A huge number of real quantities are tangled together — a circle's radius and its area, a ladder's height and its distance from a wall, a balloon's radius and its volume. When one of those quantities changes with time, so does everything related to it, and usually at a different rate. Related rates is just implicit differentiation (Module 6, Ch. 12) with time t installed as the hidden variable everything else secretly depends on. Nothing new to learn mechanically — only a new way to read the problem.

Definition — Related rates

Suppose two quantities, say \(x\) and \(y\), are linked by an equation that holds at every instant, and both are themselves functions of time \(t\) (even though \(t\) rarely appears explicitly in the equation). Differentiating both sides of that equation with respect to \(t\) — treating \(x\) and \(y\) as \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\), and applying the chain rule to each — produces a new equation relating their rates, \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\). That's the entire method: differentiate the relationship, not the numbers.

Rule — Differentiating a relationship with respect to time

For a sliding ladder of fixed length \(L\), with base distance \(x\) and height on the wall \(y\), the Pythagorean relationship \(x^2+y^2=L^2\) (Module 0's Pythagorean theorem, now doing calculus work) holds at every instant. Differentiating both sides with respect to \(t\):

\[ 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \]
(7.1.1)

Each term used the chain rule exactly as Module 6 taught it: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}[x^2] = 2x\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt}\), because \(x\) is secretly \(x(t)\). The right side is \(0\) because \(L\) is fixed — the ladder doesn't stretch, so its derivative with respect to time is genuinely zero.

Worked example — the sliding ladder

A 10-ft ladder leans against a wall. The base slides away from the wall at 1 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the base is 6 ft from the wall?

Step 1 — set up variables and the constant relationship

Let \(x\) = base's distance from the wall, \(y\) = height of the top on the wall. The ladder length is fixed: \(x^2+y^2=10^2=100\).

Step 2 — differentiate the relationship with respect to t (before plugging in any numbers)

By (7.1.1)'s template: \(2x\dfrac{dx}{dt}+2y\dfrac{dy}{dt}=0\). This holds at every instant — it has to be set up while \(x\) and \(y\) are still symbolic.

Step 3 — now find the specific numbers at this instant

Known: \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=1\) ft/s, \(x=6\). Find \(y\) from the original (undifferentiated) relation: \(6^2+y^2=100 \Rightarrow y=8\).

Step 4 — substitute into the differentiated equation and solve for dy/dt

\(2(6)(1) + 2(8)\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 12 + 16\dfrac{dy}{dt}=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{12}{16} = -\dfrac{3}{4}\) ft/s.

Result

The top is sliding down at \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) ft/s at that instant — negative because \(y\) is decreasing. Note that this rate is not constant; it depends on \(x\) and \(y\) at each moment, which is exactly why the differentiated relationship, not a single formula, has to do the work.

Worked example — a circle's growing area

A circle's radius grows at 3 cm/s. How fast is its area growing when r = 5 cm?

Step 1 — write the relationship and differentiate with respect to t

\(A=\pi r^2\). Differentiating both sides with respect to \(t\) (chain rule: \(r\) is \(r(t)\)):

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\,\frac{dr}{dt} \]
(7.1.2)
Step 2 — substitute the given instant's values

\(\dfrac{dr}{dt}=3\), \(r=5\): \(\dfrac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi(5)(3) = 30\pi\) cm²/s.

Common mistake

Plugging in the specific numeric values before differentiating. In the ladder example, it's tempting to substitute \(x=6\) into \(x^2+y^2=100\) right away, getting \(y^2=64\), \(y=8\) — a fixed, static equation between two numbers. Differentiating 36 + 64 = 100 gives 0 = 0, which is true but useless: once \(x\) has been replaced by the specific number \(6\), the equation no longer remembers that \(x\) was ever changing, so its derivative destroys exactly the information you need. Differentiate first, while everything is still symbolic and depends on \(t\) — only substitute actual numbers into the differentiated equation, as (7.1.1) was used above.

Try it yourself

A circle's radius grows at 2 cm/s. How fast is the area growing when r = 5? (type e.g. 20pi)

Practice

  1. A circle's radius grows at 4 cm/s. Find dA/dt when r = 3.
    \(dA/dt = 2\pi r\,dr/dt = 2\pi(3)(4)=24\pi\) cm²/s
  2. A sphere's radius grows at 2 cm/s. Using V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3, find dV/dt when r = 3.
    \(dV/dt = 4\pi r^2\,dr/dt = 4\pi(9)(2)=72\pi\) cm³/s
  3. A 13-ft ladder's base slides away from the wall at 2 ft/s. Find how fast the top is sliding down when the base is 5 ft from the wall.
    \(y=12\) (from \(5^2+y^2=169\)). \(2(5)(2)+2(12)\,dy/dt=0 \Rightarrow dy/dt=-20/24=-5/6\) ft/s
  4. A cube's side grows at 0.5 cm/s. Using V=s^3, find dV/dt when s = 4.
    \(dV/dt=3s^2\,ds/dt=3(16)(0.5)=24\) cm³/s
  5. Two positive quantities satisfy xy = 100. If dx/dt = 5 at the instant x = 20, find dy/dt.
    Product rule on both sides: \(x\,dy/dt + y\,dx/dt = 0\). At \(x=20\), \(y=5\): \(20\,dy/dt + 5(5)=0 \Rightarrow dy/dt = -5/4\)
  6. A square's side grows at 3 cm/s. Find dA/dt when s = 10.
    \(A=s^2 \Rightarrow dA/dt = 2s\,ds/dt = 2(10)(3)=60\) cm²/s
  7. bonus — A balloon's volume grows at a constant 100 cm³/s. Find dr/dt when r = 5 (using V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3).
    \(dV/dt = 4\pi r^2\,dr/dt \Rightarrow 100 = 4\pi(25)\,dr/dt \Rightarrow dr/dt = 1/\pi\) cm/s
  8. bonus — Car A drives north from an intersection at 60 mph; car B drives west from the same intersection at 80 mph. Find how fast the distance between them is changing 1 hour later.
    At \(t=1\): \(x=60\), \(y=80\), \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=100\). Differentiate \(x^2+y^2=z^2\): \(2x\,dx/dt+2y\,dy/dt=2z\,dz/dt\). \(2(60)(60)+2(80)(80)=2(100)\,dz/dt \Rightarrow 7200+12800=200\,dz/dt \Rightarrow dz/dt=100\) mph
  9. bonus — A rectangle's length grows at 2 cm/s while its width shrinks at 1 cm/s. Find dA/dt when l=10, w=6 (product rule + related rates).
    \(A=lw \Rightarrow dA/dt = l'w + lw' = (2)(6)+(10)(-1)=12-10=2\) cm²/s
  10. bonus — A cone with fixed radius 3 is draining so its height shrinks at 2 cm/s. Using V=\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h, find dV/dt.
    \(r\) is fixed, so \(dV/dt = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2\,dh/dt = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi(9)(-2) = -6\pi\) cm³/s
  11. challenge — For the 10-ft sliding ladder (base sliding away at 1 ft/s), find the rate of change of the angle θ between the ladder and the ground when the base is 6 ft out. (Use cos θ = x/10.)
    Differentiate: \(-\sin\theta\,d\theta/dt = \tfrac{1}{10}\,dx/dt\). At \(x=6\), \(y=8\), so \(\sin\theta = y/10 = 0.8\). \(-0.8\,d\theta/dt = 0.1(1) \Rightarrow d\theta/dt = -0.125\) rad/s — the angle is shrinking as the ladder slides down, as expected.
