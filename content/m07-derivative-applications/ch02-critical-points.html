Chapter 2 · concept:critical-points

Critical Points

Chapter 1 pointed the derivative at rates changing over time. This chapter points it at the graph itself: the derivative's job now is to flag the handful of x-values where something interesting — a peak, a valley, a sharp corner — could be happening.

Why this matters

A function can have infinitely many x-values, but only a small, finite set of them are ever candidates for a local max or min. Critical points are exactly that shortlist — every local extremum has to be one, which turns "find the highest/lowest point" from a search over infinitely many x-values into checking a handful of specific candidates.

Definition — Critical point
\[ x=c \text{ is a critical point of } f \iff c\in\operatorname{dom}(f) \ \text{and}\ \begin{cases} f'(c)=0 \\ \text{or } f'(c)\text{ is undefined} \end{cases} \]
(7.2.1)

Two genuinely different cases, both worth checking: a horizontal tangent (\(f'(c)=0\) — the smooth, familiar case) and a point where the derivative simply doesn't exist (\(f'(c)\) undefined) even though \(f(c)\) itself is perfectly defined — typically a sharp corner or a vertical tangent line. Both are "critical" in the sense that the function's behavior could plausibly change there.

Worked example — the f′(c)=0 case

Find all critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x².

Step 1 — differentiate (power rule, Module 6)

\(f'(x)=3x^2-6x\)

Step 2 — set f′(x)=0 and solve

\(3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)=0 \Rightarrow x=0 \text{ or } x=2\)

Step 3 — check the domain condition

\(f\) is a polynomial, defined for all real \(x\), so both values qualify.

Step 4 — check for any f′(x) undefined case

\(f'(x)=3x^2-6x\) is itself a polynomial — defined everywhere, no exceptions to check. (This is generally true for any polynomial: its derivative is always another polynomial, so the "undefined" case in (7.2.1) never applies.)

Result

Critical points at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).

Worked example — the f′(c) undefined case

Find the critical points of f(x) = x^{1/3}.

Step 1 — differentiate (power rule with a fractional exponent)

\(f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{3x^{2/3}}\)

Step 2 — check x=0 specifically

At \(x=0\), the denominator \(3x^{2/3}=0\), so \(f'(0)\) is undefined — division by zero.

Step 3 — check whether f itself is defined at x=0

\(f(0)=0^{1/3}=0\), perfectly defined, so \(x=0\) is in \(f\)'s domain.

Result

\(x=0\) is a critical point — not because the derivative is zero, but because it's undefined at a point the function itself is defined. Graphically, this is a vertical tangent line (a cusp-like flattening of the S-curve at the origin), not a horizontal one.

Common mistake

Only solving f'(x)=0 and stopping there — forgetting the second half of (7.2.1) entirely. For example, f(x)=x^{2/3} has f'(x)=\tfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}, which is never zero for any real \(x\) — so a search for "where is f' zero" comes back empty, and it's tempting to conclude there are no critical points at all. But \(f'(0)\) is undefined (division by zero again) while \(f(0)=0\) is defined — \(x=0\) is a critical point, just of the second kind (this one's a genuine cusp, not just a vertical tangent). Whenever a function involves a root, a fractional exponent, or an absolute value, always check explicitly for x-values where the derivative breaks down, not just where it's zero.

Practice

  1. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x² − 6x + 5.
    \(f'(x)=2x-6=0 \Rightarrow x=3\)
  2. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x³ − 12x.
    \(f'(x)=3x^2-12=0 \Rightarrow x=\pm2\)
  3. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x⁴ − 4x³.
    \(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)=0 \Rightarrow x=0,3\)
  4. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = 1/x. (Careful with the domain.)
    \(f'(x)=-1/x^2\) is never \(0\), and while it's undefined at \(x=0\), \(f\) itself isn't defined there either — so \(x=0\) fails the domain condition in (7.2.1) and doesn't count. No critical points.
  5. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x^{2/3}.
    \(x=0\) — \(f'\) is undefined there but \(f(0)=0\) is defined (see the Common Mistake box).
  6. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x² − 2x + 1.
    \(f'(x)=2x-2=0 \Rightarrow x=1\)
  7. bonus — Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x³ − 3x + 1.
    \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0 \Rightarrow x=\pm1\)
  8. bonus — Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = \sqrt{x} on its domain x≥0.
    \(f'(x)=1/(2\sqrt{x})\) is undefined at \(x=0\), but \(x=0\) is in the domain (it's the domain's endpoint) and \(f(0)=0\) is defined — so \(x=0\) is a critical point.
  9. bonus — Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x⁴.
    \(f'(x)=4x^3=0 \Rightarrow x=0\) only
  10. bonus — Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = (x-2)^{2/3}.
    \(x=2\) — same structure as \(x^{2/3}\), just shifted: \(f'\) is undefined there, \(f(2)=0\) is defined.
  11. challenge — Find all critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x² − 9x + 5, and explicitly justify why there's no "f′ undefined" case to check for this function.
    \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9=3(x^2-2x-3)=3(x-3)(x+1)=0 \Rightarrow x=3,-1\). No undefined case applies: \(f\) is a polynomial, so \(f'\) is also a polynomial, and polynomials are defined for every real number — the second case in (7.2.1) is only ever relevant for functions involving roots, fractional powers, rational expressions, or absolute values.
