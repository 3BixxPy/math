Chapter 8 · concept:mean-value-theorem

The Mean Value Theorem

Chapters 1–7 all used the derivative to compute something. This chapter is a genuine change of pace: a theorem that guarantees a derivative equals a specific value somewhere, without ever computing where.

Why this matters

Module 6 (Ch. 1) defined average rate of change as the slope of the secant line between two points. The Mean Value Theorem is the guarantee that, for any smooth enough curve, some point along it has an instantaneous rate of change exactly matching that average — the tangent line is parallel to the secant somewhere in between. It's the theoretical bridge between "average speed over a trip" and "there was a moment going exactly that fast."

Theorem — The Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), then there exists at least one \(c\) in \((a,b)\) such that:

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \]
(7.8.1)

Geometrically: somewhere strictly between \(a\) and \(b\), the tangent line is parallel to the secant line connecting \((a,f(a))\) and \((b,f(b))\). A special case worth naming — Rolle's Theorem — is when \(f(a)=f(b)\): the right side of (7.8.1) becomes \(0\), so the guaranteed tangent is exactly horizontal.

\[ f(a)=f(b) \;\Rightarrow\; \exists\, c\in(a,b): f'(c)=0 \]
(7.8.2)
Worked example — verifying the MVT for f(x)=x² on [0,2]
Step 1 — confirm the hypotheses hold

\(f(x)=x^2\) is a polynomial — continuous everywhere and differentiable everywhere, so both conditions hold automatically on \([0,2]\).

Step 2 — compute the average rate of change (the right side of 7.8.1)

\(\dfrac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0} = \dfrac{4-0}{2}=2\)

Step 3 — solve f′(c) = that value

\(f'(x)=2x\), so \(2c=2 \Rightarrow c=1\).

Result

\(c=1\) is inside \((0,2)\), confirming the theorem: the tangent line at \(x=1\) has slope \(2\), exactly matching the secant slope between the endpoints.

Common mistake

Treating the MVT as if it told you exactly where c is, or that c is unique — it only guarantees existence, and there can be more than one valid c. Take f(x)=x³-3x on [-√3, √3]: \(f(-\sqrt3)=-3\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=0\) and \(f(\sqrt3)=3\sqrt3-3\sqrt3=0\), so \(f(a)=f(b)\) — Rolle's Theorem (7.8.2) applies. Solving \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\) gives \(x=\pm1\), and both are inside \((-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)\) — two different valid values of \(c\), not one. The theorem promises "at least one," never "exactly one," and never hands you a formula for finding it without solving \(f'(c)=\)(average rate) yourself.

Try it yourself

For f(x) = x² on [0, 4], find the MVT-guaranteed value of c.

Practice

  1. Verify the MVT for f(x) = x² on [1, 3]: find c.
    Avg rate \(=(9-1)/2=4=2c \Rightarrow c=2\), inside \((1,3)\) ✓
  2. Verify the MVT for f(x) = x³ on [0, 2]: find c.
    Avg rate \(=(8-0)/2=4=3c^2 \Rightarrow c^2=4/3 \Rightarrow c=2/\sqrt3\approx1.155\), inside \((0,2)\) ✓
  3. Does Rolle's Theorem apply to f(x) = x² − 4 on [−2, 2]? If so, find c.
    \(f(-2)=0=f(2)\), so yes. \(f'(x)=2x=0 \Rightarrow c=0\), inside \((-2,2)\) ✓
  4. Can the MVT be applied to f(x) = 1/x on [−1, 1]? Explain.
    No — \(f\) is undefined (and discontinuous) at \(x=0\), which lies inside \([-1,1]\), so the continuity hypothesis fails before differentiability is even relevant.
  5. Verify the MVT for f(x) = \sqrt{x} on [0, 4]: find c.
    Avg rate \(=(2-0)/4=0.5\). \(f'(x)=1/(2\sqrt x)=0.5 \Rightarrow \sqrt c=1 \Rightarrow c=1\), inside \((0,4)\) ✓ (continuity at the endpoint \(x=0\) is fine; only differentiability is required on the open interval)
  6. For f(x) = x² − 2x on [0, 3], find the MVT-guaranteed c.
    Avg rate \(=(3-0)/3=1=2c-2 \Rightarrow c=1.5\), inside \((0,3)\) ✓
  7. bonus — Does Rolle's Theorem apply to f(x) = \sin x on [0, π]? If so, find c.
    \(f(0)=f(\pi)=0\), yes. \(f'(x)=\cos x=0 \Rightarrow c=\pi/2\), inside \((0,\pi)\) ✓
  8. bonus — Explain why f(x) = |x| on [−1, 1] does NOT satisfy the MVT's hypotheses, even though it's continuous everywhere.
    It fails differentiability at \(x=0\) (a sharp corner), which is inside the open interval \((-1,1)\) — continuity alone isn't enough.
  9. bonus — For f(x) = x³ − 3x on [−√3, √3], find both values of c guaranteed by Rolle's Theorem.
    \(c=\pm1\) — see the Common Mistake box for the full derivation. Both are valid; the theorem doesn't promise uniqueness.
  10. bonus — A car travels 120 miles in 2 hours. Using the MVT, explain why it must have been traveling at exactly 60 mph at some instant, no matter how its speed varied.
    Let \(f(t)\) be position (continuous and differentiable for any physically reasonable motion). Average velocity \(=120/2=60\) mph \(=\) average rate of change of \(f\) over \([0,2]\). The MVT guarantees some instant \(c\) where \(f'(c)\), the instantaneous velocity, equals that average exactly — \(60\) mph, at least once.
