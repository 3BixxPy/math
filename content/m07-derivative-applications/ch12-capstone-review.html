Module 7 Capstone — Mixed Review & Diagnostic Retake

Eleven chapters of "what is a derivative actually for," shuffled together, plus a look back at what Module 6's toolkit could only compute in isolation until this module gave it something real to solve.

Why mixed, not grouped by chapter

Same reasoning as every capstone before this one: a problem that doesn't announce its own chapter is the real test of whether an idea moved into durable memory, not just short-term pattern matching against a chapter title. This module makes that test especially meaningful — related rates, curve sketching, optimization, and Newton's method don't look anything alike on the surface, but every one of them reduces to the exact same handful of Module 6 mechanics underneath. Getting these right without a chapter label attached is the clearest evidence yet that it's the underlying structure that stuck, not the label.

Mixed review (all of Module 7)

  1. A circle's radius grows at 3 cm/s. How fast is the area growing when r=4?
    \(dA/dt=2\pi r\,dr/dt=2\pi(4)(3)=24\pi\) (Chapter 1)
  2. Find the critical point(s) of f(x) = x² − 8x + 1.
    \(f'=2x-8=0 \Rightarrow x=4\) (Chapter 2)
  3. Use the First Derivative Test: is x=2 a local max or min for f(x)=(x-2)²?
    \(f'=2(x-2)\): \(-\) before, \(+\) after → local min (Chapter 3)
  4. What's the concavity of f(x) = -3x² at any x?
    \(f''=-6<0\) → concave down (Chapter 4)
  5. For f(x) = x³ − 3x², this module's running example, state its inflection point.
    \((1,-2)\) (Chapter 5, tying together Chapters 3–4's separate analyses of this same function)
  6. A rectangle has perimeter 30. What side length maximizes its area?
    \(7.5\) (a square) (Chapter 6)
  7. An open-top box is folded from a 6×6 sheet by cutting squares of side x from each corner. What x maximizes volume?
    \(x=1\) (Chapter 7)
  8. Verify the MVT for f(x) = x² on [0, 6]: find the guaranteed c.
    Avg rate \(=(36-0)/6=6=2c \Rightarrow c=3\) (Chapter 8)
  9. Use linear approximation to estimate √25.2 with a=25.
    \(f(25)=5\), \(f'(25)=1/10\). \(L(25.2)=5+0.1(0.2)=5.02\) (Chapter 9)
  10. Evaluate limx→0 sin(3x)/x using L'Hôpital's Rule.
    0/0 form. \(\lim 3\cos(3x)/1 = 3\) (Chapter 10)
  11. Do one iteration of Newton's method for f(x) = x² − 7, x₀ = 3.
    \(f(3)=2\), \(f'(3)=6\). \(x_1=3-2/6=8/3\approx2.667\) (Chapter 11)
  12. Two positive numbers sum to 40. What values maximize their product?
    \(20\) and \(20\) (Chapter 6, a second optimization problem)

Synthesis — concepts combining, not just shuffled

Two problems that each need multiple chapters' tools working together, not just one technique per problem.

Synthesis 1
Ch. 4 — concavity & inflection pointsCh. 5 — curve sketching

Fully analyze f(x) = x⁴ − 4x³: find and classify all critical points, then find all inflection points. What's notable about x=0?

Step 1 — critical points (Ch. 2)

\(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)=0 \Rightarrow x=0,3\).

Step 2 — classify with a sign chart (Ch. 3)

\(f'(x)=4x^2(x-3)\): since \(x^2\geq0\) never flips sign, the sign of \(f'\) matches the sign of \((x-3)\) everywhere except at \(x=0\) itself. So \(f'<0\) on both \((-\infty,0)\) and \((0,3)\) — no sign change across \(x=0\) → neither a max nor a min there. \(f'\) goes from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(x=3\) → local min, \(f(3)=81-108=-27\).

Step 3 — inflection points via f″ (Ch. 4)

\(f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2)=0 \Rightarrow x=0,2\). Sign of \(f''\): \(+\) on \((-\infty,0)\), \(-\) on \((0,2)\), \(+\) on \((2,\infty)\) — genuine switches at both. \(f(0)=0\), \(f(2)=16-32=-16\).

Result

x=0 does double duty: it's a critical point that is not a local extremum (Chapter 3's test says "neither"), and it's simultaneously an inflection point (Chapter 4's concavity switches there). This is exactly the kind of feature a plugged-in-points sketch (Chapter 5's Common Mistake) would be likely to miss entirely — a flat-looking spot on the graph that's secretly two different features at once.

Synthesis 2
Ch. 1 — related ratesCh. 9 — linear approximation

A spherical balloon's radius is 5 cm and growing at 0.2 cm/s. (a) Use related rates to find how fast its volume is growing right now. (b) Use a differential (linear approximation) to estimate the volume's total change over the next 1 second. What do you notice?

Step 1 — part (a): differentiate V=(4/3)πr³ with respect to t (Ch. 1)

\(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \dfrac{dr}{dt}\). At \(r=5\), \(dr/dt=0.2\): \(dV/dt = 4\pi(25)(0.2) = 20\pi\) cm³/s.

Step 2 — part (b): use the differential dV = 4πr² dr (Ch. 9)

Over \(1\) second, \(dr \approx 0.2\,\text{cm/s} \times 1\,\text{s} = 0.2\) cm. \(dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi(25)(0.2) = 20\pi\) cm³.

Result

Both answers come out to exactly \(20\pi\) — not a coincidence. Over a \(1\)-second interval, the differential \(dV=\dfrac{dV}{dt}\cdot dt\) is literally the related-rate answer multiplied by the elapsed time. Linear approximation and related rates aren't two unrelated tricks; a differential is what a related rate looks like once it's been asked "how much, total, over this stretch of time" instead of "how fast, right now."

Diagnostic retake

Module 6 gave you the toolkit — every rule for computing a derivative, from the power rule through implicit differentiation. Module 7 is the first time that toolkit actually solved a problem you'd want solved, not just a formula to differentiate for its own sake.

1. A rectangular garden plot needs 60 ft of fencing total. What dimensions maximize its enclosed area?

Originally: Module 6 could compute d/dx[x(30−x)] in isolation without blinking — but had no reason to ask why anyone would want to. This chapter is the first time that same derivative had a real question behind it.

A \(15\times15\) square, area \(225\) sq ft. See Chapter 6.

2. A curve has f'(x)<0 on (−∞,1), f'(x)>0 on (1,∞), and f''(x)>0 everywhere. Describe its essential shape: is there a local extremum? Any inflection points?

Originally: Module 6 taught you to mechanically compute f′ and f″ from a formula. This module taught you to read what the signs of those derivatives actually say about the shape of the graph.

Local min at \(x=1\) (sign of \(f'\) goes \(-\to+\)). Concave up everywhere, so no inflection points — concavity never switches. See Chapters 3–5.

3. Does f(x) = x² − 4 satisfy the Mean Value Theorem's hypotheses on [−2, 2]? If Rolle's Theorem applies, find the guaranteed c.

Originally: Module 6 made sure you could differentiate anything continuous, anywhere. MVT is the first time "differentiable everywhere" got used to guarantee that something exists, instead of just computing a value.

\(f(-2)=0=f(2)\), so Rolle's Theorem applies. \(f'(x)=2x=0 \Rightarrow c=0\), inside \((-2,2)\). See Chapter 8.
