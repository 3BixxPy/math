Module 5 Capstone — Mixed Review & Diagnostic Retake

Eleven chapters, shuffled together, plus the calculus section of the very first diagnostic — the part that came back as a genuinely blank slate, not even a wrong guess.

Why mixed, not grouped by chapter

Same reasoning as Module 0, 1, and every capstone since: a problem that doesn't announce its own chapter is the real test of whether the idea moved into durable memory, not just short-term pattern matching against a chapter title. Calculus makes this test even more meaningful than usual — every technique here was completely unavailable to you twelve chapters ago, so getting these right shuffled together is the clearest possible evidence that the ideas actually landed.

Mixed review (all of Module 5)

  1. Does limx→a f(x) require that f(a) be defined?
    No (Chapter 1) — the limit only describes behavior near \(a\), never at \(a\).
  2. For f(x) = {x−1 if x<3; x+1 if x≥3}, does limx→3 f(x) exist?
    No (Chapter 2) — left-hand limit \(\to2\), right-hand limit \(\to4\); they disagree.
  3. Which of the three ways a limit can fail to exist applies to limx→0 sin(1/x)?
    Oscillation (Chapter 3) — bounded in \([-1,1]\), but never settles as \(x\to0\).
  4. Evaluate limx→2 (3x²−5x+1) using the limit laws.
    \(12-10+1=3\) (Chapter 4).
  5. Evaluate limx→4 (x²−16)/(x−4).
    Factor and cancel: \(x+4\to8\) (Chapter 5).
  6. Evaluate limx→∞ (2x²+1)/(x²+5x).
    Equal degrees, ratio of leading coefficients: \(2\) (Chapter 6).
  7. Evaluate limx→3⁺ 1/(x−3).
    \(+\infty\) (Chapter 7).
  8. Is f(x)=1/(x−2) continuous at x=2?
    No (Chapter 8) — condition (1) fails, \(f(2)\) is undefined.
  9. Classify the discontinuity of (x²−4)/(x−2) at x=2.
    Removable (Chapter 9) — the limit exists (\(=4\)), \(f(2)\) is undefined.
  10. If −x² ≤ f(x) ≤ x² near x=0, find limx→0 f(x).
    \(0\) (Chapter 10, squeeze theorem).
  11. Evaluate limx→0 sin(4x)/x.
    \(4\) (Chapter 11).
  12. Classify the discontinuity of g(x) = {x if x<1; x+3 if x≥1} at x=1.
    Jump (Chapters 8–9) — left-hand limit \(1\), right-hand limit \(4\); they disagree, so no single redefinition of \(g(1)\) could fix it.

Synthesis — concepts combining, not just shuffled

Two problems that each need multiple chapters' tools working together, not just one technique per problem.

Synthesis 1
Ch. 4 — limit lawsCh. 5 — factoring

Evaluate limx→1 [(x²−1)/(x−1) + (3x−2)].

Step 1 — split via the sum law (Ch. 4): handle each piece separately

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\left[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}+(3x-2)\right] = \lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} \;+\; \lim_{x\to1}(3x-2)\)

Step 2 — first piece is 0/0; factor and cancel (Ch. 5)

\(\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1\) for \(x\neq1\), so this piece's limit is \(1+1=2\).

Step 3 — second piece is a plain polynomial; direct substitution (Ch. 4)

\(3(1)-2=1\).

Result

The sum law recombines the two pieces: \(2+1=3\).

Synthesis 2
Ch. 8 — continuityCh. 9 — discontinuity types

Let f(x) = (x²−9)/(x−3) for x≠3, and f(3) = k. For what value of k is f continuous at x=3? If k=10 instead, what type of discontinuity results?

Step 1 — find the limit (factor and cancel, Ch. 5's tool, reused here)

\(\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}=\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}=x+3\) for \(x\neq3\), so \(\lim_{x\to3}f(x)=6\).

Step 2 — apply the three-part continuity test (Ch. 8) with f(3)=k

Condition (1): \(f(3)=k\), defined by construction, for any \(k\). Condition (2): the limit exists and equals \(6\). Condition (3) needs \(f(3)=\) the limit, i.e. \(k=6\).

Result

f is continuous at \(x=3\) exactly when \(k=6\). If \(k=10\) instead, conditions (1) and (2) still hold but (3) fails (\(6\neq10\)) — by Chapter 9's classification, that's precisely a removable discontinuity: the hole would simply be patched with the wrong value.
