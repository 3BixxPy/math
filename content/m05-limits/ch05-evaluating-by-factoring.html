Evaluating 0/0 Limits — Factoring & Rationalizing

Chapter 4's laws hit a wall the instant direct substitution produces 0/0. Chapter 1's opening example — estimated back then with nothing but a table — finally gets its algebraic shortcut.

Why this matters

0/0 is not "the answer is zero" and it's not "undefined, give up." It's a specific signal: the numerator and denominator share a hidden common factor, and once that factor is exposed and cancelled, direct substitution works perfectly well on whatever's left. This entire chapter is Module 0's factoring chapters, redeployed on a new kind of problem — no new algebra to learn, just a new reason to reach for it.

Definition — Indeterminate form 0/0

When direct substitution into a quotient produces 0/0, that numeric output carries no information about the actual limit — it is neither "the answer" nor a proof the limit fails to exist (Chapter 1 already showed a 0/0 case with a perfectly good limit of 4). It's a flag meaning: this expression needs to be rewritten into an equivalent form before substitution will work.

Worked example — factoring

Evaluate limx→3 (x²−9)/(x−3).

Step 1 — try direct substitution

\(\dfrac{3^2-9}{3-3}=\dfrac{0}{0}\) — indeterminate. Try a rewrite.

Step 2 — factor the numerator (Module 0, Chapter 7)

\(x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)\)

Step 3 — cancel the common factor

\(\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}=x+3\), valid for every \(x\neq3\) — which is exactly the only kind of \(x\) a limit ever looks at (Chapter 1).

Result — substitute into the simplified form

\(\lim_{x\to3}(x+3)=3+3=6\).

Worked example — rationalizing

Evaluate limx→0 (√(x+4)−2)/x.

Step 1 — try direct substitution

\(\dfrac{\sqrt{4}-2}{0}=\dfrac{0}{0}\) — indeterminate, and there's no common polynomial factor to spot here because of the radical. Rationalize instead.

Step 2 — multiply by the conjugate, top and bottom

Multiply by \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}\): \(\dfrac{(\sqrt{x+4}-2)(\sqrt{x+4}+2)}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)} = \dfrac{(x+4)-4}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)} = \dfrac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)}\)

Step 3 — cancel the common factor of x

\(\dfrac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}\), valid for \(x\neq0\).

Result — substitute into the simplified form

\(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt4+2}=\dfrac{1}{4}\).

Common mistake

Concluding a limit "doesn't exist" the moment direct substitution fails, instead of trying an algebraic rewrite first. A 0/0 result specifically (as opposed to, say, nonzero/0, which genuinely does signal unbounded behavior — Chapter 7) is precisely the signal to try harder, not to stop. Factor first; if radicals are in the way, rationalize. Only after both fail to simplify anything should "the limit might not exist" even be on the table.

Practice

  1. Evaluate limx→5 (x²−25)/(x−5).
    Factor: \((x-5)(x+5)/(x-5)=x+5\). Limit: \(10\).
  2. Evaluate limx→−2 (x²−4)/(x+2).
    Factor: \((x-2)(x+2)/(x+2)=x-2\). Limit: \(-4\).
  3. Evaluate limx→1 (x²−1)/(x−1).
    Factor: \((x-1)(x+1)/(x-1)=x+1\). Limit: \(2\).
  4. Evaluate limx→4 (x²−3x−4)/(x−4). (Factor the trinomial first.)
    \(x^2-3x-4=(x-4)(x+1)\), so the expression simplifies to \(x+1\). Limit: \(5\).
  5. Evaluate limx→0 (√(x+9)−3)/x. (Rationalize.)
    Multiply by the conjugate: \(\frac{(x+9)-9}{x(\sqrt{x+9}+3)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}\). Limit: \(1/6\).
  6. Evaluate limx→9 (x−9)/(√x−3). (Recognize \(x-9\) as a difference of squares in disguise: \(x-9=(\sqrt x-3)(\sqrt x+3)\).)
    \(\frac{(\sqrt x-3)(\sqrt x+3)}{\sqrt x-3}=\sqrt x+3\). Limit: \(6\).
  7. bonus — Evaluate limx→2 (x³−8)/(x−2). (Factor the difference of cubes: \(x^3-8=(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)\).)
    Simplifies to \(x^2+2x+4\). Limit: \(4+4+4=12\).
  8. bonus — Evaluate limx→−3 (x²+5x+6)/(x+3).
    Factor: \((x+3)(x+2)/(x+3)=x+2\). Limit: \(-1\).
  9. bonus — Evaluate limx→0 (√(x+1)−1)/x.
    Rationalize: \(\frac{(x+1)-1}{x(\sqrt{x+1}+1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1}+1}\). Limit: \(1/2\).
  10. bonus — In your own words, explain why the cancellation step (x−3)(x+3)/(x−3) = x+3 is legitimate for evaluating a limit, even though the two sides aren't identical everywhere (the left side is undefined exactly at x=3, while x+3 is perfectly defined there).
    The two expressions agree for every \(x\neq3\), and a limit only ever examines values near \(a\) — never the value at \(a\) itself (Chapter 1). Since the limiting process literally never uses \(x=3\), the one point where the two sides disagree is invisible to it.
  11. challenge — Evaluate limx→7 (√(x+2)−3)/(x−7) using rationalizing, and separately verify your answer with a table of at least two x-values on each side of 7 (same style as Chapter 1).
    Rationalize: \(\frac{(x+2)-9}{(x-7)(\sqrt{x+2}+3)}=\frac{x-7}{(x-7)(\sqrt{x+2}+3)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+2}+3}\). Limit at \(x=7\): \(1/(3+3)=1/6\approx0.1667\). Table check: e.g. \(x=6.9\to\approx0.1668\), \(x=7.1\to\approx0.1666\) — both sides converge to \(1/6\).
