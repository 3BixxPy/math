Chapter 8 · concept:continuity-definition

Continuity

Every chapter so far studied what f does near a point without necessarily caring what f does at that point — Chapter 1 built the whole module around keeping those two questions separate. Continuity is exactly the special case where "near" and "at" finally agree, made precise.

Why this matters

Informally, a continuous function is one you could trace with a pencil without ever lifting it off the page — no jumps, no holes, no places it shoots off to infinity. That picture is useful, but it isn't a definition you can check on a function you can't see graphed. This chapter turns it into a precise, three-part test built entirely out of tools you already have: evaluation (Module 1), and limits (Chapters 1–7). Nothing new has to be invented — continuity is what happens when those existing pieces line up.

Definition — Continuity at a point
\[ f \text{ is continuous at } x=a \iff \begin{cases} \text{(1)}\ \ f(a)\text{ is defined} \\ \text{(2)}\ \ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)\text{ exists} \\ \text{(3)}\ \ \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = f(a) \end{cases} \]
(5.8.1)

All three conditions must hold together. (1) is a domain question (Module 1) — is the function even defined there. (2) is the existence question from Chapters 2–3 — the limit has to exist in the ordinary sense, not just "sort of" via one side, and not via an infinite or oscillating blow-up. (3) is the genuinely new part: the number the function is heading toward has to be the exact number it actually lands on. A function is continuous on an interval if it's continuous at every single point in that interval. As a direct consequence of Chapter 4's limit laws, every polynomial is continuous everywhere — direct substitution always satisfies all three conditions at once.

Worked example — checking all three conditions

Is f(x) = {x+1 if x<2; 3 if x≥2} continuous at x=2?

Step 1 — condition (1): is f(2) defined?

Yes — the \(x\geq2\) piece owns \(x=2\) (Module 1, Chapter 9), so \(f(2)=3\).

Step 2 — condition (2): does lim x→2 f(x) exist?

Left-hand: as \(x\to2^-\), \(x+1\to3\). Right-hand: as \(x\to2^+\), the constant piece gives \(3\). Both sides agree at \(3\), so by (5.2.1) the limit exists and equals \(3\).

Step 3 — condition (3): does the limit equal f(2)?

Limit is \(3\); \(f(2)\) is \(3\). They match.

Result

All three conditions hold, so \(f\) is continuous at \(x=2\) — despite being piecewise. Piecewise doesn't automatically mean discontinuous; it just means all three conditions need to be checked explicitly rather than assumed.

Common mistake

Checking only one or two of the three conditions and stopping there. It's entirely possible for a function to satisfy conditions (1) and (2) — f(a) is defined, and the limit exists — while still failing (3) because the two numbers simply don't match. For example, g(x) = x² for x≠3, with g(3) separately defined as 10: g(3)=10 (condition 1 ✓), limx→3 g(x)=9 (condition 2 ✓, it exists), but 9≠10 — condition (3) fails, so g is not continuous at x=3, despite passing two of the three checks. All three, every time.

Try it yourself

Is f(x) = 1/x continuous at x=0?

Practice

  1. For f(x)=x²−3x+2, is f continuous at x=5? Why?
    Yes — it's a polynomial, and polynomials are continuous everywhere.
  2. For g(x)=1/(x−4), is g continuous at x=4? Which condition fails?
    No — condition (1) fails, since \(g(4)\) is undefined.
  3. For h(x) = {2x if x<1; 2 if x≥1}, check continuity at x=1 using all three conditions.
    (1) \(h(1)=2\), defined. (2) Left-hand \(\to2\), right-hand \(=2\), so the limit exists and equals \(2\). (3) Limit \(2\) = \(h(1)\) \(2\). All hold — continuous.
  4. For k(x) = x² everywhere except k(3)=10, check continuity at x=3.
    (1) \(k(3)=10\), defined. (2) \(\lim_{x\to3}k(x)=9\), exists. (3) \(9\neq10\) — fails. Not continuous at \(x=3\).
  5. Is every polynomial continuous at every real number? Justify briefly using Chapter 4.
    Yes — Chapter 4's limit laws guarantee direct substitution always works for a polynomial, which automatically satisfies all three conditions at every point.
  6. For m(x) = {x+2 if x≤0; x²+2 if x>0}, check continuity at x=0.
    (1) \(m(0)=2\) (first piece owns \(x=0\)). (2) Left-hand \(=2\), right-hand \(\to0^2+2=2\); limit exists, equals \(2\). (3) Matches \(m(0)=2\). Continuous.
  7. bonus — For n(x) = √x, is n continuous at x=−1? Explain using condition (1).
    No — \(n(-1)\) isn't defined at all (negative under a square root), so condition (1) fails immediately; nothing else needs checking.
  8. bonus — For p(x) = {3x−1 if x<2; 5 if x=2; x+3 if x>2}, check all three conditions at x=2.
    (1) \(p(2)=5\), defined. (2) Left-hand \(\to5\), right-hand \(\to5\); limit exists, equals \(5\). (3) Matches. Continuous at \(x=2\).
  9. bonus — True or false: if limx→a f(x) exists, then f must be continuous at a.
    False — \(f(a)\) might be undefined, or defined but unequal to the limit. Existence of the limit is only one of the three required conditions.
  10. bonus — Explain briefly why every rational function P(x)/Q(x) is continuous at every point in its domain (every point where Q(a)≠0).
    Where \(Q(a)\neq0\), Chapter 4's quotient law guarantees direct substitution works, satisfying all three conditions at once — same reasoning as polynomials, extended by the quotient law.
  11. challenge — Construct one function that satisfies conditions (1) and (2) but fails (3), and a separate function that satisfies (2) but fails (1) (so (3) can't even be evaluated). Explain why each is discontinuous despite partially passing the test.
    (1)&(2) but not (3): \(f(x)=x\) for \(x\neq0\), with \(f(0)=5\) — defined (1 ✓), limit exists and equals \(0\) (2 ✓), but \(0\neq5\) (3 fails). Not continuous. (2) but not (1): \(g(x)=(x^2-1)/(x-1)\) at \(x=1\) — literally \(0/0\), so \(g(1)\) is undefined (1 fails) even though \(\lim_{x\to1}g(x)=2\) exists (2 ✓). With (1) failing, there's no \(g(1)\) value for (3) to compare against, so (3) can't hold either — one failed condition is already enough to rule out continuity, which is exactly why all three have to be checked rather than stopping early.
