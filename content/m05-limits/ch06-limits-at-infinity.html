Limits at Infinity — Horizontal Asymptotes

Module 1, Chapter 14 handed you the horizontal-asymptote rule as a pattern to compare and memorize: check the degrees. This chapter derives it. The rule was a limit the whole time — you just didn't have limit notation yet to see it.

Why this matters

So far every limit has asked what happens as x approaches a specific finite number. This chapter asks a different but closely related question: what happens as x grows without bound? The technique is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it: as x gets huge, the highest power of x in an expression completely dominates every lower power — so dividing everything by that highest power turns the lower-order terms into things that visibly vanish, leaving only what actually matters.

Rule — Limits at infinity of rational functions
\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{a_nx^n+\cdots}{b_nx^n+\cdots} = \frac{a_n}{b_n} \quad\text{(equal degrees } n\text{)} \]
(5.6.1)

The full picture, comparing the degree of the numerator to the degree of the denominator:

  • degree of numerator < degree of denominator → limx→∞ f(x) = 0
  • degree of numerator = degree of denominator → the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients, (5.6.1)
  • degree of numerator > degree of denominator → unbounded; there is no horizontal asymptote (Chapter 7 makes "unbounded" itself precise)

This is exactly Module 1, Chapter 14's rule, word for word — it was always a statement about a limit at infinity.

Worked example — equal degrees

Evaluate limx→∞ (3x²+1)/(x²−5).

Step 1 — divide numerator and denominator by the highest power present, x²

\(\dfrac{3x^2+1}{x^2-5} = \dfrac{3+1/x^2}{1-5/x^2}\)

Step 2 — let x→∞

\(1/x^2\to0\) and \(5/x^2\to0\) as \(x\) grows without bound — any constant divided by a growing power of \(x\) shrinks to nothing.

Result

\(\dfrac{3+0}{1-0} = 3\) — matching (5.6.1) directly, since both degrees are 2 and the leading coefficients are 3 and 1.

Worked example — numerator degree lower

Evaluate limx→∞ (2x+1)/(x²+1).

Step 1 — divide by the highest power present overall, x²

\(\dfrac{2x+1}{x^2+1} = \dfrac{2/x+1/x^2}{1+1/x^2}\)

Result

Every term in the numerator now has an \(x\) in the denominator, so the whole numerator \(\to0\), while the denominator \(\to1\): \(\dfrac{0}{1}=0\) — matching (5.6.1)'s first case (numerator degree 1 < denominator degree 2).

Common mistake

Assuming a limit at infinity is always 0, or always ∞, regardless of the specific function. It depends entirely on comparing the degrees, per the rule above — a limit at infinity of a rational function can land on 0, a nonzero finite number, or be unbounded, and only checking the actual degrees tells you which.

Practice

  1. Evaluate limx→∞ (4x²−1)/(2x²+3).
    Equal degrees: \(4/2=2\).
  2. Evaluate limx→∞ (x+7)/(x²−1).
    Numerator degree lower: \(0\).
  3. Evaluate limx→∞ (6x³+x)/(2x³−5).
    Equal degrees: \(6/2=3\).
  4. Evaluate limx→∞ (x²+1)/(x+1).
    Numerator degree higher: unbounded (\(+\infty\)) — no horizontal asymptote.
  5. Evaluate limx→−∞ (3x+2)/(x−4). (Same technique works for x→−∞.)
    Equal degrees: \(3/1=3\).
  6. Without fully computing, state which case limx→∞ (5x⁴−x)/(x⁴+x²+1) falls into, then compute it.
    Equal degrees case; value \(5/1=5\).
  7. bonus — Evaluate limx→∞ 7/(x+2).
    Numerator degree \(0\) is lower than denominator degree \(1\): limit \(0\).
  8. bonus — Evaluate limx→∞ (x²−4)/(2x²+3x−1).
    Equal degrees: \(1/2\).
  9. bonus — Using Module 1, Chapter 14's rule, state the horizontal asymptote of f(x)=(x−1)/(3x+2) without recomputing — then confirm it with this chapter's technique.
    \(y=1/3\) by the degree-comparison rule. Confirming: divide by \(x\): \(\frac{1-1/x}{3+2/x}\to\frac{1}{3}\) as \(x\to\infty\).
  10. bonus — Evaluate limx→∞ (x³−2x)/(5x²+1), and explain in words what "no horizontal asymptote" means for the graph's long-run behavior.
    Numerator degree higher: unbounded (\(+\infty\)). Graphically, the function keeps climbing forever rather than leveling off toward any fixed height as \(x\) grows.
  11. challenge — For f(x) = (ax²+bx+c)/(dx²+ex+g) (with d≠0), explain in general why limx→∞ f(x) always equals a/d, no matter what b, c, e, g are. Connect your explanation to the "divide by the highest power" technique.
    Dividing every term by \(x^2\) gives \(\frac{a+b/x+c/x^2}{d+e/x+g/x^2}\). Every term except \(a\) and \(d\) has an \(x\) in its own denominator, so every one of them \(\to0\) as \(x\to\infty\) — regardless of what \(b,c,e,g\) actually are — leaving exactly \(a/d\).
