Chapter 4 · concept:limit-laws

Limit Laws

Chapters 1–3 built limits by inspection: tables, one-sided reasoning, naming failure modes. This chapter gives algebraic rules so most limits can be evaluated directly, with no table required — and explains precisely why "just plug in" is usually legal, and exactly when it stops being legal.

Why this matters

Limits of well-behaved functions turn out to pass cleanly through ordinary arithmetic: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, the limit of a product is the product of the limits, and so on. That's not obvious in advance — it has to be proven (a fully rigorous proof needs the formal ε–δ definition, outside this course's scope, but the statements themselves are exactly what you'd hope for). Once you have these rules, "direct substitution" stops being a guess you hope works and becomes a provable shortcut for an entire family of functions — which is exactly why Chapter 5 needs a different technique the moment this family's assumptions break down.

Rule — Sum and difference law
\[ \lim_{x\to a}\left[f(x)\pm g(x)\right] = \lim_{x\to a}f(x) \pm \lim_{x\to a}g(x) \]
(5.4.1)

Provided both individual limits on the right exist.

Rule — Product law
\[ \lim_{x\to a}\left[f(x)\cdot g(x)\right] = \lim_{x\to a}f(x) \cdot \lim_{x\to a}g(x) \]
(5.4.2)

Same condition — both individual limits must exist. A constant multiple is a special case (g(x)=c everywhere): the limit of c·f(x) is just c times the limit of f(x).

Rule — Quotient law
\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)}{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}g(x)}, \quad\text{provided } \lim_{x\to a}g(x) \neq 0 \]
(5.4.3)

The nonzero-denominator condition is not fine print — it's the exact seam where Chapter 5 picks up. When it fails, this law simply doesn't apply; it says nothing at all about whether the limit exists, only that this particular tool can't answer the question.

A direct consequence of applying (5.4.1) and (5.4.2) repeatedly: for any polynomial p(x) (built entirely out of sums, differences, and products of x with constants), limx→a p(x) = p(a) — direct substitution always works. More generally this holds for any function that is continuous at a, a term Chapter 8 makes precise; for now, treat "polynomial, or a nice combination like it" as the working definition.

Worked example — direct substitution as an application of the laws

Evaluate limx→2 (3x²−5x+1) by applying the laws piece by piece, rather than just plugging in.

Step 1 — split the sum via (5.4.1)

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}(3x^2-5x+1) = \lim_{x\to2}3x^2 \;-\; \lim_{x\to2}5x \;+\; \lim_{x\to2}1\)

Step 2 — evaluate the most basic limit first: lim x→2 x

The limit of the identity function \(x\) as \(x\to2\) is trivially \(2\) — as \(x\) approaches 2, \(x\) itself approaches 2.

Step 3 — build each term with the product law (5.4.2)

\(\lim_{x\to2}3x^2 = 3\cdot\lim_{x\to2}x\cdot\lim_{x\to2}x = 3(2)(2)=12\). \(\lim_{x\to2}5x = 5\cdot2=10\). \(\lim_{x\to2}1 = 1\) (a constant's limit is itself).

Result — combine

\(12 - 10 + 1 = 3\). This matches plugging \(x=2\) directly into \(3x^2-5x+1\) — which is exactly the point. "Just plug in" isn't a separate shortcut; it's what these three laws collapse to, every time, for a polynomial.

Common mistake

Applying the quotient law (5.4.3) when the denominator's limit is 0. For example, limx→2 (x²−4)/(x−2) — the exact expression from Chapter 1's worked example — has denominator limit 0 at x=2, so (5.4.3) simply does not apply here; writing "the limit is 0/0, so it doesn't exist" is not a valid conclusion, it's a sign the wrong tool was reached for. Chapter 1 already showed this limit exists and equals 4 (by table); Chapter 5 gives the algebraic method that gets there directly.

Practice

  1. Evaluate limx→3 (4x−1).
    \(11\)
  2. Evaluate limx→−2 (x²+3x).
    \(4-6=-2\)
  3. Evaluate limx→0 (5x³−2x+7).
    \(7\)
  4. Evaluate limx→4 √x. (State briefly why direct substitution is justified here.)
    \(2\) — \(\sqrt{x}\) is continuous at \(x=4\) (it's in its domain, with no gaps nearby), so direct substitution applies the same way it does for a polynomial.
  5. Evaluate limx→2 (x²+1)/(x+3).
    Denominator's limit is \(5\neq0\), so (5.4.3) applies directly: \(5/5=1\).
  6. Explain why limx→5 (x−5)/(x−5) can't be evaluated with the quotient law directly, even though the expression obviously simplifies to 1 for every x≠5.
    The quotient law's precondition requires the denominator's limit, \(\lim_{x\to5}(x-5)=0\), to be nonzero — it isn't, so the law simply doesn't apply here. That doesn't mean the limit fails to exist (it does exist, and equals 1) — it means this particular law can't be the tool that shows it.
  7. bonus — Evaluate limx→−1 (2x+1)(x−4) using the product law explicitly (state the two individual limits before multiplying).
    \(\lim(2x+1)=-1\), \(\lim(x-4)=-5\). Product: \((-1)(-5)=5\).
  8. bonus — Evaluate limx→0 (x²+2x+1)/(x+5).
    \(1/5\)
  9. bonus — True or false: every rational function P(x)/Q(x) can always be evaluated at any point a by direct substitution.
    False — only where \(Q(a)\neq0\); the quotient law's precondition fails otherwise.
  10. bonus — Evaluate limx→3 7 (the limit of a constant function). Which fact does this rely on?
    \(7\) — a constant function's limit anywhere is just that constant; it's the trivial base case these laws build on top of.
  11. challenge — Using only the sum/difference and product laws (not "just plug in"), show step by step that limx→a xⁿ = aⁿ for a positive integer n, by repeatedly applying the product law.
    Base case: \(\lim_{x\to a}x=a\) (trivial). Inductive step: assume \(\lim_{x\to a}x^k=a^k\). Then \(\lim_{x\to a}x^{k+1}=\lim_{x\to a}(x^k\cdot x)=\left(\lim_{x\to a}x^k\right)\left(\lim_{x\to a}x\right)=a^k\cdot a=a^{k+1}\), by the product law. By induction, this holds for every positive integer \(n\).
