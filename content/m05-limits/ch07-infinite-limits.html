Chapter 7 · concept:infinite-limits

Infinite Limits — Vertical Asymptotes

Chapter 3 flagged that writing "the limit is infinity" isn't quite an ordinary limit statement. This chapter makes that notation precise, and connects it directly to Module 1's vertical asymptotes.

Why this matters

Unbounded growth toward a specific x-value is extremely common, and it's worth having a compact, standard way to describe it — even though, strictly, the ordinary limit still doesn't exist in this situation (Chapter 3). This chapter gives that description a precise meaning and a precise method (sign analysis) for working it out, rather than leaving it as a vague "it blows up."

Definition — Infinite limit
\[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty \]
(5.7.1)

This notation means: for every bound you could name, f(x) eventually exceeds it as x gets close enough to a — f(x) grows without bound. It is not a claim that some number called ∞ is actually being approached, because ∞ isn't a real number at all. Written this way, it's a precise description of unbounded growth, not a genuine numeric limit value; limx→a f(x) = −∞ means the same thing in the negative direction.

Worked example — sign analysis at a vertical asymptote

Evaluate the one-sided infinite limits of f(x) = 1/(x−2) at x=2.

Step 1 — approach from the right (x→2⁺)

As \(x\to2^+\), \(x-2\) is a small positive number (like \(0.01\)). \(1\) divided by a small positive number is a large positive number, growing without bound: \(\lim_{x\to2^+}\dfrac{1}{x-2}=+\infty\).

Step 2 — approach from the left (x→2⁻)

As \(x\to2^-\), \(x-2\) is a small negative number (like \(-0.01\)). \(1\) divided by a small negative number is a large negative number: \(\lim_{x\to2^-}\dfrac{1}{x-2}=-\infty\).

Step 3 — combine

The one-sided behaviors don't even agree in sign, so \(\lim_{x\to2}\dfrac{1}{x-2}\) does not exist — this is exactly Chapter 3's "unbounded behavior" failure mode, now written precisely with signed one-sided notation.

Connecting to Module 1

\(x=2\) is exactly the vertical asymptote Module 1, Chapter 14 would find for this function (numerator \(1\) never cancels with the denominator). An infinite one-sided limit like this is the rigorous definition of a vertical asymptote — Module 1 spotted the pattern; this chapter is what was actually happening underneath it.

Common mistake

Writing limx→a f(x) = ∞ and then treating ∞ as if it were an ordinary number in further algebra — plugging it into Chapter 4's limit laws, for instance, which strictly require every piece's limit to exist as a genuine real number. It doesn't. ∞ here is a description of behavior, not a value that can be added, multiplied, or substituted like 4 or −2 can.

Practice

  1. Evaluate limx→3⁺ 1/(x−3).
    \(+\infty\)
  2. Evaluate limx→3⁻ 1/(x−3).
    \(-\infty\)
  3. Evaluate limx→0⁺ 1/x².
    \(+\infty\) (squaring keeps it positive from either side)
  4. Evaluate limx→0 1/x² (two-sided). Do both one-sided limits agree here?
    \(+\infty\) — unusually, both one-sided limits agree (both \(+\infty\)), so we conventionally write the two-sided limit the same way, still meaning unbounded growth, not a real number.
  5. For f(x)=1/(x+5), find the vertical asymptote and describe the sign of f(x) just to the left and just to the right of it.
    Asymptote at \(x=-5\). Just left of \(-5\): \(x+5\) is a small negative number, so \(f\) is negative (\(\to-\infty\)). Just right: \(x+5\) is a small positive number, so \(f\) is positive (\(\to+\infty\)).
  6. Evaluate limx→1 1/(x−1)², and state whether this two-sided limit "exists" in the strict sense despite the fact that we can write =+∞.
    \(=+\infty\) as written, but strictly the limit does not exist — there is no real number it approaches; the notation only records which unbounded behavior occurs.
  7. bonus — Evaluate limx→4⁻ 1/(4−x). (Watch the sign inside carefully.)
    As \(x\to4^-\), \(4-x\) is a small positive number, so the limit is \(+\infty\).
  8. bonus — Evaluate limx→−2⁺ 1/(x+2).
    \(+\infty\) — \(x+2\) is small and positive just to the right of \(-2\).
  9. bonus — True or false: "limx→a f(x) = ∞" means f is continuous at a.
    False — \(f\) isn't even defined at \(a\) in this situation; it's about as far from continuous as a function can be (Chapter 8 makes this precise).
  10. bonus — For g(x) = 3/(x−6)², does g have a two-sided infinite limit at x=6? Use sign analysis to justify.
    Yes, \(+\infty\) on both sides — \((x-6)^2\) is positive on both sides near \(6\), and multiplying by the positive constant \(3\) doesn't change the sign.
  11. challenge — Construct a rational function with vertical asymptotes at both x=1 and x=−1, where the one-sided limits at x=1 agree (both +∞) but the one-sided limits at x=−1 disagree (+∞ and −∞). Explain the difference using even vs. odd multiplicity of the surviving factor.
    E.g. \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2(x+1)}\). At \(x=1\): \((x-1)^2\) is an even power, so it stays positive on both sides — the one-sided limits agree (\(+\infty\), \(+\infty\)). At \(x=-1\): \((x+1)\) is an odd power (multiplicity 1), so its sign flips across the point — the one-sided limits disagree (\(+\infty\) on one side, \(-\infty\) on the other). General rule: even multiplicity in the surviving factor ⇒ agreeing one-sided signs; odd multiplicity ⇒ a sign flip.
