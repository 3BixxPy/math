Chapter 8 · concept:dimension

Dimension

Chapter 7 defined a basis. This chapter asks the natural follow-up: does it matter which basis you pick, at least in terms of how many vectors it has?

Why this matters

Dimension is one of the very few numbers you can attach to an entire vector space, rather than to one particular vector inside it — and it's only well-defined because of a genuinely nontrivial fact: no matter which basis you choose for a given space, it always has the same number of vectors. That shared number is the space's dimension.

Definition — Dimension
\[ \dim(V) = \text{the number of vectors in any basis for } V \]
(11.8.1)

This is only a valid definition because of a fact stated here without proof: every basis of a given vector space \(V\) has exactly the same number of vectors. That's the nontrivial part — Chapter 7 showed several different valid bases for \(\mathbb R^2\), and this fact guarantees every single one of them has exactly 2 vectors, never 3, never 1.

Worked example — an arrow space and a matrix space

State \(\dim(\mathbb R^3)\), then find the dimension of the vector space of all 2×2 matrices.

Step 1 — R³

The standard basis \(\langle1,0,0\rangle,\langle0,1,0\rangle,\langle0,0,1\rangle\) has 3 vectors, so \(\dim(\mathbb R^3)=3\).

Step 2 — all 2×2 matrices (Chapter 1's callback)

Consider \(E_{11}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\), \(E_{12}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\), \(E_{21}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\), \(E_{22}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) — each has a single 1 and zeros elsewhere. Any 2×2 matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\) is exactly \(aE_{11}+bE_{12}+cE_{21}+dE_{22}\) (spanning), and the only way a combination \(aE_{11}+bE_{12}+cE_{21}+dE_{22}\) can equal the zero matrix is \(a=b=c=d=0\) (independence, since each coefficient controls one distinct entry). So \(\{E_{11},E_{12},E_{21},E_{22}\}\) is a basis, with 4 vectors.

Result

\(\dim(M_{2,2})=4\) — a concrete, non-arrow example of a 4-dimensional vector space.

Common mistake

Assuming "dimension" always means "how many numbers describe one element." For the 2×2 matrix space, each "vector" (each matrix) genuinely does have 4 numbers in it, and the space's dimension really is 4 — but that's a coincidence of this particular example, not a rule. "How many numbers describe one element" and "how many basis vectors are needed" are two different questions that happen to agree here. They won't always: later on, the vector space of polynomials of degree ≤ \(n\) needs \(n+1\) basis vectors (\(\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}\)) — again coincidentally matching "\(n+1\) coefficients" — but never take that pattern for granted without actually checking a basis directly for whatever new space you meet.

Try it yourself

What is the dimension of \(\mathbb R^4\)?

Practice

  1. What is \(\dim(\mathbb R^2)\)?
    2
  2. What is \(\dim(\mathbb R^5)\)?
    5
  3. What is the dimension of the vector space of all 3×3 matrices?
    9 — the basis of nine "single 1, rest zeros" matrices, one for each entry.
  4. A basis for a vector space \(V\) has 6 vectors. What is \(\dim(V)\)?
    6.
  5. True or false: a vector space can have two different bases with different numbers of vectors.
    False — this chapter's whole point: every basis of a given space shares the same size.
  6. What is the dimension of the trivial vector space \(\{\mathbf 0\}\) (containing only the zero vector)?
    0, by convention — its only independent-and-spanning set is the empty set, since the zero vector alone is never independent (Chapter 5).
  7. bonus — What is the dimension of ordinary 2-component column vectors (i.e. \(\mathbb R^2\) again, written as columns)?
    2 — writing the same vectors as columns instead of rows doesn't change the space.
  8. bonus — A set of 5 vectors is given in \(\mathbb R^3\). Can it possibly be a basis for \(\mathbb R^3\)?
    No — a basis for \(\mathbb R^3\) needs exactly 3 vectors; 5 vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) are automatically dependent.
  9. bonus — What is the dimension of the space of all polynomials of degree ≤ 3, with basis \(\{1,x,x^2,x^3\}\)?
    4.
  10. bonus — You're handed exactly 3 vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) and told they're independent. Do you still need to separately check that they span \(\mathbb R^3\) before calling them a basis?
    No — in a space of dimension \(n\), any \(n\) independent vectors automatically span it too (used already in Chapter 7's worked example). This shortcut only works when the count exactly matches the dimension.
  11. challenge — Explain why "dimension" would stop being well-defined if different bases for the same space could have different sizes — what exactly would break?
    If basis size could vary, "what is \(\dim(V)\)?" would have no single answer — the honest reply would always be "it depends which basis you happened to pick," which defeats the entire point of attaching one fixed number to the space itself rather than to a particular basis. The (unproven-here but true) fact that every basis of \(V\) shares exactly the same size is precisely what rescues "dimension" from that ambiguity.
