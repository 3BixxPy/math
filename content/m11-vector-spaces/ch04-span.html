Chapter 4 · concept:span

Span

Chapter 3 asked, one target vector at a time, whether it happens to be a linear combination of some given vectors. This chapter asks the bigger question: what's the entire set of vectors reachable that way?

Why this matters

Span is how you describe an entire subspace compactly, without listing its — usually infinite — elements one at a time. Instead of writing out every point on a line, you name the one vector that generates it; instead of describing every vector in a plane, you name two vectors that generate all of it together. It's the difference between listing and generating.

Definition — Span

The span of vectors \(\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\) is the set of all possible linear combinations of them:

\[ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\}=\{c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k : c_1,\ldots,c_k\text{ any scalars}\} \]

A span is always a subspace in its own right — worth a quick sanity check against Chapter 2's test: it contains \(\mathbf 0\) (take every \(c_i=0\)); it's closed under addition (a combination of combinations is still a combination — just add the coefficients); and it's closed under scalar multiplication (scale every coefficient by the same constant). A span always automatically passes the subspace test, no matter which vectors you started with.

Worked example — a line versus the whole plane

Describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle2,1\rangle\}\), then describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1\rangle,\langle1,-1\rangle\}\).

Step 1 — span of a single nonzero vector

\(\operatorname{span}\{\langle2,1\rangle\}=\{c\langle2,1\rangle : c\in\mathbb R\}\) — every scalar multiple of \(\langle2,1\rangle\). Geometrically, that's exactly the line through the origin in the direction of \(\langle2,1\rangle\); one vector can only ever reach points along its own line.

Step 2 — span of two non-parallel vectors

For \(\langle1,1\rangle\) and \(\langle1,-1\rangle\), Chapter 3's challenge-style argument generalizes: given any target \(\langle a,b\rangle\), solve \(c_1+c_2=a\), \(c_1-c_2=b\) for \(c_1,c_2\) — adding gives \(c_1=\frac{a+b}{2}\), subtracting gives \(c_2=\frac{a-b}{2}\), and this works for every \(a,b\) with no exceptions. So every vector in \(\mathbb R^2\) is reachable: \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1\rangle,\langle1,-1\rangle\}=\mathbb R^2\), the whole plane.

span{⟨1,0⟩, ⟨2,0⟩} — parallel, just a line
span{⟨1,1⟩, ⟨1,-1⟩} — non-parallel, all of R²
Common mistake

Assuming two vectors always span all of \(\mathbb R^2\), no matter which two you pick. They don't: two parallel vectors — scalar multiples of each other — only ever span a line, no matter how many of them you throw in. \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0\rangle,\langle2,0\rangle\}\) is still just the \(x\)-axis, because \(\langle2,0\rangle=2\langle1,0\rangle\) contributes no new direction — every combination of the two collapses right back onto the one line \(\langle1,0\rangle\) already generates alone.

Try it yourself

Does \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0\rangle,\langle2,0\rangle\}\) cover all of \(\mathbb R^2\)?

Practice

  1. Describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle0,3\rangle\}\).
    The \(y\)-axis — every vector of the form \(\langle0,t\rangle\).
  2. Describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1\rangle,\langle2,2\rangle\}\).
    Just the line \(y=x\) — the two vectors are parallel (\(\langle2,2\rangle=2\langle1,1\rangle\)), so the second contributes nothing new.
  3. Does \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,2\rangle,\langle3,1\rangle\}\) equal all of \(\mathbb R^2\)?
    Yes — the two vectors aren't parallel (\(1/3\neq2/1\)), so, exactly like the worked example, any target vector is reachable.
  4. Is \(\langle2,5\rangle\) in \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1\rangle,\langle1,-1\rangle\}\)?
    Yes — solve \(c_1+c_2=2\), \(c_1-c_2=5\): \(c_1=3.5,\ c_2=-1.5\), a valid (if non-integer) solution, and since this span is all of \(\mathbb R^2\), every vector is in it anyway.
  5. Describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0,0\rangle,\langle0,1,0\rangle\}\) as a subset of \(\mathbb R^3\).
    The \(xy\)-plane — every vector \(\langle x,y,0\rangle\) (\(z=0\)).
  6. Is \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,2\rangle\}\) the same set as \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle2,4\rangle\}\)?
    Yes — both are the same line through the origin, since \(\langle2,4\rangle\) is just a scalar multiple of \(\langle1,2\rangle\); scaling the generating vector doesn't change which line it generates.
  7. bonus — Describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,0\rangle,\langle0,1\rangle,\langle1,1\rangle\}\) (three vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\)).
    Still all of \(\mathbb R^2\) — the third vector is redundant (it's already a combination of the first two), a preview of Chapter 6's "dependence."
  8. bonus — Is the zero vector always in the span of any set of vectors?
    Yes — take every coefficient to be 0.
  9. bonus — Does \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle0,0\rangle\}\) (the span of just the zero vector) equal \(\{\langle0,0\rangle\}\) or all of \(\mathbb R^2\)?
    Just \(\{\langle0,0\rangle\}\) — no matter what scalar \(c\) you pick, \(c\langle0,0\rangle=\langle0,0\rangle\); the zero vector alone can never generate anything else.
  10. bonus — In \(\mathbb R^3\), describe \(\operatorname{span}\{\langle1,1,1\rangle,\langle2,2,2\rangle\}\).
    A line — the two vectors are parallel, so together they still only reach the direction of \(\langle1,1,1\rangle\).
  11. challenge — Explain why, no matter how many vectors you put into a set, if every one of them is parallel to a single fixed vector \(\mathbf v\), the span can never be more than a line. Connect this to what Chapter 6 will call "dependence."
    Every vector in such a set is some scalar multiple of \(\mathbf v\), so every linear combination of them is a sum of scalar multiples of \(\mathbf v\) — which is itself just another scalar multiple of \(\mathbf v\). No combination can ever step off \(\mathbf v\)'s line, no matter how many parallel vectors you add. Chapter 6 will call a set like this "linearly dependent" — the vectors aren't contributing independent new directions.
