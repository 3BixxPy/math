Transformations II — Stretches, Compressions & Reflections

Yesterday's shifts moved a graph without changing its shape. Today's coefficient changes the shape itself — and one number does all three jobs at once.

Why this matters

A single multiplier out front — a in y = a·f(x) — controls stretching, compressing, and flipping, all through its size and sign. Once this is automatic, reading the shape of an unfamiliar equation on sight (before ever plotting a point) becomes routine.

Rule — Vertical stretch / compression / reflection
\[ y = a \cdot f(x) \]
(1.7.1)
  • |a| > 1: vertical stretch — every output multiplied further from 0, graph looks narrower/steeper
  • 0 < |a| < 1: vertical compression — outputs pulled closer to 0, graph looks flatter/wider
  • a < 0: also a reflection over the x-axis — the graph flips upside down
Common mistake

It's tempting to guess "bigger number → wider graph." It's the opposite: y=3x² grows faster than y=x², so for the same x it reaches a taller y sooner — the graph looks narrower and steeper, not wider. Check with a number: at \(x=2\), \(x^2=4\) but \(3x^2=12\) — three times taller at the same input, which visually compresses the curve horizontally even though nothing about x changed.

y = x² (baseline)y = a·x²
a = 1 — same as the baseline
Worked example

Compare y = -0.5x² to y = x².

Step 1 — size of a

\(|a| = 0.5 < 1\) → vertical compression (flatter/wider looking)

Step 2 — sign of a

\(a < 0\) → reflected over the x-axis (opens downward instead of up)

Result

Same parabola shape as \(x^2\), but flattened to half height and flipped to open downward — it now has a highest point at the origin instead of a lowest one.

Practice

  1. Is y = 5x² a stretch or compression of y = x²?
    Stretch (|5| > 1)
  2. Is y = 0.2x² a stretch or compression of y = x²?
    Compression (0.2 < 1)
  3. Does y = -x² open upward or downward?
    Downward — the negative sign reflects it over the x-axis.
  4. Compare y = 2x² to y = x² at x = 3 — compute both outputs and see how much taller the stretched one is.
    \(x^2=9\), \(2x^2=18\) — exactly double, matching the factor of 2.
  5. What single number, applied to y = x², would both flip it upside down and compress it to a quarter of its height?
    \(a = -0.25\)
  6. Is y = x² itself a stretch, compression, or neither, compared to y = x²?
    Neither — a = 1 leaves the graph unchanged.
  7. bonus — Compare y = -2√x to y = √x: describe both effects.
    Vertical stretch by 2, and reflected over the x-axis.
  8. bonus — A graph is compressed to a third of its height but not flipped. What is a?
    \(a = 1/3\) (positive, since it's not flipped)
  9. bonus — True or false: reflecting a graph over the x-axis changes its x-intercepts.
    False — the x-intercepts are where y=0, and flipping upside down doesn't move points that were already at y=0.
  10. bonus — True or false: reflecting a graph over the x-axis changes its y-intercept (assuming it isn't 0).
    True — the y-intercept's sign flips (e.g. (0,4) becomes (0,-4)).
  11. challenge — Combine everything from Chapters 6 and 7: describe, in order, what happens to y=x² to produce y=-2(x-3)²+5.
    Stretched vertically by 2, reflected over the x-axis (opens downward), shifted right 3, shifted up 5 — vertex ends at (3,5).
