Quadratic Functions & Vertex Form

Three chapters' worth of tools converge here: transformations (Ch. 6–7) plus completing the square (Module 0, Ch. 13) turn any quadratic into a shape you can read on sight.

Why this matters

Quadratics almost never arrive in the convenient (x-h)²+k form — they show up as ax²+bx+c. Converting between the two is just completing the square wearing different clothes, and the payoff is immediate: vertex form hands you the highest or lowest point of the graph, which is exactly what "optimization" problems in Calculus I ask for.

Definition — Vertex form
\[ y = a(x-h)^2 + k \]
(1.8.1)

The vertex — the graph's lowest point if \(a>0\), highest if \(a<0\) — is exactly \((h,k)\), read directly off the equation using Chapters 6–7. The vertical line \(x=h\) through it is called the axis of symmetry: the parabola is a mirror image of itself across that line.

Shortcut — Finding the vertex from standard form
\[ h = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad k = f(h) \]
(1.8.2)

This falls straight out of completing the square on \(ax^2+bx+c\) (same derivation as the quadratic formula, Module 0 Ch. 13) — it's the location of the axis of symmetry. Once you have \(h\), just evaluate the original function at it to get \(k\).

Worked example — both methods, cross-checked

Find the vertex of y = x² − 6x + 5.

Method 1, Step 1 — complete the square

\(x^2-6x+5 = (x-3)^2 - 9 + 5\)

Method 1, Step 2 — simplify to vertex form

\(y=(x-3)^2-4\) → vertex \((3,-4)\)

Method 2, Step 1 — use the shortcut, a=1, b=-6

\(h = -\dfrac{-6}{2(1)} = 3\)

Method 2, Step 2 — evaluate at h to find k

\(k = f(3) = 9-18+5 = -4\)

Both methods agree

Vertex \((3,-4)\) either way — a good habit is to have a second method ready to cross-check, especially on something as error-prone as a sign.

Common mistake

Forgetting the negative sign in h = -b/(2a). For y=x²-6x+5, \(b=-6\), so \(h=-(-6)/2 = 3\) — the two negatives cancel. It's easy to rush and write \(h=-6/2=-3\), dropping one of the negatives. Write out \(-b\) as its own mini-step before dividing, the same habit from the discriminant in Module 0.

Practice

  1. Convert to vertex form and state the vertex: y = x² − 4x + 1
    \((x-2)^2-3\), vertex \((2,-3)\)
  2. Convert to vertex form and state the vertex: y = x² + 2x − 8
    \((x+1)^2-9\), vertex \((-1,-9)\)
  3. Find the vertex using the shortcut: y = 2x² − 8x + 3
    \(h=2\), \(k=-5\), vertex \((2,-5)\)
  4. Find the vertex: y = -x² + 4x
    \(h=2\), \(k=4\), vertex \((2,4)\)
  5. Does y = 3(x+2)² − 5 open up or down, and what's its vertex?
    Up (a=3>0); vertex \((-2,-5)\)
  6. Write the vertex-form equation with vertex (4, -2) and a = 1.
    \(y=(x-4)^2-2\)
  7. bonus — Find the vertex: y = x² + 5
    \((0,5)\)
  8. bonus — Convert to vertex form and state whether it's a max or min: y = -2x² + 8x - 3
    \(-2(x-2)^2+5\), vertex \((2,5)\), a maximum since it opens down
  9. bonus — Expand back to standard form: y = 2(x-1)² + 3
    \(2x^2-4x+5\)
  10. bonus — A ball's height is h(t) = -16t² + 32t + 5 (feet, t in seconds). Find its maximum height and when it occurs.
    \(h=-32/(2\cdot-16)=1\) second; max height \(=-16+32+5=21\) feet.
  11. challenge — Find the vertex form of y = 3x² − 12x + 7 two ways: complete the square by hand, then confirm with the shortcut formula.
    \(3(x^2-4x)+7 = 3[(x-2)^2-4]+7 = 3(x-2)^2-5\), vertex \((2,-5)\). Shortcut: \(h=-(-12)/6=2\), \(k=3(4)-24+7=-5\) ✓.
