Linear Functions & Slope

Slope is "how fast y changes as x changes." Hold onto that phrase — it's the entire idea a derivative generalizes in Calculus I.

Why this matters

A straight line has the same steepness everywhere, which makes it the simplest possible relationship between two changing quantities — and the perfect place to build the idea of "rate of change" before curves make it more complicated. Every calculus derivative you'll ever compute is, at its core, still asking this same slope question, just on a curve instead of a line.

Definition — Slope
\[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]
(1.5.1)

Often remembered as "rise over run" — how much y changes (rise), divided by how much x changes (run), between any two points on the line. For a straight line this ratio is the same no matter which two points you pick.

Worked example

Find the slope between (1, 2) and (4, 11).

Step 1 — apply the formula

\(m = \dfrac{11-2}{4-1} = \dfrac{9}{3}\)

Step 2 — simplify

\(m = 3\)

Run = 3 (horizontal), rise = 9 (vertical). 9 ÷ 3 = 3, the slope — and it's the same ratio wherever you measure it on this line.

Rule — Slope-intercept form
\[ y = mx + b \]
(1.5.2)

m is the slope, b is the y-intercept (Chapter 4). Once you know a line's slope and one point on it, you can find b by substituting that point in and solving.

Worked example

Find the slope-intercept equation of the line through (1, 7) with slope 4.

Step 1 — start with y = mx + b, plug in the slope

\(y = 4x + b\)

Step 2 — substitute the known point to solve for b

\(7 = 4(1) + b \Rightarrow b = 3\)

Result

\(y = 4x + 3\)

Common mistake

Mixing up which coordinate goes on top in the slope formula, or subtracting in a different order top and bottom. (y2-y1)/(x2-x1) and (y1-y2)/(x1-x2) both give the correct (same) answer — but (y2-y1)/(x1-x2) flips the sign and is wrong. Pick an order for point 1 and point 2, and use that same order consistently in both the top and the bottom.

Try it yourself

What is the slope between (2, 3) and (5, 12)?

Practice

  1. Find the slope between (2, 3) and (5, 12).
    \(m=9/3=3\)
  2. Find the slope between (-1, 4) and (3, -8).
    \(m=-12/4=-3\)
  3. Write the equation of the line with slope 2 and y-intercept -5.
    \(y=2x-5\)
  4. Find the slope-intercept equation through (0, 3) and (2, 11).
    slope\(=4\); since (0,3) is already the y-intercept: \(y=4x+3\)
  5. What is the slope of a horizontal line? Of a vertical line?
    Horizontal: 0. Vertical: undefined (division by zero in the formula).
  6. Find the equation of the line through (2, -1) with slope -3.
    \(y=-3x+5\)
  7. bonus — Find the slope between (3, 5) and (3, 9). What kind of line is this?
    Undefined — same x-coordinate means a vertical line.
  8. bonus — Find the slope between (-2, 6) and (4, 6).
    0 — same y-coordinate, a horizontal line.
  9. bonus — Two lines both have slope 2 but different y-intercepts. What do you know about them geometrically?
    They're parallel — same steepness, never meet.
  10. bonus — A line has slope -1/2. Is it steeper or less steep than a line with slope 3? Explain using the sign and size of each number.
    Less steep — steepness depends on the size (absolute value) of the slope, not its sign; \(|{-1/2}| = 0.5 < 3\).
  11. challenge — A line passes through (1, 5) and (3, 13). Find its slope-intercept equation, then use it to predict y when x = 10.
    slope\(=8/2=4\); \(y-5=4(x-1) \Rightarrow y=4x+1\). At \(x=10\): \(y=41\).
