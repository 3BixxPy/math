Chapter 9 · concept:pythagorean-identity

The Pythagorean Identity

The single most-reused equation in this module. It isn't a new fact to memorize — it's the unit circle's own equation from Chapter 2, restated.

Why this matters

Nearly every identity, equation-solving trick, and simplification for the rest of this module leans on this one relationship between \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\). It's called "Pythagorean" for a reason you already know: it is the Pythagorean theorem, applied to the unit circle's radius.

Theorem — Pythagorean Identity
\[ \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \]
(2.9.1)

True for every real \(\theta\) — no exceptions, no domain restriction.

Worked example — deriving it, not memorizing it

Derive equation 2.9.1 directly from the unit circle's own equation.

Step 1 — start from the unit circle's defining equation (Chapter 2)

\(x^2+y^2=1\) for any point \((x,y)\) on the unit circle.

Step 2 — substitute the unit-circle definition of sine and cosine

Chapter 2 defines \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) as exactly that point (equation 2.2.1), so substitute \(x=\cos\theta\), \(y=\sin\theta\).

Result

\(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), which is equation 2.9.1 written in the other order. There's no separate proof to remember — it's the circle's equation with a name.

Worked example — using it, with a sign catch

Given sin θ = 3/5 with \(\theta\) in quadrant II, find cos θ.

Step 1 — plug into the identity

\(\left(\dfrac35\right)^2 + \cos^2\theta = 1 \Rightarrow \dfrac{9}{25}+\cos^2\theta=1\)

Step 2 — solve for cos²θ

\(\cos^2\theta = 1-\dfrac{9}{25} = \dfrac{16}{25}\)

Step 3 — take the square root — two possibilities

\(\cos\theta = \pm\dfrac{4}{5}\). The identity alone can't tell you the sign — squaring destroys sign information.

Step 4 — resolve the sign using the quadrant (Chapter 3)

QII: cosine is negative ("All Students..." — only sine is positive in QII). So \(\cos\theta = -\dfrac45\).

Common mistake

Solving \(\cos^2\theta=16/25\), writing \(\cos\theta=4/5\), and stopping — forgetting that a square root always introduces a \(\pm\), and forgetting to resolve which sign is correct using the quadrant. The identity by itself is sign-blind; you always need a second piece of information (which quadrant \(\theta\) is in) to pick the right sign. Skipping this step, or applying the wrong sign out of habit, is the most common error with this identity.

Try it yourself

If sin θ = 1/2 and θ is in quadrant I, find cos θ.

Practice

  1. If cos θ = 4/5 and θ is in QI, find sin θ.
    \(\sin^2\theta=1-16/25=9/25\); QI sine positive: \(\sin\theta=3/5\)
  2. If sin θ = -1/2 and θ is in QIII, find cos θ.
    \(\cos^2\theta=1-1/4=3/4\); QIII cosine negative: \(\cos\theta=-\sqrt3/2\)
  3. If cos θ = -12/13 and θ is in QII, find sin θ.
    \(\sin^2\theta=1-144/169=25/169\); QII sine positive: \(\sin\theta=5/13\)
  4. Verify equation 2.9.1 numerically at θ = π/4.
    \(\sin^2(\pi/4)+\cos^2(\pi/4) = (\sqrt2/2)^2+(\sqrt2/2)^2 = 1/2+1/2=1\) ✓
  5. If sin θ = 0.6 and θ is in QII, find cos θ as a decimal.
    \(\cos^2\theta=1-0.36=0.64\); QII negative: \(\cos\theta=-0.8\)
  6. Explain why equation 2.9.1 has no domain restriction, unlike, say, tan θ = sin θ/cos θ.
    It's just the unit circle's equation — every real θ produces a point on the circle (Chapter 2), so it holds unconditionally. tan θ, by contrast, involves division, which fails only when cos θ = 0.
  7. bonus — If sin θ = 5/13 and θ is in QI, find tan θ. (Use the identity first, then Chapter 4's definition.)
    \(\cos\theta=12/13\) (QI positive); \(\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta=5/12\)
  8. bonus — If cos θ = -3/5 and θ is in QIII, find sin θ and tan θ.
    \(\sin^2\theta=1-9/25=16/25\); QIII negative: \(\sin\theta=-4/5\); \(\tan\theta=(-4/5)/(-3/5)=4/3\)
  9. bonus — Could sin θ = 0.9 and cos θ = 0.9 both be true for the same θ? Use the identity to justify your answer.
    No — \(0.9^2+0.9^2=1.62\neq1\), violating equation 2.9.1.
  10. bonus — If sin θ = -√3/2 and θ is in QIV, find cos θ.
    \(\cos^2\theta=1-3/4=1/4\); QIV cosine positive: \(\cos\theta=1/2\)
  11. challenge — A problem gives sin θ = 7/25 but doesn't say which quadrant θ is in. How many possible values of cos θ are consistent with the identity alone, and what are they? What extra information would pin down a single answer?
    Two: \(\cos\theta=\pm24/25\) (from \(\cos^2\theta=1-49/625=576/625\)). Pinning down one requires knowing the quadrant (or equivalently the sign of cos θ directly) — the identity alone is always sign-ambiguous.
