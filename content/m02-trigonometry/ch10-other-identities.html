Chapter 10 · concept:pythagorean-identity-variants

Identities Derived from the Pythagorean Identity

Two more identities that feel like separate facts but are really one algebra move — dividing Chapter 9's identity through by cos²θ or sin²θ — applied twice.

Why this matters

These two variants show up constantly when simplifying expressions involving tangent/secant or cotangent/cosecant. The point of walking through the derivation (not just handing you the result) is that you should be able to regenerate either one from equation 2.9.1 any time you forget it — that's far more reliable than memorizing three separate identities.

Worked example — dividing by cos²θ

Derive the identity relating tangent and secant.

Step 1 — start from the Pythagorean identity

\(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\) (equation 2.9.1)

Step 2 — divide every term by cos²θ

\(\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} + \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta}\)

Step 3 — recognize each piece using Chapter 4's definitions

\(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\tan\theta\), so the first term is \(\tan^2\theta\). The middle term is 1. The right side, \(1/\cos^2\theta\), is \(\sec^2\theta\).

Result
\[ 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta \]
(2.10.1)
Worked example — dividing by sin²θ

Derive the identity relating cotangent and cosecant, the same way.

Step 1 — same starting point

\(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\)

Step 2 — divide every term by sin²θ this time

\(\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} + \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\)

Step 3 — recognize each piece

First term is 1. \(\cos\theta/\sin\theta=\cot\theta\), so the middle term is \(\cot^2\theta\). Right side \(1/\sin^2\theta = \csc^2\theta\).

Result
\[ 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta \]
(2.10.2)
Worked example — using a variant to simplify

Simplify sec²x − tan²x.

Step 1 — rearrange equation 2.10.1

\(1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x \Rightarrow \sec^2x - \tan^2x = 1\)

Result

\(\sec^2x-\tan^2x = 1\) — no further simplification possible or needed; it's already a constant.

Common mistake

"Verifying" an identity by plugging in one convenient angle (say \(\theta=45°\)), getting a true numeric statement, and treating that as a proof. It isn't — a single matching value shows the identity isn't obviously false, nothing more. This is exactly the same logical gap as checking one row of a truth table and calling a boolean expression a tautology: one satisfying case never proves a universal claim. Spot-checking a specific angle is a reasonable sanity check after a real algebraic derivation (like the ones above) — but it is never a substitute for one.

Try it yourself

Simplify sec²(x) − 1.

Practice

  1. Simplify csc²x − cot²x.
    \(1\) (rearranged equation 2.10.2)
  2. If sec θ = 13/5, find tan θ (assume θ in QI, tan positive).
    \(\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta-1=169/25-1=144/25\); \(\tan\theta=12/5\)
  3. Simplify csc²x − 1.
    \(\cot^2x\)
  4. If tan θ = 3/4, find sec θ (assume θ in QI).
    \(\sec^2\theta=1+9/16=25/16\); \(\sec\theta=5/4\)
  5. Derive, in your own words (one or two steps), what dividing the Pythagorean identity by sin²θ gives you — without looking back at the worked example.
    \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\) — divide every term of \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) by \(\sin^2\theta\).
  6. Simplify (1 + tan²x) − sec²x.
    \(0\) — that's exactly equation 2.10.1 rearranged to equal zero.
  7. bonus — If cot θ = 2, find csc θ (assume θ in QI).
    \(\csc^2\theta=1+4=5\); \(\csc\theta=\sqrt5\)
  8. bonus — Show that sec²x · cos²x simplifies to a constant, and find it.
    \(\sec^2x=1/\cos^2x\) (Chapter 4), so \(\sec^2x\cdot\cos^2x = 1\) directly — doesn't even need 2.10.1.
  9. bonus — A classmate claims 1 − sin²x = cos²x is "the same identity" as equation 2.9.1. Are they right? Show the one-line rearrangement that proves it.
    Yes — subtract sin²x from both sides of \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) to get \(\cos^2\theta=1-\sin^2\theta\). Same identity, rearranged.
  10. bonus — Explain why dividing the Pythagorean identity by tan²θ would not directly produce a "named" identity the way dividing by sin²θ or cos²θ does.
    tan²θ isn't one of the two terms already in the identity (sin²θ, cos²θ, or the constant 1) — dividing by it mixes sin and cos together in an unfamiliar ratio rather than cleanly turning each term into another named function's square, the way dividing by sin²θ or cos²θ does.
  11. challenge — A classmate "verifies" that sec²x − tan²x = 1 by plugging in x = 0 (getting 1 − 0 = 1, true) and calls the identity proven. Explain specifically what's missing from their argument, and what a complete proof requires.
    One numeric match only shows the identity holds at x=0 — it says nothing about any other angle. A complete proof needs the general algebraic derivation (dividing the Pythagorean identity by cos²x for every θ where cos θ ≠ 0, as done above), which holds for the entire domain at once rather than one sampled point.
