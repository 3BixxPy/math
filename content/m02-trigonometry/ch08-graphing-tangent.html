Chapter 8 · concept:tangent-graph

Graphing y = tan x

Sine and cosine are bounded, smooth waves. Tangent looks nothing like them — because Chapter 4 already told you why: it's undefined wherever cos x = 0, and that shows up on the graph as vertical asymptotes.

Why this matters

It's tempting to assume every trig graph looks like a sine wave. Tangent is the clearest counterexample, and understanding why it looks the way it does (unbounded, repeating faster, breaking at specific points) reinforces that a graph's shape always comes from the function's actual behavior — never just "trig functions are wavy."

Rule — Period of tangent
\[ \tan(x+\pi) = \tan x \]
(2.8.1)

Tangent's period is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\) like sine and cosine. Why: \(\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta\) is a slope (Chapter 4) — and rotating an angle by exactly \(\pi\) (180°) sends both \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) to their negatives, \((-\sin\theta)/(-\cos\theta)=\sin\theta/\cos\theta\), so the ratio is unchanged after only half a full rotation.

Vertical asymptotes

Wherever \(\cos\theta=0\) — Chapter 4's domain restriction — tan θ is undefined, and the graph has a vertical asymptote: at \(x=\pi/2, 3\pi/2, -\pi/2, -3\pi/2, \ldots\) (every \(\pi/2+k\pi\)).

Dashed lines mark the asymptotes. The curve itself never draws across them — plotFunction stops the moment a sampled value leaves the visible y-range and starts a fresh segment once it's back, so there's no false horizontal line connecting one branch to the next.

Worked example

Describe one full period of y = tan x, from one asymptote to the next.

Step 1 — pick a period: -π/2 to π/2

Both endpoints are asymptotes (excluded from the domain); the graph is defined everywhere strictly between them.

Step 2 — key points inside that period

\(\tan(-\pi/4)=-1\), \(\tan(0)=0\), \(\tan(\pi/4)=1\) — the curve is increasing the whole way through, crossing the origin.

Step 3 — behavior near the edges

As \(x\to\pi/2^-\), \(\tan x\to+\infty\). As \(x\to-\pi/2^+\), \(\tan x\to-\infty\). Every subsequent period (shift by \(\pi\)) repeats this exact shape.

Worked example — a transformed tangent

Find the period and asymptotes of y = 2tan(x - π/4).

Step 1 — the "2" does not affect period

Unlike sine/cosine, there's no amplitude to read from the 2 — it stretches the graph vertically (steeper branches) but the period logic depends only on the coefficient of \(x\) inside, which is 1 here.

Step 2 — period

Coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so period is still \(\pi\) (same as the base tan x).

Step 3 — asymptotes shift with the phase shift

Base asymptotes at \(\pi/2+k\pi\) shift right by \(\pi/4\): new asymptotes at \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}+\dfrac{\pi}{4}+k\pi = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}+k\pi\), i.e. \(\ldots, -\pi/4, 3\pi/4, 7\pi/4,\ldots\)

Common mistake

Assuming tangent has an amplitude, or a maximum/minimum value, the way sine and cosine do. It doesn't — tangent is unbounded, climbing toward \(+\infty\) and falling toward \(-\infty\) within every single period. A coefficient out front like the "2" in y=2tan x stretches the steepness of the climb, but there is no ceiling or floor to stretch away from. If a problem asks for tangent's "amplitude," the honest answer is that the concept doesn't apply.

Practice

  1. What is the period of y = tan x?
    \(\pi\)
  2. Name the vertical asymptote of y = tan x closest to (but less than) x = 0.
    \(x=-\pi/2\)
  3. Is y = tan x increasing or decreasing between two consecutive asymptotes?
    Always increasing within one period.
  4. Find the asymptotes of y = tan(2x). (Hint: the input to tan must equal π/2 + kπ.)
    \(2x=\pi/2+k\pi \Rightarrow x=\pi/4+k\pi/2\)
  5. What is tan(0), and what does that tell you about where y = tan x crosses the x-axis?
    \(\tan0=0\); the graph crosses the x-axis at every multiple of π (0, π, 2π, -π, ...), the zeros of sin θ.
  6. Does y = tan x have a maximum value? Explain.
    No — it increases without bound approaching each right-hand asymptote, so there's no largest value.
  7. bonus — Find the period of y = tan(x/3).
    Coefficient of x is 1/3; period \(=\pi/(1/3)=3\pi\)
  8. bonus — How many vertical asymptotes does y = tan x have on the interval (0, 2π)?
    Two: \(x=\pi/2\) and \(x=3\pi/2\)
  9. bonus — Find the asymptotes of y = tan(x + π/2), and explain (using Chapter 4) why this shifted tangent turns out to equal -cot x.
    Base asymptotes π/2+kπ shift left by π/2, landing at kπ — exactly where sin θ = 0, cotangent's own asymptotes (Chapter 4). This match isn't a coincidence: shifting tan by π/2 swaps sine and cosine's roles, which is exactly what turns tan into (a negative of) cot.
  10. bonus — True or false: tan(x + 2π) = tan(x) for every x in the domain.
    True (it holds), but it's not the smallest such shift — π already works (equation 2.8.1), so π is "the" period, not 2π.
  11. challenge — Explain, using tan θ = sin θ/cos θ (Chapter 4), exactly why y = tan x blows up to +∞ (not -∞) as x approaches π/2 from the left.
    Just below π/2, sin x is close to 1 (positive), and cos x is a small positive number approaching 0 (still in QI). A positive number divided by a shrinking positive number grows without bound toward \(+\infty\). (Just above π/2, cos x becomes a small negative number instead, flipping the sign — which is why the two sides of the same asymptote go to opposite infinities.)
