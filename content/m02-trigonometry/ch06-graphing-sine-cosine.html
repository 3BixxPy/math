Graphing y = sin x and y = cos x

The diagnostic's "something about a wave" was pointing at this graph. Now it gets an equation, a precise period, and marked coordinates instead of a vague shape.

Why this matters

Every value you computed by hand in Chapters 2–4 is one point on this graph. Plotting them in order — as the angle sweeps around the unit circle over and over — is what produces the wave. The wave isn't a new idea; it's the same circle unrolled along an x-axis.

Definition — Periodic function

A function \(f\) is periodic with period \(p\) if \(f(x+p)=f(x)\) for every \(x\), and \(p\) is the smallest positive number for which that's true. Since going all the way around the unit circle (\(2\pi\) radians) always lands back on the same point (Chapter 2), sine and cosine repeat every \(2\pi\):

\[ \sin(x+2\pi) = \sin x \]
(2.6.1)

The same holds for cosine: \(\cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x\). Both have period exactly \(2\pi\) — no smaller number works for every \(x\).

y = sin xy = cos x

Marked points are y = sin x at its key x-values — 0, π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π, and their negative mirrors.

Worked example

Read the amplitude, period, zeros, maximum, and minimum directly off the graph of y = sin x.

Step 1 — amplitude: how far the wave swings from its midline

The graph never goes above 1 or below -1, and its midline is \(y=0\). Amplitude \(=1\).

Step 2 — period: the x-distance before the shape repeats

The pattern from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\pi\) repeats exactly — period \(=2\pi\) (equation 2.6.1).

Step 3 — zeros: where the curve crosses y = 0

\(x = 0, \pi, 2\pi, -\pi, -2\pi, \ldots\) — every multiple of \(\pi\).

Step 4 — max and min

Maximum \(y=1\) at \(x=\pi/2\) (and every \(2\pi\) after); minimum \(y=-1\) at \(x=3\pi/2\) (or equivalently \(-\pi/2\)).

Worked example — comparing the two curves

Using the graph, describe how y = cos x relates to y = sin x.

Step 1 — compare their starting values at x = 0

\(\sin0=0\) (crosses the midline); \(\cos0=1\) (starts at its maximum).

Step 2 — compare the shapes

The two curves have identical shape, amplitude, and period — cosine's graph is just sine's graph shifted left by \(\pi/2\). (Chapter 7 makes this precise as a phase shift.)

Common mistake

Mixing up which curve starts at a maximum. Cosine starts at its maximum (\(\cos0=1\)) — sine starts at the midline going up (\(\sin0=0\)). If you only remember one anchor fact, remember \(\sin0=0\): sine passes through the origin. Everything else about the shape can be re-derived from the unit circle points in Chapter 2 if you forget.

Practice

  1. What is the amplitude of y = cos x?
    \(1\)
  2. Where does y = cos x equal zero, for x in [0, 2π]?
    \(x=\pi/2\) and \(x=3\pi/2\)
  3. At what x-value does y = cos x reach its minimum, for x in [0, 2π]?
    \(x=\pi\)
  4. Is y = sin x increasing or decreasing on the interval (π/2, π)?
    Decreasing — it's falling from its max at π/2 toward zero at π.
  5. Sketch mentally: for x in [-2π, 0], at what x-value does y = sin x reach its maximum?
    \(x=-3\pi/2\) (equivalent to \(\pi/2\) one period earlier)
  6. True or false: sin(x + 2π) = sin(x) for every real x. Justify briefly.
    True — equation 2.6.1, since adding \(2\pi\) is one full trip around the unit circle back to the same point.
  7. bonus — Is sin(3π) equal to sin(π)? Explain using periodicity.
    Yes — \(3\pi = \pi + 2\pi\), one full period later, so \(\sin(3\pi)=\sin(\pi)=0\).
  8. bonus — Is 4π also a valid period of y = sin x (i.e. does sin(x+4π)=sin(x))? Is it the period?
    The identity holds (it's two full periods), but it's not the period — "the period" means the smallest such positive number, which is \(2\pi\).
  9. bonus — For x in [0, 2π], at how many x-values does y = sin x equal 0?
    Three: \(x=0,\pi,2\pi\)
  10. bonus — Which function, sin or cos, is an even function (symmetric about the y-axis), based on the graph?
    cos x — its graph is a mirror image across the y-axis (\(\cos(-x)=\cos x\)). sin x is odd instead (\(\sin(-x)=-\sin x\)).
  11. challenge — Using only the unit circle (Chapter 2), explain why sin(-x) = -sin(x) for every x — this is exactly what makes the graph of sine symmetric about the origin.
    Angle \(-x\) is the mirror image of angle \(x\) reflected across the x-axis on the unit circle: same x-coordinate, opposite y-coordinate. Since \(\sin\theta\) is the y-coordinate, \(\sin(-x)=-\sin(x)\) follows directly from the reflection — no graph-reading required, just the definition.
