Angles, Degrees & Radians

The diagnostic found "one circle and something about sine waves — no concrete equations." This module builds the concrete part, starting with the unit calculus actually uses: radians, not degrees.

Why this matters

Degrees are arbitrary — 360 is a historical accident (roughly the number of days in a year, to the Babylonians). Radians are not arbitrary: they're defined directly from the circle itself, which is exactly why every clean calculus formula for sine and cosine (starting in Module 5) only works out neatly in radians. Building radian fluency now, before it's forced on you by a formula, means it's never a translation step later — just the native unit.

Definition — Angle

An angle measures rotation from a starting ray (the initial side) to an ending ray (the terminal side), both starting at a shared point (the vertex). By convention, angles are measured starting from the positive x-axis, and counterclockwise is positive.

Definition — Radian

One radian is the angle you sweep out when the arc length traveled along the circle equals the circle's radius. Since the full circumference is \(2\pi r\) (that is, \(2\pi\) radius-lengths), a full rotation is exactly \(2\pi\) radians.

60° = π/3 rad ≈ 1.047
Rule — Converting between degrees and radians
\[ 180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians} \]
(2.1.1)

To convert degrees → radians, multiply by \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\). To convert radians → degrees, multiply by \(\dfrac{180}{\pi}\). Everything else follows from this one equivalence.

Worked example

Convert 90°, 180°, and 270° to radians — the quarter-circle marks you'll use constantly.

Step 1 — apply the conversion factor π/180 to each

\(90 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\)

Step 2

\(180 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \pi\)

Step 3

\(270 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\)

Worth memorizing

Quarter turns: \(90^\circ=\pi/2\), \(180^\circ=\pi\), \(270^\circ=3\pi/2\), \(360^\circ=2\pi\). These four show up constantly.

Worked example — going the other way

Convert 5π/6 radians to degrees.

Step 1 — multiply by 180/π

\(\dfrac{5\pi}{6} \times \dfrac{180}{\pi} = \dfrac{5 \times 180}{6} = \dfrac{900}{6} = 150^\circ\)

Common mistake

Treating a radian value like 2 as "\(2\pi\)" or assuming radian answers must always contain \(\pi\). They don't have to — \(2\) radians is a perfectly valid angle (about \(114.6°\)), it's just less common to work with than the "nice" multiples of \(\pi\) this module focuses on. Also: keep your calculator's degree/radian mode straight — computing \(\sin(30)\) in radian mode is not the same question as \(\sin(30°)\), and both are valid computations of different angles.

  2. Convert to radians: 60°

  3. Convert to radians: 30°

    \(210°\)
  7. bonus — Convert to radians: 120°
    \(2\pi/3\)
  8. bonus — Convert to radians: −90°. What does the negative sign mean?
    \(-\pi/2\); negative means measured clockwise instead of counterclockwise.
  9. bonus — Convert to degrees: 5π/4
    \(225°\)
  10. bonus — How many radians are in one and a half full rotations?
    \(3\pi\) (one full rotation is \(2\pi\))
  11. challenge — Without a calculator, convert 1° to radians as a decimal approximation (use \(\pi\approx3.1416\)), and explain why "1 radian ≈ 57.3°" is a more natural-feeling benchmark than "1° in radians."
    \(1° \approx 0.01745\) rad — an awkward number, because the degree isn't naturally related to the radian. \(1\) radian \(\approx57.3°\) is more natural because radians are the circle's own unit — one radian is "one radius of arc," a real geometric quantity, not an arbitrary fraction of 360.
