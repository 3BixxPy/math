The Unit Circle — Sine and Cosine Defined

Chapter 1 gave you the unit angles are measured in. This chapter gives you the actual definition of sine and cosine — the one that makes them computable, not just "something about a wave."

Why this matters

Every fact you'll learn about sine and cosine for the rest of this module — exact values, graphs, identities, equations — traces back to one picture: a circle of radius 1, centered at the origin. Sine and cosine aren't defined by a graph or a wave; the wave is a consequence of this circle, not the definition. Get the circle right and everything downstream gets easier to re-derive instead of memorize.

Definition — Unit circle

The unit circle is the circle centered at the origin with radius 1. Its equation is \(x^2+y^2=1\).

Definition — Sine and cosine

Place an angle \(\theta\) in standard position (initial side on the positive x-axis, counterclockwise positive — Chapter 1's convention). Its terminal side crosses the unit circle at exactly one point. That point's coordinates are, by definition, cosine and sine of \(\theta\):

\[ \text{terminal point of } \theta = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta) \]
(2.2.1)

The x-coordinate is \(\cos\theta\). The y-coordinate is \(\sin\theta\). That's the whole definition — no triangle, no calculator, just a point on a circle.

θ = 60° → (cos θ, sin θ) = (0.500, 0.866)
Rule — Domain and range of sine and cosine
\[ -1 \le \cos\theta \le 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad -1 \le \sin\theta \le 1 \quad \text{for every real } \theta \]
(2.2.2)

The domain is all real numbers — any angle at all, including negatives and angles past a full turn, always lands on some point of the circle. The range is bounded to \([-1,1]\) for exactly the reason the diagram shows: the point can never leave a circle of radius 1, so neither coordinate can ever exceed 1 in absolute value.

Worked example — the three easiest angles

Find \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) at \(\theta = 0\), \(\pi/2\), and \(\pi\) — straight from the circle, no computation needed.

Step 1 — θ = 0: the terminal side hasn't moved off the positive x-axis

The point is \((1,0)\), so \(\cos 0 = 1\), \(\sin 0 = 0\).

Step 2 — θ = π/2: a quarter turn, straight up the y-axis

The point is \((0,1)\), so \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\), \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\).

Step 3 — θ = π: a half turn, straight left

The point is \((-1,0)\), so \(\cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\).

Worth memorizing

These "quadrantal" angles are the anchor points everything else in this module gets checked against: \(\theta=0 \to (1,0)\), \(\theta=\pi/2 \to (0,1)\), \(\theta=\pi \to (-1,0)\).

Worked example — the other two quadrantal angles

Find \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) at \(\theta = 3\pi/2\) and \(\theta = 2\pi\).

Step 1 — θ = 3π/2: three-quarters of a turn, straight down

The point is \((0,-1)\), so \(\cos(3\pi/2) = 0\), \(\sin(3\pi/2) = -1\).

Step 2 — θ = 2π: a full turn, back where you started

The point is \((1,0)\) again — same as \(\theta=0\). So \(\cos(2\pi)=1\), \(\sin(2\pi)=0\). This "coming back around" behavior is exactly what Chapter 6 formalizes as periodicity.

Common mistake

Mixing up which coordinate is which — writing \(\cos\theta\) for the y-value or \(\sin\theta\) for the x-value. Don't try to memorize "cosine is x" as an arbitrary fact; re-derive it from the one anchor point you're least likely to forget: at \(\theta=0\) you're sitting exactly on the positive x-axis at \((1,0)\). \(\cos 0 = 1\) matches the x-coordinate, \(\sin 0 = 0\) matches the y-coordinate. If you ever blank on which is which mid-problem, check that anchor point first.

Practice

  1. What are the coordinates of the point on the unit circle at θ = 0?
    \((1,0)\)
  2. Using the unit circle, find sin(π/2).
    \(1\)
  3. Using the unit circle, find cos(3π/2).
    \(0\)
  4. What point on the unit circle corresponds to θ = 2π? Why is that not a coincidence?
    \((1,0)\) — same as \(\theta=0\), because \(2\pi\) is exactly one full trip around the circle back to the start.
  5. Is there any real number θ for which cos θ = 2? Explain using equation 2.2.2.
    No. \(\cos\theta\) is an x-coordinate on a circle of radius 1, so it can never exceed 1 in absolute value.
  6. Find sin(π) and cos(0).
    \(\sin\pi=0\), \(\cos 0=1\)
  7. bonus — If cos θ = 0, name two possible values of θ in [0, 2π).
    \(\pi/2\) and \(3\pi/2\)
  8. bonus — A point on the unit circle is (0, -1). Name one angle θ in [0, 2π) that produces it.
    \(3\pi/2\)
  9. bonus — True or false: every point on the unit circle equals (cos θ, sin θ) for some real θ, and every real θ produces a point on the unit circle.
    True — that correspondence is exactly the definition in this chapter.
  10. bonus — Without a calculator, explain why sin θ can never equal 1.5.
    \(\sin\theta\) is a y-coordinate on a circle of radius 1 — it's bounded to \([-1,1]\) by equation 2.2.2, no matter what \(\theta\) is.
  11. challenge — Explain why cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 must be true for every θ, using only the definition in this chapter (don't just cite it as a memorized fact).
    \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) is defined as a point lying on the unit circle \(x^2+y^2=1\). Substituting \(x=\cos\theta\) and \(y=\sin\theta\) into that equation gives \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) directly — it's not a separate fact, it's the circle's own equation restated. (This becomes Chapter 9's Pythagorean identity.)
