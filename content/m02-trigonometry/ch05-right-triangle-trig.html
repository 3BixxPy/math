Right-Triangle Trigonometry (SOH-CAH-TOA)

This is where trigonometry started historically — solving triangles — and it's still the most common practical use. It turns out to be exactly the unit-circle definition from Chapter 2 in disguise.

Why this matters

Surveying, navigation, physics vectors, any "given an angle and one side, find the rest" problem — right-triangle trig is the tool. The mnemonic SOH-CAH-TOA has probably crossed your radar before even without concrete equations behind it; this chapter gives it the equations, and shows it's not a separate system from Chapter 2's unit circle at all.

Definition — SOH-CAH-TOA

For an acute angle \(\theta\) in a right triangle, label the side across from \(\theta\) the opposite side, the side next to \(\theta\) that isn't the hypotenuse the adjacent side, and the side across from the right angle the hypotenuse:

\[ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} \]
(2.5.1)

SOH-CAH-TOA: Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent.

Rule — This agrees with the unit circle

Put the right triangle's angle \(\theta\) at the origin in standard position with hypotenuse length 1. Then hyp = 1, so \(\sin\theta = \text{opp}/1 = \text{opp}\) and \(\cos\theta=\text{adj}/1=\text{adj}\) — and the opposite/adjacent legs are exactly the y- and x-coordinates of the point where the hypotenuse meets the unit circle. Right-triangle trig isn't a second definition of sine and cosine; it's Chapter 2's definition viewed through a triangle instead of a bare angle. Any hypotenuse length works the same way, because scaling a triangle up or down doesn't change its angle's ratios — only the absolute side lengths.

Definition — Angle of elevation / depression

The angle of elevation is the angle above the horizontal you look up at something higher than you. The angle of depression is the angle below the horizontal you look down at something lower. Because the horizontal lines at the two viewpoints are parallel, the angle of elevation from the ground up to a point equals the angle of depression from that point back down — alternate interior angles.

Worked example

A right triangle has an acute angle of 30° and hypotenuse 10. Find both legs.

Step 1 — opposite leg, using sine

\(\sin30° = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{10} \Rightarrow \text{opp} = 10\sin30° = 10\cdot\dfrac12 = 5\)

Step 2 — adjacent leg, using cosine

\(\cos30° = \dfrac{\text{adj}}{10} \Rightarrow \text{adj} = 10\cos30° = 10\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 5\sqrt3\)

Check

Pythagorean theorem (Module 0): \(5^2+(5\sqrt3)^2 = 25+75=100=10^2\) ✓

Worked example — angle of elevation word problem

A 12-foot ladder leans against a wall. The angle of elevation from the ground to the top of the ladder is 60°. How high up the wall does the ladder reach?

Step 1 — identify the triangle

The ladder is the hypotenuse (12 ft). The height up the wall is opposite the 60° angle at the ground. Adjacent would be the distance from the wall along the ground — not asked here.

Step 2 — set up sine (opposite over hypotenuse)

\(\sin60° = \dfrac{\text{height}}{12}\)

Step 3 — solve

\(\text{height} = 12\sin60° = 12\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 6\sqrt3 \approx 10.39\text{ ft}\)

Common mistake

Swapping which leg is "opposite" and which is "adjacent." Both labels are relative to which angle you're using — the same physical side can be the opposite leg for one acute angle and the adjacent leg for the other acute angle in the same triangle. Before writing any ratio, explicitly re-identify: which side is across from the angle I care about (opposite), and which side touches the angle but isn't the hypotenuse (adjacent)? Don't rely on "the vertical one" or "the horizontal one" — that only works if the triangle happens to be drawn a particular way.

Try it yourself

A right triangle has an acute angle of 30° and hypotenuse 10. Find the length of the opposite side.

Practice

  1. A right triangle has an acute angle of 45° and hypotenuse 8. Find both legs.
    Both legs equal \(8\cdot\sqrt2/2 = 4\sqrt2\)
  2. A right triangle has an acute angle of 60° and adjacent side 4. Find the hypotenuse.
    \(\cos60°=4/\text{hyp} \Rightarrow \text{hyp}=4/(1/2)=8\)
  3. A right triangle has opposite side 3 and adjacent side 4 (relative to angle θ). Find tan θ.
    \(\tan\theta=3/4\)
  4. The angle of elevation to the top of a 50 ft tower, from a point on the ground, is 30°. How far is the observer from the base of the tower?
    Height is opposite, distance is adjacent: \(\tan30°=50/\text{dist} \Rightarrow \text{dist}=50/\tan30°=50\sqrt3\approx86.6\) ft
  5. A right triangle has hypotenuse 2 and one acute angle 60°. Find the side opposite that angle.
    \(2\sin60°=2\cdot\sqrt3/2=\sqrt3\)
  6. Explain in one sentence why right-triangle trig gives the same values as the unit circle, even though there's no "circle" drawn.
    Scaling the hypotenuse to length 1 turns the triangle's legs directly into the unit circle's (cos θ, sin θ) coordinates — it's the same definition, just usually drawn with a longer hypotenuse.
  7. bonus — A right triangle has legs 5 and 12 (hypotenuse 13). Find sin θ and cos θ for the angle opposite the leg of length 5.
    \(\sin\theta=5/13\), \(\cos\theta=12/13\)
  8. bonus — From a lighthouse 40 m tall, the angle of depression to a boat is 20°. What angle would someone on the boat measure looking up at the lighthouse's top?
    20° — angle of elevation equals angle of depression (alternate interior angles).
  9. bonus — A right triangle has an acute angle of 45° and one leg of length 6. Find the hypotenuse.
    Both legs are equal in a 45-45-90 triangle: \(\sin45°=6/\text{hyp}\Rightarrow\text{hyp}=6/(\sqrt2/2)=6\sqrt2\)
  10. bonus — Two right triangles both have a 40° acute angle, but different hypotenuse lengths. Is sin(40°) different in the two triangles?
    No — sin θ is a ratio, and both triangles are similar (same angles), so opposite/hypotenuse stays the same regardless of overall size.
  11. challenge — A 20 ft flagpole casts a shadow. The angle of elevation from the tip of the shadow to the top of the pole is 35°. Set up (but don't necessarily simplify by calculator) the equation for the shadow's length, and identify which side is opposite vs. adjacent to the 35° angle.
    The pole (20 ft) is opposite the 35° angle; the shadow is adjacent. \(\tan35° = 20/\text{shadow} \Rightarrow \text{shadow} = 20/\tan35°\).
