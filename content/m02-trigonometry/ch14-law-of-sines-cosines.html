Law of Sines & Law of Cosines

Chapter 5's SOH-CAH-TOA only works on right triangles. These two laws solve any triangle at all — and the second one turns out to contain the Pythagorean theorem as a special case.

Why this matters

Most real-world triangles — surveying a plot of land, triangulating a position, laying out a truss — aren't conveniently right triangles. These two laws are the general-purpose toolkit: which one to reach for depends only on which pieces of the triangle you already know.

Convention: lowercase side names sit opposite the uppercase angle of the same letter — side a is opposite angle A, and so on.

Theorem — Law of Sines
\[ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} \]
(2.14.1)

True for any triangle, not just right ones. Use it when you know an angle and its opposite side (a full ratio), plus one more piece — typically an AAS or ASA setup.

Theorem — Law of Cosines
\[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \]
(2.14.2)

Also true for any triangle. Use it when you know two sides and the angle between them (SAS), or all three sides (SSS). Notice: if \(C=90°\), \(\cos C=0\) and this collapses to \(c^2=a^2+b^2\) — the Law of Cosines is the Pythagorean theorem, generalized to angles other than 90°.

Worked example — Law of Sines, AAS

Triangle with A = 40°, B = 60°, and side a = 8 (opposite A). Find side b.

Step 1 — set up the ratio using known angle-side pairs

\(\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} \Rightarrow \dfrac{8}{\sin40°} = \dfrac{b}{\sin60°}\)

Step 2 — solve for b

\(b = \dfrac{8\sin60°}{\sin40°} \approx \dfrac{8(0.866)}{0.643} \approx 10.78\)

Worked example — Law of Cosines, SAS

Triangle with sides a = 7, b = 9, and the included angle C = 50°. Find side c.

Step 1 — plug directly into equation 2.14.2

\(c^2 = 7^2+9^2-2(7)(9)\cos50° = 49+81-126\cos50°\)

Step 2 — evaluate

\(c^2 \approx 130 - 126(0.643) \approx 130-81.0 = 49.0 \Rightarrow c\approx7.0\)

Note — why Law of Sines couldn't start this one

SAS gives no complete angle/opposite-side pair to build a Law of Sines ratio from directly — that's exactly why Law of Cosines has to go first here. Once c is known, Law of Sines can be used to find the remaining angles.

Common mistake

Reaching for the Law of Sines on an SAS or SSS triangle where it doesn't apply cleanly. Law of Sines needs a complete angle/opposite-side pair to set up a ratio — with only two sides and the angle between them (SAS), or three sides and no angles (SSS), no such pair exists yet. Start with the Law of Cosines instead to find the missing piece, then switch to Law of Sines if convenient.

Also worth knowing: SSA (two sides and a non-included angle) is genuinely tricky — the "ambiguous case." Depending on the numbers, it can produce zero, one, or two valid triangles, because the given angle doesn't pin the triangle down uniquely the way SAS or ASA does. This module doesn't work through the ambiguous case in full, but recognize the setup so you don't assume a single clean answer exists.

Try it yourself

Using the Law of Cosines: if a = 3, b = 4, and C = 90°, find c. (Notice what this reduces to.)

Practice

  1. Triangle with A = 50°, C = 70°, side a = 10. Find side c. (First find angle C's opposite ratio partner — you have A and a already.)
    \(c = 10\sin70°/\sin50° \approx 12.27\)
  2. Triangle with sides a = 5, b = 6, included angle C = 60°. Find side c.
    \(c^2=25+36-60\cos60°=61-30=31 \Rightarrow c\approx5.57\)
  3. Which law would you use for a triangle where all three sides are known but no angles? Why?
    Law of Cosines — Law of Sines needs an angle/opposite-side pair, which SSS doesn't provide.
  4. Triangle with B = 35°, C = 85°, side b = 12. Find side c.
    \(c = 12\sin85°/\sin35° \approx 20.84\)
  5. Triangle with sides a = 8, c = 10, included angle B = 45°. Find side b.
    \(b^2=64+100-160\cos45°\approx164-113.1=50.9 \Rightarrow b\approx7.13\)
  6. Explain why the Law of Cosines reducing to the Pythagorean theorem when C = 90° is not a coincidence.
    cos(90°) = 0 (Chapter 2), so the "-2ab cos C" correction term vanishes entirely, leaving c² = a² + b² — the Law of Cosines is a strict generalization of the Pythagorean theorem to non-right angles.
  7. bonus — Triangle with A = 30°, B = 45°, side a = 6. Find side b.
    \(b=6\sin45°/\sin30°=6(0.707)/0.5\approx8.49\)
  8. bonus — Triangle with sides a = 4, b = 4, included angle C = 120°. Find side c.
    \(c^2=16+16-32\cos120°=32-32(-0.5)=32+16=48 \Rightarrow c=\sqrt{48}=4\sqrt3\approx6.93\)
  9. bonus — A triangle has all three angles given (A=60°, B=70°, C=50°) but no sides. Can the Law of Sines find the actual side lengths? Why or why not?
    No — with only angle ratios and no single known length, the Law of Sines can find the relative proportions between sides but not their absolute lengths (infinitely many similar triangles share those angles). At least one side length is required.
  10. bonus — In the SSA "ambiguous case," what does it mean geometrically for two different triangles to both satisfy the same given side-side-angle information?
    The given angle and adjacent side fix a ray and a circle (the other side's length) that can intersect the triangle's third side's line at two different points — swinging the unknown side to either intersection produces a valid, different triangle satisfying the same three given measurements.
  11. challenge — Triangle with sides a = 5, b = 7, c = 10. Find angle C (the angle opposite the longest side) using the Law of Cosines solved for an angle.
    Solve 2.14.2 for cos C: \(\cos C = \dfrac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab} = \dfrac{25+49-100}{70} = \dfrac{-26}{70}\approx-0.371\). So \(C = \arccos(-0.371) \approx 111.8°\) (Chapter 13's arccos, applied to a real triangle problem).
