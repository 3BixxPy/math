Quantifiers — ∀ and ∃

So far every proposition has stood on its own — one p, one q. Real math statements talk about every number, or some number, in a whole domain. This chapter gives those two words their own symbols, and shows their negation rule is De Morgan's law again, just scaled up from 2 wires to arbitrarily many.

Why this matters

Picture a wide bus of wires, bits b1, b2, ..., bn, and a "reduction" gate tree over them. An AND-reduce — output high only if every single bit is high — is exactly ∀i, bi=1 ("for all i"). An OR-reduce — output high if at least one bit is high — is exactly ∃i, bi=1 ("there exists i"). You've already reasoned about "is everything set" versus "is anything set" as different gate structures; ∀ and ∃ are just those two ideas freed from needing an actual physical bus — they work over infinite domains (all integers, all real numbers) where you obviously can't build the tree, but the "all of them" vs. "at least one of them" distinction is identical.

Definition — ∀ and ∃

∀x, P(x) ("for all x, P(x)") is true when the predicate P holds for every value of x in the domain under discussion — your AND-reduce over the whole domain.

∃x, P(x) ("there exists x such that P(x)") is true when P holds for at least one value of x in the domain — your OR-reduce.

A predicate P(x) is just a statement whose truth value depends on x — "x is prime," "x > 5" — not a proposition on its own (no fixed truth value until x is filled in), but it becomes one once a quantifier is attached, or once a specific x is substituted.

Theorem — De Morgan's laws for quantifiers
\[ \neg\big(\forall x\, P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x) \]
(4.4.1)
\[ \neg\big(\exists x\, P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x) \]
(4.4.2)

This is Chapter 3's De Morgan's law, scaled from 2 inputs to arbitrarily (even infinitely) many: negating an AND-reduce over the whole domain flips it to an OR-reduce of the negated predicate (4.4.1), and vice versa (4.4.2). "Not every bit is set" means "some bit is unset" — never "every bit is unset," and never "some bit is set." Both things flip together: the quantifier and the predicate inside it.

Worked example — translating into quantifier notation

Translate: "every integer has a successor."

Step 1 — identify the domain and the "for every" part

"Every integer" → \(\forall n \in \mathbb{Z}\).

Step 2 — identify the "has a ___" part — that's an existence claim, nested inside

"has a successor" → there exists some integer that is one more than n: \(\exists m \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(m = n+1\).

Result

\(\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \exists m \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } m = n+1\). Quantifiers nest exactly like this constantly — a ∀ setting up "for any instance," then an ∃ inside it making an existence claim about that instance.

Worked example — negating a quantified statement

Negate: "there exists an even prime greater than 2."

Step 1 — formalize the original

\(\exists n\ \big(\text{prime}(n) \wedge \text{even}(n) \wedge n>2\big)\)

Step 2 — apply 4.4.2: ∃ flips to ∀, negate the inside

\(\forall n\ \neg\big(\text{prime}(n) \wedge \text{even}(n) \wedge n>2\big)\)

Step 3 — De Morgan's law (Chapter 3) on the negated conjunction

\(\forall n\ \big(\neg\text{prime}(n) \vee \neg\text{even}(n) \vee n \le 2\big)\)

Result

"For every n: n is not prime, or n is not even, or n ≤ 2." And this negation is actually true — 2 is the only even prime, and it fails the "greater than 2" condition, so no n ever satisfies all three original conditions at once.

Common mistake

Flipping the quantifier but leaving the predicate alone (or the reverse). Negating ∀x, x > 0 as ∀x, x ≤ 0 is wrong — that flipped the predicate but kept ∀, claiming every x is ≤ 0, which is far stronger (and different) than what the true negation says. Negating it as ∃x, x > 0 is also wrong — that flipped the quantifier but not the predicate, which isn't even a negation, it's unrelated. The correct negation flips both, together: ∃x, x ≤ 0 — "it's not true that every x is positive" just means "some x fails to be positive," nothing about all of them.

Practice

  1. Translate: "some integer is negative."
    \(\exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\ n < 0\)
  2. Translate: "every real number squared is non-negative."
    \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \ge 0\)
  3. Negate: ∃x, x² = -1 (over the reals)
    \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \ne -1\)
  4. Negate: ∀n, n is even
    \(\exists n,\ n\) is not even (i.e. n is odd)
  5. Is "∀x, ∃y such that y > x" true over the real numbers? What does it mean in plain English?
    True — for every real number there's a bigger one; the reals have no maximum.
  6. Translate and then negate: "there exists a prime number that is even." (State both the formal statement and its negation.)
    \(\exists n\,(\text{prime}(n)\wedge\text{even}(n))\); negation: \(\forall n\,(\neg\text{prime}(n)\vee\neg\text{even}(n))\).
  7. bonus — An "all systems ready" indicator is high iff every subsystem's ready-bit is 1. Write this as a quantified statement, and its negation, over subsystems i.
    Ready: \(\forall i,\ b_i=1\). Negation (4.4.1): \(\exists i,\ b_i \ne 1\) — "at least one subsystem isn't ready."
  8. bonus — Negate: ∀x ∃y, x+y=0 (careful — two quantifiers, apply the rule twice from the outside in).
    \(\exists x\ \forall y,\ x+y \ne 0\) — outer ∀ flips to ∃ first, then the inner ∃ (now inside the negation) flips to ∀.
  9. bonus — Is ∀x ∃y, y > x the same statement as ∃y ∀x, y > x? (Try to decide true/false for each over the reals, don't just compare symbols.)
    No — order matters. The first is true (for any x, pick y=x+1). The second claims one single y bigger than every real number, which is false — no such y exists.
  10. bonus — Translate: "no integer is both even and odd." Give it directly as a ∀ statement (not a negated ∃).
    \(\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \neg(\text{even}(n) \wedge \text{odd}(n))\)
  11. challenge — Prove "no integer is both even and odd" and "there is no integer that is both even and odd" are the same claim by showing one is the De Morgan negation of ∃n\,(\text{even}(n)\wedge\text{odd}(n))\), matching your answer to the previous problem exactly.
    Negating \(\exists n\,(\text{even}(n)\wedge\text{odd}(n))\) via 4.4.2 gives \(\forall n\,\neg(\text{even}(n)\wedge\text{odd}(n))\) — identical to the previous answer, confirming "no n is both" and "not (some n is both)" are the same statement, formally.
