Direct Proof

Six chapters of vocabulary — connectives, conditionals, equivalence, quantifiers, sets — all in service of this: actually writing a proof. A direct proof is the plainest kind, and it's exactly the thing you do every time you trace a signal through a circuit you already trust.

Why this matters

When you trace a signal through a schematic — this wire is high because that gate's inputs were high, which followed from this earlier stage, which followed from the given inputs — you're doing a direct proof. You start from what's given (the hypothesis, the input values), you apply known, already-verified rules one at a time (each gate's truth table, or a subcircuit you already proved correct), and you arrive at the output (the conclusion) with no gaps. A mathematical direct proof is that same chain, written in sentences instead of wires: assume p, apply definitions and established facts through valid steps, land on q.

Definition — Direct proof

To directly prove p ⇒ q: assume p is true, then use definitions, algebra, and previously established results — through a chain of valid implications — to show q must also be true. Each link in the chain has to be justified (a definition, a prior theorem, a valid algebraic step); "it looks right" is not a link.

Definition — Even and odd integers
\[ n \text{ is even} \iff n = 2k \text{ for some integer } k \]
(4.7.1)
\[ n \text{ is odd} \iff n = 2k+1 \text{ for some integer } k \]
(4.7.2)

These look almost too obvious to write down — but from here on, "even" and "odd" mean exactly these algebraic forms and nothing fuzzier. Every proof about even/odd numbers for the rest of this course starts by invoking 4.7.1 or 4.7.2 to turn a word into an equation you can actually manipulate.

Worked example — template proof: the sum of two even integers is even

Claim: if m and n are even, then m + n is even. Each step below is labeled with its justification — that labeling is the proof, not decoration.

Step 1 — assume the hypothesis

Let m and n be even integers.

Step 2 — apply the definition of even (4.7.1) to each

Since m is even, \(m=2j\) for some integer j. Since n is even, \(n=2k\) for some integer k. (Different letters — nothing says m and n use the same k.)

Step 3 — add them and factor (algebra)

\(m+n = 2j+2k = 2(j+k)\)

Step 4 — recognize the form of 4.7.1

\(j+k\) is an integer (integers are closed under addition), so \(m+n = 2\cdot(\text{integer})\) — exactly the form equation 4.7.1 requires.

Result

Therefore m+n is even. Every step followed from either a definition or plain algebra — no gap, and it works for every pair of even integers at once, not just an example pair.

Worked example — if n is odd, then n² is odd

Claim: if n is odd, then n² is odd. (This exact claim comes back in Chapter 8, proved a completely different way — worth remembering.)

Step 1 — assume the hypothesis, apply the definition (4.7.2)

Let n be odd. Then \(n = 2k+1\) for some integer k.

Step 2 — square it

\(n^2 = (2k+1)^2 = 4k^2+4k+1\)

Step 3 — factor to expose the "2·(integer)+1" shape

\(4k^2+4k+1 = 2(2k^2+2k)+1\)

Result

Let \(m = 2k^2+2k\) — an integer, since integers are closed under multiplication and addition. Then \(n^2 = 2m+1\), exactly the form equation 4.7.2 requires. So n² is odd.

Common mistake

"Proving" a general claim by checking one specific number: plugging in n=3, confirming 3² = 9 is odd, and declaring the claim proved. That confirms the claim for one input — it says nothing about the other infinitely many odd integers. Checking an example is useful for building confidence a claim is plausible, or for finding a counterexample that kills a false claim in one shot — but it can never establish a claim that's supposed to hold for every case, because no finite number of examples covers an infinite domain. Only reasoning about the general form (n=2k+1, an arbitrary representative, not a specific number) proves it. This is exactly the gap between "I tested it on a few inputs" and "I verified the circuit for all input combinations" — a truth table with all rows is a proof; five example rows is not.

Try it yourself

Fill in the blank: "If n is even, then n = ___ for some integer k."

Practice

  1. Prove: if n is even, then n + 1 is odd.
    n=2k (def. even). n+1=2k+1, exactly the form of 4.7.2 ⟹ n+1 is odd.
  2. Prove: if n is even, then 3n is even.
    n=2k. 3n=3(2k)=2(3k), and 3k is an integer ⟹ 3n is even by 4.7.1.
  3. Prove: the product of two odd integers is odd.
    m=2j+1, n=2k+1. mn=(2j+1)(2k+1)=4jk+2j+2k+1=2(2jk+j+k)+1, form of 4.7.2 ⟹ mn is odd.
  4. Prove: if n is an integer, then n² + n is even. (Hint: consider n even and n odd as two separate cases.)
    Case n even: n=2k, n²+n=4k²+2k=2(2k²+k), even. Case n odd: n=2k+1, n²+n=(2k+1)²+(2k+1)=4k²+6k+2=2(2k²+3k+1), even. Either way, n²+n is even.
  5. Is "sum of two odd integers" even or odd? Prove it.
    Even. m=2j+1, n=2k+1. m+n=2j+2k+2=2(j+k+1), form of 4.7.1.
  6. Spot the gap: "Claim: n²−n is always even. Proof: 3²−3=6, even. QED." What's wrong, and what would a real proof need instead?
    Checked exactly one value (n=3); doesn't establish the general claim. A real proof needs the even/odd case split like problem 4, working with a general n = 2k or 2k+1.
  7. bonus — Prove: if a and b are both even, then ab is divisible by 4.
    a=2j, b=2k. ab=4jk, and jk is an integer ⟹ ab=4·(integer), divisible by 4.
  8. bonus — Prove: the sum of any three consecutive integers is divisible by 3. (Let the integers be n, n+1, n+2.)
    n+(n+1)+(n+2)=3n+3=3(n+1), and n+1 is an integer ⟹ divisible by 3.
  9. bonus — Prove: if n is odd, then n + 2 is odd.
    n=2k+1. n+2=2k+3=2(k+1)+1, form of 4.7.2 (with integer k+1) ⟹ odd.
  10. bonus — Why does the proof of "sum of two evens is even" use two different letters (j and k) instead of writing both as 2k?
    m and n are only known to individually be even — nothing says they share the same "multiplier." Writing both as 2k would silently (and wrongly) assume m=n.
  11. challenge — Prove: if n is even, then n³ is even, without splitting into cases — using only the definition and algebra, in the same style as the sum-of-evens template.
    n=2k (def. even). \(n^3=(2k)^3=8k^3=2(4k^3)\), and \(4k^3\) is an integer ⟹ n³ is even by 4.7.1.
