Logical Equivalence & De Morgan's Laws

Chapter 2 promised that p ⇒ q is secretly built out of ∨ and ¬. This chapter delivers the tool that makes that provable instead of asserted — and it's a fact you've almost certainly already used, just wired instead of written.

Why this matters

You've built a NAND gate. A NAND gate computes ¬(p ∧ q) directly. But you've probably also built the same circuit function a second way: invert both inputs, then OR them — ¬p ∨ ¬q. Two different-looking circuits, identical output for every input. That equivalence has a name — De Morgan's law — and once it has a name, it stops being a wiring trick you happened to notice and becomes a theorem you can invoke by name, on paper, without redrawing a schematic every time.

Definition — Logical equivalence (≡)

Two compound propositions are logically equivalent, written A ≡ B, if they have identical truth tables — the same output for every possible combination of the underlying variables' truth values. This is precisely the circuit-design notion of two circuits being "functionally identical": different gates, different wiring, same truth table, so either one can replace the other anywhere.

Theorem — De Morgan's Laws
\[ \neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q \]
(4.3.1)
\[ \neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q \]
(4.3.2)

In words: negating an AND flips it to an OR of negations (4.3.1 — your NAND-equals-inverted-input-OR fact); negating an OR flips it to an AND of negations (4.3.2 — the NOR equivalent). The pattern to remember: negation distributes, and the connective flips.

Worked example — verifying 4.3.1 by truth table

Confirm ¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q by building both sides' truth tables side by side, for all four p,q rows.

Step 1 — left side: ¬(p ∧ q)

p∧q is T,F,F,F (rows TT,TF,FT,FF). Negating: ¬(p∧q) is F,T,T,T.

Step 2 — right side: ¬p ∨ ¬q

¬p is F,F,T,T. ¬q is F,T,F,T. OR them row by row: F∨F=F, F∨T=T, T∨F=T, T∨T=T → F,T,T,T.

Result
p q ¬(p∧q) ¬p∨¬q
TTFF
TFTT
FTTT
FFTT

The last two columns match on every row — that is what "≡" means, confirmed directly from the definition rather than taken on faith.

Worked example — simplifying with De Morgan's law

Simplify ¬(¬p ∧ q).

Step 1 — apply 4.3.1 with "¬p" playing the role of the law's "p"

\(\neg(\neg p \wedge q) \equiv \neg(\neg p) \vee \neg q\)

Step 2 — double negation cancels

\(\neg(\neg p) = p\), so this becomes \(p \vee \neg q\).

Result

¬(¬p ∧ q) ≡ p ∨ ¬q — same trick as simplifying an inverted-NAND-of-an-inverter chain down to fewer gates.

Common mistake

Distributing the negation but forgetting to flip the connective: writing ¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q (wrong — that keeps the ∧). Check it against row TT: left side is ¬(T∧T) = ¬T = F, but the miswritten right side is ¬T ∧ ¬T = F ∧ F = F — that row happens to match, which is exactly what makes this mistake sneaky. Check row TF instead: left side ¬(T∧F) = ¬F = T, miswritten right side ¬T∧¬F = F∧T = F — they disagree. One mismatched row is enough to disprove an equivalence, which is also why "checking one row" is never a valid way to prove one (more on that trap in Chapter 7). The rule to lock in: negating always flips ∧↔∨.

Practice

  1. Simplify using De Morgan's law: ¬(p ∨ ¬q)
    \(\neg p \wedge \neg(\neg q) = \neg p \wedge q\)
  2. Simplify: ¬(¬p ∨ ¬q)
    \(\neg(\neg p)\wedge\neg(\neg q) = p \wedge q\)
  3. Is ¬p ∨ q logically equivalent to ¬(p ∧ ¬q)? (Apply 4.3.1 to the second form and compare.)
    Yes: \(\neg(p\wedge\neg q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg(\neg q) = \neg p \vee q\) — identical.
  4. A NOR gate computes ¬(p ∨ q). Write an equivalent circuit description using only inverters and an AND gate.
    Invert p, invert q, AND the results: \(\neg p \wedge \neg q\) (equation 4.3.2).
  5. Simplify: ¬(p ∧ q) ∨ p (apply De Morgan first, then look for a shortcut).
    \((\neg p \vee \neg q)\vee p\). Since \(\neg p \vee p\) is always true, the whole thing is always true regardless of q.
  6. Check whether row FT makes ¬(p∧q) and ¬p∧¬q agree or disagree (the miswritten "law" from the mistake box).
    Disagree: \(\neg(F\wedge T)=\neg F=T\), but \(\neg F \wedge \neg T = T\wedge F = F\).
  7. bonus — Simplify: ¬(¬p ∧ ¬q)
    \(p \vee q\)
  8. bonus — Is ¬p ∨ ¬q the same as ¬(p ∨ q)? Settle it with one row, not a full table.
    No — row TT: \(\neg p\vee\neg q = F\vee F=F\), but \(\neg(p\vee q)=\neg T=F\)... try row TF instead: \(\neg p\vee\neg q=F\vee T=T\), \(\neg(p\vee q)=\neg T=F\). Disagree — not equivalent.
  9. bonus — A circuit built from an inverter feeding into a NOR gate (i.e. computing ¬(¬p ∨ q)) — simplify its function.
    \(\neg(\neg p)\wedge\neg q = p \wedge \neg q\)
  10. bonus — Using De Morgan's law, rewrite p ⇒ q in a form using only ∨ and ¬. (Hint: use equation 4.2.1's "fault" idea, ¬(p ∧ ¬q), then apply 4.3.1.)
    \(\neg(p\wedge\neg q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg(\neg q) = \neg p \vee q\). So \(p\Rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \vee q\) — the promised gate-level rewrite of implication.
  11. challenge — Prove ¬(p ∧ q ∧ r) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q ∨ ¬r by applying De Morgan's law twice (treat q ∧ r as a single block first).
    \(\neg(p\wedge(q\wedge r)) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg(q\wedge r)\) (4.3.1 on the outer ∧) \(\equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \vee \neg r)\) (4.3.1 again on the inner ∧) \(= \neg p \vee \neg q \vee \neg r\).
